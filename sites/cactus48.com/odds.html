Father must tell
offspring about odds.

By Bob Cork
New Haven Register
June 14, 2000  

 

Stories appear frequently about grieving parents seeking civil lawsuits and criminal justice to punish people they hold accountable for the deaths of their adult children, who were breaking laws and doing stupid things when they were killed.

Although none of us who are parents know how we would react in similar situations, and we pray we never find out, it seems appropriate to ask if parents are telling kids that some actions "increase the odds" that death will come too soon.

When my youngest son was 14, he and two friends were "horsing around." Two boys lured the third outside, tried to shut him out of the house, his arm went through glass. I got home just in time to hold a towel compress on an ugly, gaping wound that required sixty stitches.

After the ambulance left with the boy, I told my son and his other friend that shunning horseplay does not guarantee safety, and horseplay does not guarantee accidents, but horseplay always "increases the odds" that somebody will get hurt.

But kids, of course, and many adults, don't think it will ever happen to them. Those who court disaster and survive are lucky, sometimes those who do nothing wrong end up dead, and sometimes we blame God when God was not involved.

William Sloane Coffin, former Yale chaplain, was frustrated when a parishioner expressed condolences for the death of Coffin's son by saying it was difficult to understand the will of God.

"It wasn't the will of God that my son die," Coffin said, "my son died because he got drunk and drove into Boston Harbor."

Not many parents would have the courage to be so candid, and even fewer would concede it may be more than coincidence that a large percentage of those who die while doing stupid things are young adults who have parents with different last names.

Just as horseplay "increases the odds" that an accident will happen, broken families "increase the odds" that young adults will get involved in dangerous horseplay.

I am amused by men who say they are good fathers because they spend quality time with their kids on alternate weekends. Sometimes problems don't develop on alternate weekends. Sometimes it's a remark or expression on Tuesday morning over breakfast that transmits a signal a father needs to receive.

My loving parents stayed together, but my father, partly because of his own traumatic childhood, did not know how to deal with my drinking problem, and so, not knowing how, he did nothing.

Eventually, at age 32, I bottomed out, after getting fired from a job I thought I did well. I got stinking drunk, smoked marijuana given to me by a friend, miraculously drove home without killing myself, and hallucinated all night, It would have been my fault, not my frend's, if I had died.

Was I lucky? Yes. Do I thank God for then giving me strength to stop poisoning my body and mind? Yes. Did that experience help me learn to keep my ears and eyes open on Tuesday mornings? Yes. Did it stop me from making mistakes as a father? Of course not.

There are times, especially during the critical years of 10 to 15, when many fathers have done some really stupid things, making their wives and children justifiably angry.

But men who stick it out, who stay married when marriage seems more like a swamp than a rose garden, who put dreams on the shelf and figuratively dig ditches to feed their families with commitment as well as groceries, discover that remote control never works with teenagers.

Being a father is more than contributing sperm and money. Being a father is making mistakes and admitting them. Being a father is being ashamed and seeking forgiveness. Being a father is being there when times are tough, even if you don't know how.

Being a father is telling children that getting drunk, using drugs, stealing cars, and running from police are habits that increase the odds they will die. A father's habit of skipping Tuesday morning breakfast, before they are old enough to drink and drive, increases the odds they won't take the time to listen.
