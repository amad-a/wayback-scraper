ASPECTS OF THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE THEORY OF GUERRILLA WAR: A CRITIQUE OF THE WRITINGS OF REGIS DEBRAY

                                they fought for comes about in spite of their

                                what they meant, and other men have to fight

ONE      

REVOLUTION AND GUERRILLA WAR

 

A reading of history indicates that guerrilla warfare is not in itself a revolutionary form of struggle, for it has occurred in many periods of man's history as the classic way for the less well armed to fight the organised power of a strong opponent, and has often served as an adjunct of regular warfare in the purely military sense. It has been used by " reactionary and counter revolutionary forces,as well as by revolutionary movements; banditry has often had a guerrilla character"(1) .Going back to the eighteenth century, we find that guerrilla warfare was employed in the American War of Independence, especially in the Southern colonies (guerrillas like Francis Marion "the swamp fox"; committees of public safety "employing terror against the Tories, burned their homes, tarred and feathered them, and confiscated their property"(2)); in the American Civil War, partisan bands were extensively employed by the Confederacy organised as adjuncts of the regular armies (the Mosby's and the Ashby's)(3). On the Continent guerrilla warfare was employed against Napoleon both in the Russian campaign and in Spain; while back in the States the Indians pursued guerrilla warfare well into the later half of the nineteenth century. At the conclusion of the Spanish American War, guerrilla bands in the Philippines waged war on the Americans in the period 1899-1906(4). In the twentieth century guerrilla warfare bas been extensively waged in Arabia, China, Vietnam, Algeria, Greece, Palestine, Malaya, Philippines, Occupied Europe, Kenya, Cyprus, Cuba, to name only a few. At no time did communist parties claim or hold a monopoly over the conduct of guerrilla warfare. The communists were indeed insistent that "at no time have we seized upon one form of struggle as applicable to all situations"(5). The reason for the eruption of the struggle in their view,was inherent in all countries with deep seated social wrongs; there were apt to be at any given time, some people who can be inspired to take up arms, even when a revolutionary situation did not obtain, in the hope of changing their conditions of life. Such acts, whether spontaneous or conspiratorially planned, the communists were emphatic, were no more than isolated cases of desperation and\or adventurism.

 

Lenin had explicitly set down the pre requisites for the existence of a revolutionary situation ; this obtained when "the lower classes do not want to live in the old way, and the upper classes cannot carry on in the old way"(6). The revolution is impossible without a nationwide crisis affecting both exploited and exploiters. But what Lenin was talking about was really grounded in actual Russian particularity; what the Bolsheviks successfully carried though in Russia in 1917 was an armed insurrection; the success of the Revolution was only to be achieved later with the successful termination of the civil war, in which both regular and guerrilla warfare were extensively used. Lenin's  strictures have sounded hollow in the post second world war period, when revolutionaries were faced with a different social and political environment, and the struggle became much more protracted. Lenin's deterministic strictures were denied by reference to Marx's dictum, "World history would indeed be very easy to make, if the struggle were taken up only on condition of infallibly favourable chance". Lenin himself had denied the supreme role of guerrilla war(which later adherents were to ascribe to it) writing in 1906 that " the party of the proletariat can never regard guerrilla warfare as the only, or even as the chief method of struggle; this method must be subordinated to other methods; it must be commensurate with the chief methods of warfare, and must be ennobled by the enlightening and organising influence of socialism"(7). The Russian Revolution gave birth to two great debates, one related to the existence of a distinct Marxist strategy of war(the Unified Military Doctrine), and the other, concerning the doctrine of revolution from without(the Export of Revolution). Frunze, the main exponent of the "Unified Military doctrine", held that " a new science of war was necessary, which would express the social character of the working class as the new master of society"(8).He argued against the hierarchy and iron discipline of Trotsky's Red Army, and demanded instituting a democratised structure of command, with military commissars subordinated to political commissars, themselves elected  from below and not appointed from above. On the strategic doctrine of the proletarian army, Frunze insisted that the war of manoeuvre predominated over the war of position, and that the working class was superbly suited to conduct mobile offensive actions. The offensive was to be the new doctrine of the proletariat's army. Trotsky, commissar for war, rejected the idea of the existence of a distinct Marxist strategy of war. He regarded military affairs as a technical branch of science. In 1922 , he wrote, "There is not and there has never been a military science. There does exist a whole number of sciences upon which military affairs rest"(9). He argued that manoeuvrability and the offensive were nothing peculiarly proletarian, quoting French military manuals and the works of Marshal Foch(10),and insisted that those who talked about the new science both misunderstood Marxism, and were falsely generalising the specific conditions of the Civil War. The doctrine of Revolution From Without was championed by Tukachevsky(11). He advocated offensive proletarian war against neighbouring bourgeois states to overthrow capitalism and help install the local communist parties in power. He called for the creation of an International General Staff of the Proletarian Revolution, to co- ordinate external operations all over the continent(12). Tukachevsky had the opportunity to put his ideas into practice in the Polish Campaign(which incidentally was opposed by Trotsky) and which ended in total defeat for the Red Army at the gates of Warsaw. Very little was heard of the doctrine in the following years(it could of course be argued that the communist states of Eastern Europe were set up in exactly the way Tukachevsky had advocated).

 

It was however in China that the theory and practice of peoples war was being tested; the historical result of this massive struggle was the formulation which had eluded the Soviet debates of the 1920's; a Marxist strategy that theorised a new form of military conflict - the peoples protracted war.

 

Mao formulated in practice his doctrine of guerrilla war and produced a new theory of revolutionary practice unifying within it, politics and war. This was based on a number of considerations; that the revolutionaries consider mass support the primary condition for their success, thus winning and maintaining popular support remain the central objective of the guerrilla movement throughout the struggle; that the popular support of the guerrilla is predicated upon the moral alienation of the masses from the existing government, thus the revolutionaries chief aim is to activate and perpetuate the moral isolation of the enemy, until such isolation becomes total and irrevocable.Mao emphasizes that the steps leading up to a revolutionary war cannot be created by conspiracy; they are partly inherent in a situation of rapid social change where the ruling elite fails over an extended period to formulate and maintain a process of legitimization, as well as transforming the social and economic set up. The revolutionary guerrilla movement, concentrates on out-administering and not on out-fighting(though this too) the enemy. Thus the undertaking as such is a constructive and not a destructive one( a point constantly ignored by theoreticians of counter revolutionary war). The use of terror by the guerrillas should be largely foregone; when it is necessary to use it, it should be highly selective and combined with other forms of political action to make its meaning clear. Finally, the existence of an external sanctuary though important at various stages of the struggle, is of greater psychological and diplomatic than military or political value to the guerrillas(13). The struggle cannot begin before the organisation of a solid communist apparatus ; the Red Army develops from, and under the control of, this party apparatus, where the military command is constantly subordinate to the political leadership. Base areas must be established as a precondition for development of a protracted war. The war launched by the Red Army from its secure and fixed bases should aim at the countryside slowly encircling the cities. The war is conducted through a united front of all patriotic and nationalist forces, and proceeds through three stages. Initially it takes the form of guerrilla operations, after a period of consolidation the struggle is stepped up and intensifies, to be enshrined finally in a regular war of position. The revolutionary forces are composed of local militia, guerrilla units, and regular forces. The war can only be successfully terminated after the success of the third stage. Transition from one stage to the next is not mechanical and varies according to local conditions; the most difficult stage is undoubtedly the opening one, the guerrillas being then at their weakest and with little experience. The socialist revolution is to be launched only after the protracted war against the enemy has been won. This was more or less the course of the Chinese armed struggle following the failure of the urban revolution in 1927. Lin Piao, veteran of the struggle from its early beginnings, emphasised the relevance of the Chinese experience to the struggle in the Third World, and in the world wide confrontation between socialism and capitalism. His messages was that "the establishment of rural revolutionary base areas and the encirclement of the cities from the countryside, is of outstanding universal practical significance for the present revolutionary struggle of all the oppressed nations and peoples in Asia, Africa and Latin America."(14) Peoples war as practiced by the Chinese is held to carry universal validity on a global scale, with the Third World(the countryside) surrounding and defeating the capitalist world(the cities).

 

Two special factors of extreme importance have been noted in consideration of the Chinese experience, which it is argued, render it unique, and inapplicable to the struggle in the Third World. First, China was engaged in a war against a foreign occupying power, Japan, which makes a National Bloc policy operational, and which lends strength and adherents to the struggle waged by the red army : secondly, the size of China is a special factor, the communists could seize "a remote area(as indeed they did) and hold it long enough to restructure its social relations before any government invasion"(15). In the post war period with the speedy dismantling of colonial empires, and the relatively small size of most Third World states, these two main pre-conditions for a successful re-enaction of the Chinese model, remain unfulfilled. The nearest successful revolution to conform to the Chinese model was the Algerian Liberation war, with important qualifications. (The Vietnamese model, similar in many respects to China, yet possessing very unique characteristics, will for the sake of simplification be regarded as one with the Chinese).

 

The Algerian Liberation war(1954-1962) contributed next to nothing to the development of guerrilla theory, and its post independence development has been a direct consequence of its spontaneous and ideologically unrevolutionary outlook. In many ways it serves as a what should not be done model for revolutionaries. The Algerians, fighting against a colonial power, succeeded in securing a base in the relatively impenetrable Aures mountains. In both spheres,(a national struggle uniting diverse classes, and the securing of a relatively impenetrable base) the Algerian war satisfied the conditions of the Chinese model. Yet the success of the Algerian nationalists was due more to political than to military factors. In 1958 the paratroopers had won the battle for Algiers the capital, and by 1961, the army was winning the war in the countryside, having at the same time insulated the movement from its major sources of supply across the border. Yet the FLN continued to succeed in out-administering the French authorities, and in spite of severe military setbacks, the years 1960-62 witnessed a resurgence of violent mass activity in Algiers itself. Final political victory was a the result of a failure of the will in Paris(similarly with the Cyprus model), and unlike the Chinese, not victory in the field. Algeria did not proceed to the building of socialism after independence, and ties with the metropolitan country remained strong. The Algerian Revolution has been regarded by many observers as an example of a revolution that failed. It was left to Cuba to provide a new theory and practice of revolution.

 

The success of the Cuban Revolution is in itself based on a heresy, and constitutes a continuous provocation to communist orthodoxy. The armed struggle was initiated by a handful of men who were nearly completely wiped out in their first engagement; the guerrilla movement lacked a coherent political social and economic program, and the leadership of the movement combined both political and military functions. Guevara, who sought to build a new model for revolution on the basis of the Cuban experience, claimed that Cuba contributed three fundamental lessons to the conduct of the revolutionary movement in Latin America(16). First, that popular forces can win against the enemy; second ,that it is not necessary to wait until all conditions for making the revolution exist, the revolution itself can create them; and third, that in underdeveloped Latin America, the countryside is the main arena of armed struggle. The guerrilla fighter is thus above all "an agrarian revolutionary"(17). Guevara called for the creation of the professional revolutionary, his job, to make the revolution, his politics to be born in the struggle itself.

 

Guevara's formulation is a challenge to the leadership of the orthodox communist parties of Latin America. They have continuously voiced their belief in Revolution, insisted on the primary role of the urban working class, and rejected any one road to revolution (in practice, their favourite path has been the electoral road, and the United Front tactic) The communist leadership takes its cue from revolutionary doctrine as expounded by the Soviet Union. With the advent of the policy of peaceful co existence and the Sino Soviet split, the Kremlin has declared orthodoxy in the Third World to consist of three main tenets. First, that the national bourgeoisie is essentially progressive(18); secondly, that in the changing balance of force between the Socialist and the capitalist bloc, armed revolution is not necessarily the path  to emancipation and development(19); and thirdly that the center of world revolution remains in the industrialised developed world(20)(a rejection of the Chinese-Cuban thesis that the weakest link of the international capitalist system, and its most sensitive one, is in the Third World). Thus from the vantage point of communist orthodoxy, the Cuban rebel band followed a Blanquist strategy, described by Engels as the view  "that a relatively small number of resolute well organised men would be able at a given favourable moment, not only to seize the helm of the state but also to keep power, by energetic and unrelenting action until they had succeeded in drawing the mass of the people into the revolution by marshaling around a small band of leaders"(21). Yet the fact was that the rebel band had succeeded in Cuba, and in a relatively short time(the revolution did, contrary to popular myth, possess a political movement - the July 26th movement- and had intensive contacts with other progressive forces on the Island and in exile; the armed struggle was only a part- the most important- of a much larger political struggle against the dictatorship).

 

In the 1960's Latin America abounded with small organisations of Fidelesta, some of whom had broken away from the communist parties while others had came from the reformist nationalist parties, and who laboured to re-enact the Cuban model in their respective countries. Invariably, every single one of these movements failed. Every Foco installed by revolutionaries had by the late 1960's been eradicated by the army . To many, it seems that the failure of the strategy is not inherent in itself, but was due to the faulty understanding and application of the Cuban lessons. The Cuban Revolution in its success,has posed the problem of alliances and fronts ; of the revolutionary potentialities of the national bourgeoisie; of the role of the vanguard; of the maturity of the revolutionary situation;of the necessity of the revolutionary party; and of the extent to which the subjective factors could either utilise or create the revolutionary situation(22). Without formulating the correct answer to these questions, victory was impossible.

 

R.Debray applied himself to the task of formulating the answers to the questions posed. In his earliest writings on Latin America(1963) he was already expounding the thesis that the countryside rather than the town is the main field of struggle. His early writings attempt to formulate a short account of the political context of the revolutionary movement, & forwards the contention that the Cuban Revolution, by its very success, has revolutionised the forces of counter revolution more speedily and effectively than it did the revolutionary movement itself. But it was only in 1967 with the publication in Havana of  Revolution in the Revolution, that he put forward a comprehensive view of the unfolding of the revolutionary process. The book appeared at a time when the Fidelesta forces had sustained a shattering defeat and a shift was taking place from the countryside back to the town. Yet until the full implications of the defeat of Guevera's Bolivian venture became apparent, the Cuban model continued to posses a certain degree of validity(and still does). Urban guerrillas have seized upon Debray's formulation and are trying to adapt them to their conditions. In the long run, the aim of the guerrilla groups is still manifestly to open a guerrilla front in the countryside; the shift is a purely tactical one. The preparatory stage of the struggle can only be initiated in the towns; Debray's analysis is seen as misrepresenting the real nature of the development of the Cuban Revolution.

 

The work itself has been described as " an accurate account of the thinking of the leaders of the Cuban Revolution on those subjects.....we have here for the first time a comprehensive and authorised presentation of the revolutionary thought of Castro and Guevara"(23). This was to be the authorised version of the Revolution as released by Havana, and an institutionalisation of the Cuban model. The Cuban introduction to the book says that , "no one else who has written about the Cuban Revolution has had access to such a wealth of material for historical research..(he had) complete access to the leaders of the Cuban Revolution and to unpublished documents"(24). Indeed this was how Debray's work was received , by both communists and revolutionaries. Cuba's success had itself posed the question whether its particularity could be generalised into a workable model for continental revolution. Debray sets out to prove that the answer is yes.

 

 

TWO       

THE DEBRAY THESIS

 

Introduction

The outstanding feature of Debray's ideas lies in their striking contradictions (the earlier articles versus Revolution in the Revolution)(1). The argument has been put forward that these two periods compliment each other(2); the first, dwelling on the socio-political aspects of the struggle, the latter on the armed struggle itself. Such a presentation does not however seem tenable. Nowhere does Debray present an economic, social and political analysis of Latin American conditions; yet in his writings there is a consistent mixture of the military and the political. A large part of "Revolution..." is concerned with refuting the strategy of the orthodox Latin American Communist parties, while his first article(3) concentrates mainly on the military side and is a refutation of the viability of urban guerrilla action.

 

It is undeniable ,however ,that there is a strong strand of continuity in his works. As early as 1963 he was putting forward the theory of the rural Foco as the correct form of struggle, and repudiating the reformist policies of the American communist parties. What is missing in his latter works is the emphasis on the political struggle; his repudiation of the promise not to set up a  Cuban Model, irrespective of the particularity of each country's conditions, and his emphatic rejection of the armed struggle as a categorical imperative. By 1967 the political lines in Latin American had hardened with the open split between left revolutionaries and orthodox communists, and Debray ascribed to his theories a continental significance. The following section will deal briefly with his earlier formulations.

 

Revolution and Counter Revolution

Debray saw the success of the Cuban Revolution as the direct cause of the transformation of the conditions of transformation of Latin America. In the struggle, there are "no military or political experiences that are unilateral"(4), and Cuba's success had revolutionised the forces of counter revolution more speedily and effectively than it did the revolutionary movement itself. Imperialism, possessing all the material means of organised violence and a determination to preserve the status quo is in a better position to learn the lessons of the past. Thus the door which Cuba had prised open has been solidly bolted; from within by the ruling oligarchies uniting into a single counter revolutionary front, from without by imperialism reinforcing its military repressive apparatus, and extending increased military aid and training programs. An attention to theory is thus the highest priority for Cuba; " the triumph of revolutionary spontaneity has become the death of that spontaneity"(5). The efforts to set up a Foco in slavish imitation of Cuba had all foundered, but Castro insisted that the fault lay with the revolutionaries; the objective conditions for revolution existed, but there was "an absence of subjective conditions in the vanguard"(6). It is imperative to recognise that Cuba had put an end to all models, even a Cuban one, and revolutionaries have to make their own way, to find a new and unsuspected weakest link.

 

Debray regards his Foco theory as a formulation of the Lenninst concept of the weakest link in the special conditions of Latin America; only a minority organisation of professional revolutionaries is regarded capable of carrying through a successful struggle. Conscious of similarities with Blanquism, he denies that the Foco aims at seizing power on its own or thorough military defeat of the enemy, but that it "aspires to engage the masses themselves in the struggle"(7). The struggle is national and anti colonial, thus a vanguard party is not an indispensable condition as Cuba has demonstrated. Moreover a National Liberation struggle " cannot be conducted under the banner of the Marxist Lenninst Party"(8) because of the aristocrisation of the small working class, and the struggles national character. But the Foco does not constitute a strategy in itself and its appearance pre supposes an analysis of "national contradictions understood in class terms"(9). The pre conditions can only be fulfilled by deciding by whom, where, with what program and with what alliances, and within what integrating strategy can the armed struggle be launched. The working class, numerically insignificant, bureaucratic, corrupt, and removed from the main battle field, cannot play the leading role; this falls to the poor peasantry who are the truly vanguard class. The basic area of the struggle is the countryside. The Battle of Caracas showed that the urban guerrilla cannot win(10); he has no hope of ever developing into a regular army capable of confronting the established army, he cannot convert people, and cannot perform his role as a social reformer. The only conclusion possible is that drawn by the Venezuelan guerrillas themselves , who withdrew into the country side as the only viable environment for the successful continuation of the armed struggle.

 

Revolution in the Revolution

Debray sets himself three main tasks; to examine the lessons falsely drawn from the Cuban Revolution, to discuss the tactics used by Latin American revolutionaries(and which led to failure), and to attempt a formulation of the correct strategy for armed struggle in the whole Continent.

 

It is because of an overdue emphasis on theory, Debray suggests, that so much confusion exists among the ranks. The main danger lies in the assertion that "the Cuban model can no longer be repeated in Latin America". Though the increased awareness on the part of revolutionaries of the revolutionary works of Mao, Giap, Lenin is basically beneficial, Latin America's possession of highly distinct and special conditions leads Debray to conclude that, " all the theoretical works on peoples war do as much harm as good "(11). Revolution has to be learnt in practice through the actual process of armed struggle. He holds up the model of the Cuban leadership; "one may well consider it a stroke of good luck that Fidel had not read the military writings of Mao before disembarking on the coast of Oriente"(12). But this one model, the Cuban, due to the similarity of conditions does apply to the rest of Latin America. Its actual processes offer an answer to the most central question: how to overthrow the power of the capitalist state. This, Debray poses as essentially a problem of finding a solution to the question of the breaking up of the reactionary army. The Cuban answer is the building up, through guerrilla warfare in suitably chosen areas of "a mobile strategic force"(13). The problem in Latin American cannot but be posed in a military form; any political line "which in terms of its consequences, is not susceptible to expression as a precise and consistent military line, cannot be considered revolutionary"(14).

 

In examining revolutionary struggles in Latin America, Debray points to the failure of Armed Self Defense in Colombia(1964) and Bolivia(1965). The reason for this failure is inherent in the defensive nature of the method. Both these movements accepted the System and fought defensively within it for narrow interests. This is implicitly a denial of the role of the armed unit which is organically separate from the civilian population. It is an attempt to repeat the successful Chinese and Vietnamese models in Latin America. But while in China armed self defense zones extended to areas already military secure, and viable as a result of the war being waged on other fronts with regular and mobile troops, in Latin America Armed Self Defense has been used to create autonomous zones. The Bolivian miners defeat by the regular army in 1965 was ample proof of the impossibility of successfully carrying through an armed insurrection against a modern well equipped army. Debray draws the lesson that "in order to destroy one army, another is necessary"(15). This can only be born through the guerrilla struggle.

 

Self Defense reduces the guerrilla force to an exclusively tactical role; its responsibilities(the protection of peasant families) hinder it from playing an offensive role. While it is able to provide protection for the population for a limited time, in the long run "it undermines the security of the civilian population"(16). In order to destroy the repressive shock forces of the state, the revolution needs a shock force of its own.

 

Debray turns his attention to another favourite of Latin American revolutionaries, Armed Propaganda. This they consider a preliminary to direct action, and is aimed at winning popular support. Thus the first nucleus of fighters is divided into small propaganda patrols, which cover the mountains, go into the villages, holding meetings, making speeches, explaining the social goals of the revolution. Debray views these attempts at political agitation as based on a misconception of the real nature of state power.

 

The problem is one of state violence; "the neo-colonialist ideal is still to show force in order not to have to use it"(17). In order to destroy the idea of the unassailability of the forces of order, there is nothing better than combat. The guerrillas are not like fish in water. They themselves initially are "the foreigners, lacking status, who at the beginning can offer the populace nothing but bloodshed"(18). Armed Propaganda ,other than being a lengthy process, weakens the initially small forces of the guerrillas. The Foco would have to be divided into small patrols with insignificant firepower. Thus instead of the guerrillas first tearing up the enemy's fingers, they open up and spread their own fingers. The best and only form of successful Armed Propaganda is direct action, physical destruction of enemy material and personnel; thus, "the destruction of a troop transport truck, or the public execution of a police torturer, is more effective propaganda than a hundred speeches"(19). This kind of action shows that the revolution is on the march, and that the enemy is no longer invulnerable. Debray calls on Castro's example who "during two years of warfare, did not hold a single political rally"(20). In itself, Armed Propaganda, conducted before the start of guerrilla operations, is a provocation to the enemy, exposes the guerrillas to danger, and the future and possible zones of operations to government action.

 

Linked to his rejection of Self Defense Zones and Armed Propaganda, Debray also denies the feasibility of a Fixed Guerrilla Base. Special factors in China(extensive territory, high density of population, absence of airborne troops, existence of regular guerrilla armies) facilitated the success of this strategy. None of the necessary conditions however, exist in Latin America. For the guerrilla force to attempt to occupy a fixed base is to deprive itself of its major weapon, mobility , and to permit itself to be contained within a fixed zone of operation, offering the enemy an identifiable target. Debray denies the myth of inaccessible zones, for "no place is inaccessible; if anyone has been able to reach it, then so can the enemy"(21). A fixed base plays into the enemy's hands, whose ideal is "to wage a short war to destroy the Foco in its embryonic stage", and a fixed base affords him just such a target, before the guerrillas have linked themselves closely to the local population or acquired a minimum of experience. There can only be one guerrilla base in the initial period, and that is the territory where the guerrilla happens to be moving.

 

A major part of Debray's work is devoted to a polemical attack on the leadership of the orthodox communist parties for not embracing the strategy of the armed struggle. They subordinate the guerrilla Foco to the needs of their political struggles in the City, and treat guerrilla war as only one of several possible political tactics. This resulting subordination of the military to the political leadership of the city leads to harmful effects. The leadership in the towns, out of touch with the actual conditions in the mountains, cannot understand the necessities of the struggle, for "the jungle of the city is not so brutal; life is for all unequally given, but given nevertheless"(22). The result is logistic and military dependence; seen from the outside, the vital needs of the guerrillas are "details", "material limitations of the class struggle", or simply "the technical side". Debray calls for a unification of the military and the political leadership, as the absence of a single command means that "there is no clear strategy, and in the absence of a clear strategy no plan for action"(23). The guerrilla groups are reduced to anarchy in their pursuit of the struggle. This vital necessity to adopt a clear and explicit strategy can only be achieved by answering questions like - What is the fundamental form of class struggle ? What is the terrain ? What is the primary objective ? To all these questions the communists give answers which are at variance with the needs and objectives of Latin America and its revolutionary militants.

 

The struggle in the mountains results in the practical transformation of the guerrilla into a socialist revolutionary. While the communists wait for the successful revolution before they can educate the masses, the guerrilla army educates people in the struggle and contributes towards the creation of socialist man . The first stages of life in the mountain are devoted to unlearning the degrading habits of the city; it is "a battle within the guerrilla himself to overcome his old habits, to erase the marks left on his body by the incubator of the city"(24), to recreate himself the new man.

 

Debray poses the question central to his thesis as a choice between the party and the guerrilla. In previous revolutions, the object was not the destruction of the repressive apparatus (the army) but the seizure of state power in order to transform the social structure. The peoples army was thus an instrument of its implementation, while it was the party, the vanguard of the class , which determined the political context, and directed the military apparatus. In Latin America, Debray insists that the situation is inherently different. Unlike China and Vietnam where "the class struggle took the form of a patriotic war"(25), Latin America is composed of politically independent states, where the communist parties, for various reasons, did not take root and develop. Thus the pre-requisites of the struggle are different. Echoing Castro ("Who will make the Revolution in Latin America ? The people, the revolutionaries, with or without the party"(26), Debray states that there is "no metaphysical equation in which vanguard = Marxist Leninist party"(27). Those who want to make the revolution can go ahead and constitute themselves the vanguard. The time has passed for believing that it suffices to be in the party to be a revolutionary. The value of a revolutionary, like that of a party, depends on activity, and primarily on armed activity.

 

The guerrilla movement does not seek to create a new party; it seeks rather to wipe out and unify within its ranks, round the most urgent military tasks, doctrinal and party divisions. This implies abolition of the "plethora of commissions, secretariats, congresses, etc."(28). Eventually the future peoples army will itself beget " the party of which it is to be theoretically the instrument. Essentially the party is the army"(29). At the same time the guerrilla movement alone can achieve a class alliance between workers and peasants, and is a confirmation in action of this alliance. As Debray sees it, "in the mountains, the workers, peasants, and intellectuals meet for the first time"(30).

 

The shortest amount of space in given to the actual problem of the setting up of the Armed Foco. Following the Cuban experience, Debray enunciates guerrilla war as going though three stages, "the stage of establishment, the stage of development, and the stage of revolutionary offensive"(31). The first stage is the hardest to surmount, and the most exposed to all sorts of accidents.

 

The guerrilla unit is visualised initially as a small group completely self-supporting and scrupulously shunning the rural population. It does not assume its defense and avoids any open contact with peasants, operating in complete secrecy. The maxim of the group is "constant vigilance, constant mistrust, and constant mobility"(32). The essential watch word is Security and the guerrillas must "maintain a certain aloofness" from the civilians in a given zone as the latter are vulnerable to infiltration and moral corruption by the enemy. This mistrust should encompass the guides who help the guerrillas, and comrades who have to make various trips to the towns, as the latter "demoralise and disorganise weak members of the Foco"(33).

 

As far as the recruits are concerned, in addition to conviction, Debray mentions physical fitness as the most basic of all skills, to the extent that "there is a close link between ideology and biology". A perfect Marxist education is not an imperative condition, but "physical aptitude is the pre-requisite of all other aptitudes"(34). The group requires no political commissars and no cadre training schools(both manifestations of subordination to the party). The Foco itself is the best education for future leaders. Simultaneously, Democratic Centralism has no place within the Foco; decisions belong to the command. Organising meetings and elections lead to a relaxation of conscious discipline, and to a loss of efficiency and self confidence. The Foco should be conducted in a strictly military professional manner.

 

The Setting Up of A Model

Debray in putting forward an argument for the decisive role of violence rests his arguments on two premises which are to some degree inter-dependent : first, that no possessing class gives up power without a struggle, and second, that no popular movement which fails successfully to challenge the ruling powers monopoly of violence, will be capable of the social energy necessary to invent a totally new order of society(35). Latin America has had a long tradition of popular violent insurrections: looking back only to the last fifty years we note the Mexican Revolution, Sandino's guerrillas in Nicaragua, the Tenente rebellion led by Prestes in Brazil, Columbia's frustrated class struggle of the Violencia, and the aborted Bolivian Revolution in 1952.

 

The defense which Debray formulated on behalf of the Foco is basically that it has been misunderstood. Debray concedes that it is utopian to see it as an isolated military detonator organised by itself and independent of an urban network, but contends that his aim was "to break down the theoretical and mental block hindering the development of the armed struggle only where it was already under way"(36). Thus the work is concerned not with the question of whether a revolutionary situation exists or not, but what happens when it does (Venezuela, Guatemala, Colombia). Yet Debray admits that there is in his writings an ambiguity of the "Descriptive and the normative lending itself (despite its own self) to the setting up of a Model"(37). But he insists that he was not consciously trying to set up a model. Cuba is used as an example and "an example is not a model"(38). This choice was based on the fact that Cuba is the least known and most talked about revolution. At the same time the emphasis on the military is not exclusive, it is only under specific conditions that " the political can be expressed mainly in a military form"(39), while the absence of a socio political analysis is indicative that it is not the main aim of the book and is provisionally taken for granted.

 

What Debray is saying in essence is that the whole Foco theory is not what it seems to be, and while trying to raise some defense of his theories, he accepts as justified criticism that whatever the intentions, his work has been interpreted both by supporters and detractors as an effort to build a Cuban Model for revolution throughout Latin America. That as such it is grossly inadequate and moreover has been a prescription for failure.

 

THREE       

A THEORY OF PURE ACTIVISM

 

The Cuban Revolution

 

Debray's impressive credentials as regards the Cuban experience are sadly irrelevant to his presentation of the practice of the Cuban revolutionaries. It departs dramatically from their actual revolutionary practice, is selective in its emphasis and strongly sectarian in its recommendations. Debray simply does not come to grips with the Cuban Revolution.

 

The implication throughout is that Cuba was ripe for Revolution in the 1950's simply as a result of exploitation and misery. Yet on these grounds, most societies should have been ripe for revolution from the beginning of human history. We are not treated to any discussion of the special constellation of economic, social and political conditions which made the Revolution possible. Cuba's success in making the Revolution without a vanguard party(indeed in spite of the communist party till late 1958) only explains its particularity and not its generality as Debray would like us to think. Initially the Revolution did not have a socialist character and it was not Castro's guerrillas who made possible Cuba's transformation from Liberation to Socialism, but the alliance with the Communist Party after the termination of the armed struggle. The fighting in the Sierra Maestra need not necessarily have led to socialism. Not all revolutionary movements have succeeded ; in a number of cases the guerrilla leaders degenerated into self-seeking corrupt politicians, and the peoples army became a self perpetuating Junta. Algeria is a glaring example.

 

The role of the bourgeoisie in Cuba was decisive. The revolution did not succeed in the face of bourgeois opposition but with its support. Castro, in his defense in 1953 after the aborted attack on the Moncada Barracks, did not frighten the liberal bourgeoisie. He demanded civil rights, justice, libertarian freedoms, a fair share for the masses, and a return to constitutionality. During the struggle, he maintained contact with the cities and never alienated the bourgeoisie to the extent that it would turn against him. Two Cuban authors(1), commenting on Debray's writings, have emphasised that at certain stages of the struggle, the revolution was completely dependent on Bourgeois participation in the shape of supplies of arms and money. The national bourgeoisie played an important part in the struggle, one that is unlikely to be repeated in any other Latin American country. Debray's observation on the struggle between the City and the Sierra, a reflection of the lack of homogeneity of the forces participating in the struggle, is irrelevant to the rest of Latin America. There, the revolutionary forces are openly socialist, and the character of the revolution will be socialist from the start. After Cuba's success, as Debray himself reminds us, the forces of reaction will not be taken by surprise. The bourgeoisie becoming increasingly less national, will force a direct confrontation of classes from the beginning. This in itself necessitates the formation of the revolutionary party.

 

Debray argues that Cuba made the revolution without the party, yet it was a political organisation, the 26th July Movement, which gave birth to the armed struggle. The guerrillas did not create the party. The July 26th Movement(with no counterpart in     Debray's model) formed the guerrilla forces, the embryo of the rebel army, which only after victory, developed in association with the communist party. It is a misreading of history and the facts to state that the Cuban Revolution was started by the Foco: by the time Granmma landed, Cuba had long been well prepared for invasion. The Foco was only the center of a much bigger revolutionary movement, comprising ,besides the July 26th Movement, the Student Directorate, and various other organisations both in Cuba and in exile. These carried out intensive propaganda from the early 1950's, while the communist party influenced certain sectors of the working class promoting other complimentary forms of political activity in the overall context of the struggle. The Cuban people indeed had a long history of organisational experience and mass struggle, and Castro's victory was only "the culmination of a long series of thwarted revolutions"(2). Added to this were the special conditions of the Sierra Maestra, characterised by Guevara as the emergence of a dissatisfied class of middle peasants. The Students Directorate itself waged a campaign of urban violence, which pinned down a large part of the government forces and produced intensive counter terror, leading to the loss of the last shreds of support for Batista. The Foco did not in itself constitute an organising magnet ; real activation came about through the various bodies composing the political organisations supporting the armed struggle, and not, as Debray would have us believe, due to the existence of a radio transmitter, a purely technical medium, elevated by Debray into an organisational apparatus mobilising people through the sole use of propaganda.

 

If Cuba signified anything, it was in its demonstration effect, showing that the Revolution is possible "in a micro-country, without the immense resources of China, and bordering the United States"(3). Its main features, absence of foreign intervention, bourgeois support, loss of legitimacy of the Batista regime, special conditions of the Sierra, proletarianisation of the peasants by capitalist agriculture, the small size of the Island, and effective leadership, makes for its uniqueness, and the particularity of its example.

 

The Party and the Foco

 

Debray's portrayal of the party-guerrilla relationship is posed in essentially geographic terms, with irreducible antagonistic content. Yet in the prevalent conditions of Latin America (different from Cuba), there is an imperative necessity for the revolutionary party to carry on the political task of combating bourgeois ideology in the countryside in what promises to be a long drawn out war. Debray, writing an entire book on how to make the revolution says precious little about the development of political consciousness. Activism is the only form of political work/education he recognises. The party has no useful role to play. At the same time, he "applies to all parties in general, the criticisms of a particular genus of political parties"(4). He rejects the whole method because of a particular (the communist)use of that method.

 

A problem, central to Marxism, the relationship between the guerrilla and the peasant  is scarcely mentioned. The only specific suggestion we are offered to win over the peasants is Combativeness. Debray never discusses what the guerrillas fight for. But without a program and specific socio-economic reforms, the struggle between the state and the guerrillas would appear to the peasantry as "a squabble between two parties equally alien to them."(5).

 

The subordination of the city to the countrywide is based on a complete under-estimation of the political process. We are not told how the old party can be repudiated "without losing links with the working class"(6), or how to arouse the support of the exploited masses for the guerrillas, so that they will be the vanguard of the exploited, and not simply their own vanguard; or how the activities of the guerrilla fronts could be centrally organised. To the large and politically organised section of the urban working class, Debray has nothing to say,, except perhaps that they should make for the mountains.

 

As far as the Foco is concerned, Debray envisages a whole period (initiation of the Foco to final victory) during which the military struggle is the only form of political activity. Only after a new party develops can political activity be pursued. To the orthodox communist parties, irrelevant to the armed struggle, Debray finds it impossible to visualise an alternative. Revolutionary action thus becomes the creator of political consciousness. But the communist parties themselves have fought in a number of places (Venezuela, Columbia), and many right wing groups have been known to take up arms. This did not create within them the consciousness that Debray assumes the actual process of fighting would. The lesson seems to be that war itself does not unify nor create revolutionaries. What does, is common objectives. This pre-supposes an ability "to analyse the historical process correctly,(that is) an organisation which is disciplined and cohesive- a party"(7).

 

What we are told about the formation of the Foco is very sketchy (the Foco is assumed already armed, equipped, installed and entrenched). Indeed , nowhere does Debray pose the question of how the Foco is to be prepared or who will prepare it. His answer is a tactical formulation for the assembly of cadres, exploring the terrain and engaging the enemy. But how are the guerrillas to shoot in such a way that the exploited masses will understand that these shots are fired by their vanguard, and to go on to participate in the struggle ? We are not told how the guerrilla peasant relationship is to be formed. Initially the guerrilla is advised to shun the civilian population, but in attempting to become invisible to the enemy, the Foco runs the risk of becoming invisible to the peasants (contrast with Mao)(8). In a sense, this prescription for avoidance of the population shows an awareness that the conditions for revolution do not really exist. The Foco is not only distinct from the rural population it is also distrusted by it. The three golden rules of conduct are based on a constant fear of betrayal, "constant vigilance, mistrust, mobility"(contrast with Mao's Three Rules and Eight Points)(9). In Debray's own words, the guerrilla goes through a phase of absolute nomadism.

 

What is so blatantly missing throughout Debray's analysis is any meaningful mention of the Peruvian experience(10). Luis de la Puente, who had spent some time in Cuba, was consciously trying to imitate the Cuban experience, and he failed in record time. His failure casts doubts on the validity of many of Debray's formulation, yet this is one of the major guerrilla experiences in Latin America, which he claims to be examining, but which he inexplicably chooses to ignore.

 

Debray's denial of any role for the urban masses, in addition to his strictures on the inherent bourgeois nature of the city, leads him to a complete rejection of armed urban action. This not only contradicts the Cuban and the Venezuelan experiences, but in addition we have the development of urban action in the late 1960's as a direct refutation of Debray's recommendations . As long ago as 1915 , Connoly wrote on the suitability of the city for armed actions characterising it as "a huge maze of passes or glens formed by streets and lanes. Every difficulty that exists for the operation of regular troops in the mountains is multiplied a hundredfold in a city"(11). Contemporary urban guerrilla movements, engaging in diverse forms of action,(armed struggle as only one of the methods employed), have demonstrated the possibilities and the utility(in the short run at least) of such action.

 

The Necessity for Revolutionary Theory

 

Revolution in the Revolution sets out to challenge the traditional Marxist thesis of revolution as a movement of the industrial proletariat. At the same time, it rejects the Maoist version of a peasant guerrilla army led by the communist party. But while it is true that Latin America is not China, we must also recognise that it is not Cuba. Debray's main assumption, that Latin America is ripe for Revolution, is not substantiated by his observations ; yet his greatest weakness lies not in specific errors (large though these are), but in his attempt to prescribe a course which all Latin American revolutionaries must follow irrespective of the contrasting and differing conditions of the Continent. He is entitled to his rejection of revolutionary theory , but it is difficult to accept his contention that conditions in a whole region can be so unique so as to transcend history and render irrelevant past revolutionary theory, no less than practice. This is in no way a denial of the importance of the congruence between tactics and strategy and local conditions, without which no revolution can succeed. But Debray's thesis, based on the uniqueness of Latin America, does not bring out meaningfully those distinct features of the region which bear on his theory. We are presented with no distinguishing features which unite the Continent, and at the same time separate it from the rest of the underdeveloped world.

 

Latin America presents us with a number of objective features(12); a culture of poverty(13), pressure on the land, growth of population and urban explosion, degenerating terms of trade, continued military involvement in politics, and increased American political and economic intervention. But to Debray, an analysis of social change, of specific problems and demands of concrete groups, of current formulations of slogans and programs, seems unimportant and idle tasks. His works deal mainly with tactics and ignore problems of broad political strategy. He is pre-occupied with military techniques, and this leads him to demolish all alternative political strategies; urban insurrections, peasant leagues, self defense zones, Trotskyites , Maoists, Communist Parties - all are contaminated. But then who is left to make the Revolution ?

 

Debray poses a number of problems : the armed struggle and power, the peasantry and the countryside, class and strategic terrain;  his principle achievement lies in demonstrating that the orthodox communist parties are incapable of leading the proletariat to the conquest of power. But we are left with the question unanswered : Is the countryside the fundamental terrain of armed struggle because the peasantry as a class are more revolutionary than the proletariat ? Or, is it because the countryside as a strategic terrain is more propitious than the towns ? Is the main aim of the Foco to demoralise the  repressive apparatus or to accumulate forces within the exploited classes ? Debray stresses the increasing effectiveness of government control of rural areas, but he reduces this to fear of the central authority's coercive apparatus. This leads to a view of the revolutionary elite as an alien group sharing no communality of interest with the peasants. Inescapably we arrive at the primacy of the military factor as a means for subverting government legitimacy. The Foco becomes predominantly destructive, with a reversal of the stages of revolutionary war. The government is to be outfought before it is outadministered. The legitimacy of the system, understood predominantly in military terms, can only be subverted by military means. As such, Debray's Foco theory is ideally suited for the US counter insurgency program. The people are spectators in a military contest, granting their support to the winning side.

 

The deeper attitude underlying Debray's formulations seems to be that Latin America needs a revolution rather than that it is ripe for one, and his method ends up by becoming his program. He presents a mechanistic view of a guerrilla force without a political base, neither explaining its motives nor protecting its peasant sympathisers, developing the military power to topple a government . He forgets the basic rule of guerrilla warfare, that the guerrilla fighter is first and foremost a political partisan, and that his battle is for the political allegiance of the oppressed and the dispossessed.

 

FOUR      

THE NECESSITY OF STRUCTURAL CHANGE

 

The death of Che Guevara in Bolivia, hailed by many as the end of the armed revolutionary struggle, only signifies the failure of a particular approach to revolution - the strategy of the Rural Foco. Debray, primarily an organisational man, has reduced complex social and political processes to a simple organisational formula, universally valid. The method was shown to have failed, but the problem persists. What is the problem ?

 

For Latin America and the underdeveloped countries of Asia and Africa political decolonisation has not effected the rate or extent of economic exploitation. The international division of labour, characteristic of imperialism, is becoming more ,not less, marked; the underdeveloped produce and export more and more primary products, the industrial capitalist countries produce and export more and more manufactured goods. This international division of labour, far from being modified, has grown sharper, "for some the tasks of producing raw materials and the sub human living standards that go with it, for others industrial expansion and the concomitant high standard of living"(1). Within the underdeveloped world, taking Latin America as our example, basic land tenure and social conditions remain unchanged. Less than 5% of landowners own over 75% of the arable land; social class are still strictly segregated between riches and poverty. Mobility is nearly non-existent and social inequality and economic stagnation produce repeated interventions by the military. Political instability and military dictatorships are still typical in Latin America in 1972, The traditional ruling classes, the landed oligarchies and the comprador bourgeoisie, failing to overthrow the social, political and cultural obstacles in the way of modernisation, have been replaced in several countries by petty-bourgeois bureaucracies, often in alliance with the military, who in their turn have also failed to achieve the desired social and economic transformation. At most, they have nationalised the commanding heights of the economy, and undertaken relative agrarian reform, but are incapable of mobilising the masses reduced to unemployment, underemployment, and sometimes even emigration, to rebuild the country. This inability is reflected in the corruption and nepotism of these bureaucrats who themselves consume the major share of national income (a large part of course is spent on maintaining the military establishment, organised violence in the direct and open service of the ruling oligarchies).

 

The United States has tried through the Alliance For Progress to provide its own solution to the problem , and it has failed. Latin America's population grows explosively at the highest rate in the world, and "as they reproduce, Latin Americans are formulating a mute challenge for which the system has no answer"(2). The system has no way of incorporating a growing segment of the population into the productive process, though this segment is still quite small compared to the wide space it inhabits, and the fabulous economic potential of the soil and the subsoil. The attempts of the United States to " absorb the problem in the womb"(3) have failed. The political and military successes achieved by the military dictatorships and their US allies have not been based on any significant change in the structural factors underlying the social and political conflicts; the underlying pressures making for Revolution still exist. The 1970 victory of Allende in Chile, raised hopes (and seemed to prove the orthodox communist theses) that the electoral road to power is viable. Yet a repeat performance in Uruguay towards the end of 1971 failed to materialise. The only alternative to armed struggle is the appearance of progressive army juntas, like in Peru and Bolivia, but the latest coup in Bolivia has indicated the shortcomings and the easy reversibility of such a path.

 

If present Latin American experience is indicative of anything, it is of the need for a combination of different tactics in the pursuit of the armed struggle, and the rejection of the dependence on any one group or strategic terrain. The rural struggle is plainly irrelevant in countries like Brazil or the Argentine, both with an overwhelming urban concentration. Moreover, the system " can co-exist with a localised insurrection in the countryside"(4), ( as indeed is currently happening in Venezuela) because the countryside in marginal to the real power centres, the cities. On the other hand, urban guerrilla actions which failed in Caracas in the early 1960's and in Brazil in the later sixties, have flourished spectacularly in Uruguay. The Tupamaros have shown the ability of an urban guerrilla organisation to survive over time. They have demonstrated that the urban guerrilla can ( contrary to Debray) link  its  action to the concrete demands and aspirations of the masses. By winning donations for hospitals and schools, by exposing financial corruption, by punishing torturers, by backing workers on strike, they have won sympathy and popularity among the urban proletariat. A different experience is offered by Argentina, where in May 1969 a spontaneous armed uprising took place, and the workers battled with troops in barricaded cities (5). Since then urban guerrilla action has been on the increase.

 

At the same time, it is important to remember that Debray's thesis on the revolution revolutionising the counter-revolution retains its validity. Improved counter insurgency techniques have been increasingly implemented with US training and support ; according to official figures " more than 50,000 Latin American officers were trained by the US between 1950-1969; on the ground US agents and military advisors provide intelligence and technical guidance"(6). US involvement extended at times to direct military action. The same author (who is extremely favourable to US policies) reports that " the special forces conducted 52 anti subversive missions in Latin America, including parachute drops into guerrilla areas, in the single year of 1965"(7).

 

Debray's Foco theory has been assimilated by revolutionaries throughout the underdeveloped world(8), and even by groups in the post industrial societies of the West(9). Even those guerrillas in Latin America who operate in the cities accept many of his organisational formats, and the rationale underlying their actions is the belief in armed action itself as the small motor which will set the big motor of the masses into action. At the same time revolutionary experience on many fronts, and not least in Vietnam (10) which has been the formative influence for many revolutionary groups including and especially those in the post industrial societies, has been in sharp contradiction to many of Debray's formulations. To take a random example, PAIGC in Portuguese Guinea has in its practice negated the universal validity of the Foco theory and demonstrated that the armed struggle could not have been undertaken if " a period of more than two years had not been devoted beforehand to preparatory political work"(11). Indeed in Africa, relations of production in many countries are at a very primitive level with the exception of small urban islands, completely separated from the rest of the country. The problem of agrarian reform as such does not pose itself. Increasingly the problem of the revolutionary potential of the peasants has itself received increased attention, with special emphasis paid to differentiation within the ranks of the peasantry.(12)

 

A consensus of opinion is slowly forming which can perhaps best be summed up in Trotsky's words in 1928 that " without an alliance of the proletariat with the peasantry, the tasks of the democratic revolution cannot be solved....but the alliance of these two classes can be realised only through an irreconcilable struggle against the national bourgeoisie"(13). Yet neither the form which this alliance should take nor the methods it should adopt - insurrection, urban guerrilla, rural guerrilla, combined legal and illegal work, etc. -have been agreed upon by contemporary revolutionaries. (In Turkey for example, the urban Foco is still maintained, and the same in Iran after the defeat of an attempted rural Foco; Maoist parties pay lip service to the armed struggle but have nothing to show ; the Naxalites in India adopted a policy of bloody terrorism; the communists continue to adhere to the peaceful road though they do not discount the use of the armed struggle as a particular form of pressure; the varieties are really endless). But considering the protracted nature of the struggle and the relative strength of the antagonists, the outstanding feature of the whole phenomenon is the persistence and continuity of the repeated attempts, sustained by an inexplicable inner force...Gramsci's optimism of the will.
