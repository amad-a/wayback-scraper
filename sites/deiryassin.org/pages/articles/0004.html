Promised land

The Daily Telegraph
Saturday 11 April 1998

As Tony Blair prepares to visit Israel to mark that nation's 50th year of statehood, the distinguished historian Martin Gilbert examines Britain's shifting allegiances in the birth of a nation

DURING his visit to Israel in 1995 John Major went to the Holocaust memorial in Jerusalem, where he saw a tree planted in memory of a British soldier - Sergeant Coward - who had saved many Jewish lives in the Second World War. Three years later Robin Cook decided not to go to Yad Vashem, but to go instead to the site of the Arab village of Deir Yassin, where the massacre in 1948 heightened Arab-Jewish animosity and led to an upsurge in the flight of the Arab population of Palestine.

The contrast between these two visits typifies the continuing conflict in British attitudes to Israel. This conflict began in 1917, with the issuing of the Balfour Declaration, which promised the Jews a "national home' in Palestine. This promise was a turning point for Jewish national aspirations. It was also seen by its opponents in Britain as a blow to Arab national aspirations in the same area. The fact that the country was large enough to accommodate two peoples, perhaps even living in harmony within a single sovereign state, did not appease the more strident supporters of what increasingly became a clash of national aims.

There was also a crucial clause in the Balfour Declaration, which stated that it must be "clearly understood that nothing shall be done which may prejudice the civil and religious rights of non-Jewish communities in Palestine"'.

Despite this caution, there is no doubt that the British, who in 1917 hoped to get the support of both American and Russian Jewry for the Allied war effort, saw Jewish statehood as one possible outcome of the Balfour Declaration. In the minutes of the War Cabinet two days before the declaration was issued, Balfour himself - he was then Foreign Secretary - explained to his colleagues the meaning of the words "national home". He told them that he understood it to mean "some form of British, American, or other protectorate, under which full facilities would be given to the Jews to work out their own salvation and to build up, by means of education, agriculture and industry, a real centre of national culture and focus of national life".

A.J. Balfour added that Britain's commitment to a Jewish national home "did not necessarily involve the early establishment of an independent Jewish state, which was a matter for gradual development in accordance with the ordinary laws of political evolution".

How did the British Government envisage those "ordinary laws"? From the outset, the existence of an Arab majority within the boundary of Mandated Palestine was seen as a situation that could readily be resolved. Britain would hold the ring between Jew and Arab until such time as continuing Jewish immigration led to a Jewish majority.

This concept was first expressed soon after the Balfour Declaration by two Foreign Office advisers, both later to achieve prominence in other spheres, Lewis Namier and Arnold Toynbee, who wrote in a joint memorandum: "The objection raised against the Jews being given exclusive political rights in Palestine on a basis that would be undemocratic with regard to the local Christian and Mohammedan population is certainly the most important which the anti-Zionists have hitherto raised, but the difficulty is imaginary."

They added: "Palestine might be held in trust by Great Britain or America until there was a sufficient population in the country fit to govern it on European lines. Then no undemocratic restrictions of the kind indicated in the memorandum would be required . . ."

Just as John Major did in 1995 - and as Tony Blair may well do when he visits Israel after Easter for its 50th year of independence - the British Government sought 80 years ago to mediate between Jewish and Arab aspirations. In 1918, under British auspices, the leader of the Arab revolt, Emir Feisal, met the Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann at the port of Akaba. Lawrence of Arabia, who was present, believed that the two men could work out "the lines of Arab and Zionist policy converging in the not distant future".

On the first anniversary of the Balfour Declaration, T.E. Lawrence told a London Jewish newspaper: "Speaking entirely as a non-Jew, I am decidedly in favour of Zionism; indeed, I look on the Jews as the natural importers of western leaven so necessary for countries of the Near East."

Even before Jewish settlers began to arrive in Palestine with Britain's encouragement, Palestinian Arab hostility had emerged. In December 1918 a petition from several leading Arabs was sent to the Foreign Office. It was signed by individual Arabs from Haifa, Safed, Acre (all three cities in Israel today); from Gaza (where Yasser Arafat now has his headquarters); and from Hebron (the scene of current Arab-Israeli confrontation). The tone of the petition was uncompromising. "Should the Jews immigrate in great numbers into Palestine," the Arabs declared, "they will doubtless defraud the natives of their rights."

Encouraged to do so by Britain, Chaim Weizmann and Emir Feisal worked to find common ground between the Zionist and Arab positions. In January 1919 they reached an agreement that "all necessary measures" should be taken "to encourage and stimulate immigration of Jews into Palestine on a large scale, and as quickly as possible to settle Jewish immigrants upon the land through closer settlement and intensive cultivation of the soil". In taking such measures, the agreement went on, "the Arab peasant and tenant farmers shall be protected in their rights, and shall be assisted in forwarding their economic development".

Weizmann and Feisal also agreed that the Muslim Holy Places in Palestine would be under Muslim control, and that no law would be made to interfere "in any way with the free exercise of religion".

In Palestine, the policy of the British Mandate made it possible for the Jews to continue to build settlements, to welcome more and more Jews

from outside, and to seek autonomy in their community life. In 1919 The Times told its readers: "Our duty as the Mandatory power will be to make Jewish Palestine not a struggling state, but one that is capable of vigorous and independent national life."

Winston Churchill, then Secretary of State for War, endorsed this. "If," he wrote, "as may well happen, there should be created in our own lifetime by the banks of the Jordan a Jewish state under the protection of the British Crown which might comprise three or four millions of Jews, an event will have occurred in the history of the world which would from every point of view be beneficial."

As a result of the Churchill White Paper of 1922, within 20 years more than 350,000 Jews emigrated to Palestine. Among the early immigrants were many British Jews. Hostility to the Jewish efforts, and in particular to Jewish land-purchase and immigration, grew from 1929, with the Arab riots of that year. According to Ernest Bevin, then a Trade Union leader, the riots arose from Arab peasant/proletarian resentment at the activities of a Jewish plutocracy.

The Jewish experiment still had its friends in London. At a secret session of a Royal Commission in 1937, Churchill was emphatic that the British Government had committed itself "to the idea that some day, somehow, far off in the future, subject to justice and economic convenience, there might well be a great Jewish state there, numbered by millions, far exceeding the present inhabitants of the country".

Churchill added: "We never committed ourselves to making Palestine a Jewish state . . . but if more and more Jews gather to that Home and all is worked from age to age, from generation to generation, with justice and fair consideration to those displaced and so forth, certainly it was contemplated and intended that they might in the course of time become an overwhelmingly Jewish state."

It was another White Paper, in May 1939, when Neville Chamberlain was Prime Minister, that took cognisance of extreme Arab hostility to Zionist settlement in Palestine and determined to make a majority Jewish state impossible at any time in the future. That month the House of Commons approved by an overwhelming majority what was in effect an Arab veto, after five years, on all further Jewish immigration. Meanwhile, from May 1939 to May 1944, Jewish immigration was to be restricted to a maximum of 100,000.

Even with these newcomers the Arabs would remain a clear majority. After the five years the Arabs would be able to establish majority rule, at which point they had made it clear that they would halt any further Jewish immigration.

Following the outbreak of war in September 1939 the British interned all "illegal" Jewish refugees who managed to reach Palestine in a camp south of Haifa. But when Churchill was Prime Minister he supported Colonial Office decisions that by-passed the restrictions on Jewish immigration to Palestine. Under one of these decisions, any Jewish refugees reaching Istanbul from the terrors of Central Europe were admitted to Palestine, irrespective of existing quotas or visa requirements. Many thousands of Jews availed themselves of this decision.

Determined not to allow the 1939 White Paper policy to be put into effect, in 1943 Churchill informed the War Cabinet: "I cannot in any circumstances contemplate an absolute cessation of immigration into Palestine at the discretion of the Arab majority." He also suggested that Britain should turn two former Italian colonies, Eritrea and Tripolitania, "into Jewish colonies, affiliated, if desired, to the National Home in Palestine".

As various Partition plans were being discussed in London for the creation of both an Arab and a Jewish state in a divided Palestine, Churchill told the Chiefs of Staff: "Obviously we shall not proceed with any plan of partition which the Jews do not support."

There was far less willingness to admit Jewish refugees to Britain. The Home Secretary, Herbert Morrison, warned the Cabinet in 1943: "It should be borne in mind that there were already about l00,000 refugees, mainly Jews, in this country, and that the accommodation problem was already most difficult and would become critical in the event of renewed air attacks."

There was, Morrison added, "considerable anti-Semitism under the surface in this country. If there were any substantial increase in the number of Jewish refugees or if these refugees did not leave this country after the war, we should be in serious trouble."

As the Allied victory drew near Churchill spoke of presiding over a Middle East peace conference at which a sovereign Jewish entity would emerge, as part of a wider Middle East federation. His hope was to bring Chaim Weizmann and King Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia to a conference table at which he would use his influence to persuade the King to accept a Jewish entity in the Arab midst.

Electoral defeat for Churchill in 1945 meant that the Weizmann-Ibn Saud meeting never took place. From its first days in office, the new Labour Government reverted to the immigration restrictions imposed by Parliament in 1939. Ernest Bevin, who had become Foreign Secretary, warned the Jewish refugees from Nazism not to "push to the head of the queue". The Jewish response included an upsurge in terrorism in Palestine. But Churchill told the House of Commons in 1946 that such terrorism was in part the result of the frustrations, bitterness and deep misunderstandings which Britain herself had created.

"We must not be in a hurry," Churchill told the Commons, after the King David Hotel bomb in Jerusalem killed more than 90 Britons, Jews and Arabs, "to turn aside from the large causes we have carried far".

The Labour Government refused to allow 100,000 Jewish survivors of the Holocaust to enter Palestine. Those who managed to reach the shores of Palestine by ship were intercepted, and sent to detention camps in Cyprus. Bevin favoured "an independent unitary state" in Palestine, with an Arab majority, and special rights for the Jewish minority.

Bevin went on to explain that although he did not accept Arab demands to halt Jewish immigration altogether, "steps must be taken to prevent a real flooding of the country by Jewish immigrants".

But in 1947 Britain had had enough of the Arab-Jewish conflict, and handed it over to the United Nations. That November the UN voted for

the establishment of two states, one Jewish and one Arab, with Jerusalem to be excluded from both, and to be placed under United Nations control.

As a result of the United Nations vote a Jewish state was established in May 1948. It was at once attacked by several armies from the neighbouring Arab states, including Egypt, which published a postage stamp showing the triumphant march of the Egyptian army into Gaza. In 1956 Britain and Israel were partners, together with France, in attacking Egypt, following Nasser's nationalisation of the Suez canal, and his closing of the canal for merchant ships bound for Israel.

During the Arab-Israel war of 1967, when Israel was attacked by Egypt, Jordan and Syria, there was considerable sympathy in Britain for Israel's plight. Israel was seen as a David, attacked by Goliath.

Such sympathy did not always influence high policy. When, during the Arab-Israeli war of 1973, Edward Heath's Government refused to allow United States aircraft to refuel at the British sovereign air base at Akrotiri in Cyprus, there was outrage in Israel. This moment of dispute was offset, from the Israeli perspective, two years later, when Britain refused to join in the United Nations' condemnation of Zionism as "a form of racism and racial discrimination". The British Government under Harold Wilson declined absolutely to participate in it. Under John Major, Britain voted to reverse that United Nations condemnation.

With the Israeli invasion of Lebanon in 1982, and the sustained Israeli air and artillery bombardment of Beirut, it seemed to many British observers that the Israeli David had turned into a Goliath. The Israeli response to the intifada - the Palestinian uprising that broke out on the West Bank and Gaza in 1987 - seemed to give credence to this criticism, which was later reflected in Margaret Thatcher's memoirs. Many British Jews felt that Israel's predicament was not being as well understood as it had been in the past. This lack of understanding seemed to have been underlined when Margaret Thatcher condemned Israel's bombing of the Iraqi nuclear reactor.

But when the peace process was under way, Britain and Israel found themselves in closer harmony. Even during the first search for a settlement between Israel and Egypt, which culminated in the Camp David agreements of 1979, Britain played an active and constructive part. One of the crucial meetings during the two-year process was held at Leeds Castle in Kent.

From 1992 to 1995 the Rabin Government found Britain sympathetic and constructive with regard to the push for Palestinian autonomy. Part of that Israeli policy was the need to give the Palestinian Authority the financial ability to stand on its own feet. When Itzhak Rabin explained this to John Major, the British Prime Minister not only understood it but also responded by a British contribution to the Palestinians that today stands, together with that of the European Union, at almost £2 billion. One of Britain's first contributions was to allocate funds for a hospital in Gaza, and to provide vehicles for the Palestinian police.

As Israel reaches its 50th anniversary of statehood, Britain, which was one of the midwives to the Jewish national home, takes seriously its responsibilities to both sides in the conflict. While some Israelis resented Robin Cook's recent intervention at the disputed Har Homa building site in East Jerusalem, other Israelis stood by with banners supporting the British Foreign Secretary.

Among those who voiced their support for Cook was Yael Dayan, the daughter of the Israeli military leader Moshe Dayan. As a young soldier fighting with the British against Vichy France in the Second World War, General Dayan had lost an eye.

Moshe Dayan's participation in the British war effort, and his daughter's support 57 years later for a British Foreign Secretary, point up the British link in the positive way that it can surely continue to grow, and must grow, if Britain is to remain a central player in the resolution of the Arab-Israel conflict.

Sir Martin Gilbert is an Honorary Fellow of Merton College, Oxford. His new book Israel: a History is published by Doubleday, at £25.
