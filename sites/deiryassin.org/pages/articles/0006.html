EXPELLING THE ARABS

Nesweek November 5, 1979 Page 68

For years, it has been almost an article of faith among Israelis that the Palestinian Arabs who abandoned their homes in 1948 did so because they were panic-stricken, or because they were obeying cynical orders from Israel's Arab enemies. But in an account that Israeli censors deleted from his newly published memoirs, former Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin provides his own footnote to history. Rabin, a 26-year-old brigade commander during Israel's 1948 war of independence, claims that Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion ordered him to expel 50,000 Arab civilians forcibly from two towns near Tel Aviv.

The expulsion story appears in an uncensored version of the memoirs made available to The New York Times by Peretz Kidron, who helped Rabin translate his book from Hebrew into English. In it, Rabin describes a strategy meeting with Ben-Gurion and his own commanding officer, Yigal Allon (who years later served as Rabin's Foreign Minister). The problem: what to do about the Arab populations of Ramla and Lydda (now called Lod), located along the strategic road from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem. "We walked outside, Ben-Gurion accompanying us," says Rabin's account. "Allon repeated his question: 'What is to be done with the population?' B. G. [Ben-Gurion] waved his hand in a gesture which said, 'Drive them out!'"

The population of Lod, Rabin recalls, "did not leave willingly. There was no way of avoiding the use of force and warning shots ... The inhabitants of Ramla watched and learned the lesson. Their leaders agreed to be evacuated voluntarily, on condition that the evacuation was carried out by vehicles."

Allon disputed Rabin's story last week, insisting that the Arabs fled out of panic and because they were instructed to do so by Transjordan's Arab Legion. "With all my high esteem for Rabin during the war of independence," Allon said, "I was his commander and my knowledge of the facts is therefore more accurate."

BIOGRAPHY: Rabin declined to reply but his expurgated version of the episode agreed with what has been written in other histories, including a biography of Ben-Gurion, in which a comparable passage was left uncensored. Rabin's manuscript originally was cleared for publication by Israeli military screeners. Then a second panel of censors, consisting of five Cabinet ministers, ordered the passage cut from his book, which has just been published in the U.S. under the title, "The Rabin Memoirs" (344 pages. Little, Brown. $12. 95).

The censored excerpt makes it clear that the incident at Lod and Ramla was burned into Rabin's memory. He writes that many of his idealistic young soldiers disagreed with the order to drive the Arabs out. "There were some fellows who refused to take part in the expulsion action," he relates. "Prolonged propaganda activities were required after the action, to remove the bitterness of these [soldiers] and explain why we were obliged to undertake such a harsh and cruel action."
