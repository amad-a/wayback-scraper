Ghost village where sanity is locked out

Israel at 50: Julian Borger in Givat Shaul, formerly Deir Yassin
Thursday April 9, 1998

In the zero-sum struggle of Middle East politics, 50 years of Israeli survival and achievement have been 50 years of Palestinian disaster. So this month, as Israel celebrates, the Palestinians will be mourning the nakba, or catastrophe.

Their grief has many focal points - more than 400 villages literally erased from the map after the 1948 war - but none so emotive as Deir Yassin. Fifty years ago today, the small hilltop village on the western edge of Jerusalem was the scene of a massacre by Jewish paramilitaries which shocked not only Arabs but also Jews who had thought their own persecuted, brutalised race incapable of such atrocities.

For all the sound and fury associated with its name, the old village of Deir Yassin is eerily quiet today. Most of the original Arab stone houses are still standing, but shaded now by 50-year-old pines and surrounded by wire fencing. The entrance is barred by a heavy metal gate, security guards and a signpost reading: Kfar Shaul Mental Health Centre.

Nowhere in modern Israel is there an old Arab village so perfectly preserved, and so lifeless. This is where the psychologically unbalanced are brought to recover, away from the tensions of modern Israel in the tranquillity of a dead village. Pilgrims suffering from "Jerusalem syndrome" (believing they are Jesus or another central biblical character) come here for the slow descent to normality.

Outside is the burgeoning Jerusalem suburb of Givat Shaul. Inside the security fence, a few inmates wander erratically among the old cottages along paths which - 50 years and a day ago - bustled with anxious Arab villagers.

On that day, a 20-year-old called Mahmoud Assad Yassini was standing guard at the highest point of Deir Yassin, from where he could see the battle of Kastel across the valley. On that day the Arab commander, Abdel Kadr Husseini, would be killed and the hopes of home-grown resistance begin to crumble.

Tension in Deir Yassin rose. Abu Mahmoud (as Mr Yassini is now known) stood guard all night with about 80 other village men. Shortly before dawn the attack came.

"I heard a bren-gun and then flares went up. It was like day," he recalled. He is within a few yards of his family house but it is out of reach on the wrong side of the wire fence.

"The fight went on all day. In the afternoon we ran out of ammunition and the fighters were forced to flee."

That is when, by most accounts, the real killing began, carried out by Jewish irregulars from the Irgun organisation and another hardline group, Lehi, known to the British as the Stern Gang.

At the moment Abu Mahmoud fled, his cousin Zeinab Akkel was being rounded up with the other women of the village. She had just seen her 14-year-old brother shot.

"My husband had given me 250," she said. "I offered it to a Jewish soldier and said, 'Please leave my brother alone, he is so young'. He took the money, but then he just knocked my brother over and shot him in the head with five bullets."

Mrs Akkel, now aged 70, said she saw Arab villagers being driven off in a truck to one of Deir Yassin's quarries. She said the truck returned empty.

Members of Irgun and the Stern Gang have always denied killing villagers in cold blood. Yehuda Lapidot, Irgun's deputy-commander that day, said: "We were outside, open to fire. To stop the shooting from the buildings, we had to fire into the buildings, but inside there were also women and children. That's how so many Arabs got killed."

That account differs dramatically not only from the reports of Arab survivors but also from the story told by Meir Pail, who took part in the attack as an observer for the mainstream Jewish forces, the Haganah.

After the battle was won by Haganah's intervention, Mr Pail says, "the people from Stern and Irgun went rushing from house to house shooting, slaughtering, killing and looting, with quite a lot of disorder.

"Arab men were taken to an old quarry between Givat Shaul and Deir Yassin. They lined them up with their backs to the wall of the quarry and shot them all. I had a soldier with me who took pictures of it."

Those pictures and Mr Pail's report were delivered the next day to the Haganah command, but they have never been published.

With the truth suppressed, Deir Yassin became a propaganda weapon almost immediately. Abu Mahmoud, who lost 38 people from his extended family at Deir Yassin, says he was approached the next day by Arab officials who told him to claim that there had been rapes and butchery with knives. "They said I should change my story, but I refused," he said.

Meanwhile, Irgun and the Stern Gang reportedly circulated exaggerated rumours to spread terror in other Arab villages. Arab broadcasters, embroidering the story in an attempt to stir sympathy for the plight of Palestine, unwittingly encouraged the Arab exodus.

The original reports said 250 died in Deir Yassin; Mr Pail thinks the figure nearer 200. Recent research suggests a toll of about 120. Abu Mahmoud swears only 93 villagers died.

It was followed by an Arab reprisal ambush on a convoy of Jews in which 78 were killed.

Half a century of wars and civil strife later, the name Deir Yassin still has a resonance which seems to echo from the heart of the endless Arab-Israeli feud.

For Mr Pail, as for many liberal Israelis, it has remained a weevil gnawing at the moral foundations of the Jewish state, even now on its 50th birthday.

"Look at it like this," he said. "You have a barrel of honey, and a barrel of shit. If you take a teaspoon of honey and put it in the shit, it won't change it. But if you take a spoonful of shit and put it in the honey, the whole barrel is spoilt... We have suffered for this until now."
