Right to Resist
 
August Saturday 19 -2000:

Israel will be made to pay, in court (1)

When the Lebanese Resistance was first launched in the aftermath of the 1982 invasion, its declared target of ousting Israel from the South was greeted with skepticism, even ridicule. Eighteen years later, Lebanon is celebrating victory over the formidable enemy.
Today, an enthusiastic lawyer is launching what he calls Legal Resistance, convinced that Lebanon could ultimately bring Israel to pay billions of dollars in war reparations.
Were seeking equity and justice. This is a national cause, Michel Tueni said in a recent interview at his office in Achrafieh. We need the Lebanese to support and believe in this legal resistance.
He explained how at first, the Lebanese were skeptical of armed resistance. But when the fighters began dealing painful blows to the Israelis, the government offered tacit support.
Under President Emile Lahoud, there was open support for the resistance, and what happened? The Israelis were ousted, he said confidently.
He expects his campaign to evolve in the minds of citizens in the same way.
Tueni cited Iraq as an example of how the international community could pressure an aggressor to pay war reparations to the victim. Some 30 percent of Iraqs restricted oil sales are channeled to a fund in Geneva which is entrusted with paying compensation to victims of Saddam Husseins 1990 invasion of Kuwait.
But why has Israel escaped such international pressure? The answer for Tueni is simple. No one has ever petitioned them before.
He says it was the Jews who first introduced the principle of compensation, when after World War II, Germany was made to pay for the atrocities committed by the Nazi regime.
Tueni was encouraged by a series of rulings by the US District Court in Washington in anti-terrorism cases. The verdicts demanded that Middle East states pay compensations of about $250 million for each kidnap or murder victim.
If the US says a human being is worth some $250 million, its our right to immediately demand almost $30 billion for Qana victims alone, Tueni said, referring to the 1996 attack on the Fijian UNIFIL headquarters in the village, in which 107 civilians perished.
No one can claim Lebanon was not attacked by Israel and was not devastated over more than 20 years of systematic aggression. Now Israel has withdrawn. Are we supposed to say, let bygones be bygones?
The extent of devastation that became apparent after the Israeli withdrawal in May, coupled with the economic recession ­ also partly due to 22 years of occupation ­ persuaded Tueni that he had to accelerate his drive to put together a case against Israel.
But before approaching the government with an abstract idea, Tueni enlisted the help of international legal experts to determine the viability of such a complaint, and the international forum best suited to handle the case. After consultations with British experts, including the renowned Ian Brownley, it became apparent that there was solid claim for compensation.
The choice was either the UN Security Council or the International Court of Justice. But we all know what happens at the Security Council, he said, referring to the possibility of the US vetoing any decision in Lebanons favor.
Besides, the ICJ is the best arbiter in inter-state conflicts.
Tueni sent his findings to various government bodies, and found that former Justice Minister Bahij Tabbara had launched a similar campaign in 1996.
He received the support and encouragement of Prime Minister Salim Hoss, who urged him to prepare the necessary files.
At this point, any suggestions concerning an appropriate figure for compensation would be guesswork, which Tueni categorically refused to discuss.
The legal documents are almost ready, Tueni said.
But hard work of completing the economic file lies ahead, which will include case-by-case evidence to support claims.
Completing the economic file is a two-fold process, involving an assessment of direct and indirect losses, Tueni said.
The former would include human and material losses that are being assessed from the testimonies of citizens, media reports and security agency files. The latter would include the impact of Israels aggression on Lebanons economy. This, he said, must be evaluated by international experts.
Lebanon was put in the freezer for more than 20 years because of Israeli attacks. Someone has to pay for its revival, said Tueni.
The technical tools for this case are there. But there has to be a political decision.
