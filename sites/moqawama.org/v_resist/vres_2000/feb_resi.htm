The shifting Lebanese military equation(1)

Ze'ev Schiff
We were barely into the third millennium when Chief of Staff Shaul Mofaz held a big news conference to sum up the "Israel" Defense Forces'* previous year of operations in Lebanon. In relative terms, 1999 was a "good year." Fatalities were down to 13, compared to more than 20 in 1998. However, in Lebanese operations the bouquets wither quickly. What began as a celebratory news conference soon became a classic example of "don't count your chickens before they're hatched."
At the time, the top brass felt that the IDF had finally achieved the upper hand in the war against Hizbullah and was finding more effective solutions for that organization's assaults. Senior commanders were happy that IDF operational intelligence had improved, thanks to a major injection of funds. They felt this was triggering quicker IDF responses, especially by the Air Force which was able to upgrade the precision of its night bombing runs. In 1999 the IDF's "smart weapon" exacted a heavier price from Hizbullah.
Scarcely mentioned, however, was the fact that IDF ground forces had been ordered to change their modus operandi. Chief of Staff Mofaz had told the IDF to "downscale its signature" - that is, minimize activities in the field and outside IDF outposts. This made sense following the declared intention to withdraw from Lebanon by July 1, 2000.
Previous operational strategy had called for the IDF to penetrate deep into Lebanese territory - north of the security zone border - and to ambush Hizbullah guerrillas in the field.
The new approach aims to restrict operations outside the outposts so as to reduce pressure on the ground forces. The practical implications of this reorientation include a reduction in IDF targets, including the number of outposts, to an absolute minimum. The South Lebanon Army** was given responsibility for logistical convoys in the field. All of these decisions formed a background for reduced IDF casualties.
There was a problem, however. Hizbullah quickly managed, by very simple means, to turn the tables. Since IDF troops had stopped going out into the field to confront its fighters, Hizbullah began to edge closer and closer to IDF outposts and to lay landmines with recently upgraded destructive capacity. This is precisely what Hizbullah did - almost on the very doorstep of the SLA's deputy commander, Aql Hachem. By using simple remote-control tactics, Hizbullah began to organize ambushes based on mortars and missiles and which targeted IDF forces rushing to the aid of troops hit by landmine.
Since the IDF was now confining itself to outposts to downscale its signature, Hizbullah began to target the firing slits in the outposts' sentry positions, and fired anti-tank rockets through those slits. For this tactic, you do not need a large army. All you need is one or two cells of fighters specialized in precision firing missiles capable of inflicting repeated damage. In this period, Hizbullah received second-generation missiles, more advanced and with night-vision sights.
On many occasions Hizbullah has violated the Grapes of Wrath understandings through its operations based in Lebanese villages. However, none of the activities in which six IDF soldiers were killed by missiles and mortars involved any Hizbullah violation of these understandings.
From "Israel's" standpoint, what counts is the final outcome. Despite all the IDF's sophisticated equipment, six of our soldiers died and the missile-shooters were not killed. In fact, they were not even prevented from getting close to the outposts. These operations are Hizbullah's most recent success - and perhaps the IDF's most recent failure - over the last few weeks. In its frustration the IDF retaliated against Lebanon's infrastructure, and this time the retaliatory strikes were carried out after military casualties, not in the wake of Katyusha rockets fired at the Galilee. Thus, the IDF has lowered its tolerance level. Barak wants more aggressive game rules that will allow the IDF to respond to any attack on "Israel" after the withdrawal - with or without a Syrian-"Israeli" agreement - and after IDF troops have been deployed along the international frontier with Lebanon.
Nevertheless, it should be pointed out that the defense establishment so far has issued no tenders for constructing new outposts on that frontier".

Katyushas have a course of their own(3)
 "We parked the Land Rover in the upper-class Christian Ashrafiya neighborhood and wondered in our hearts about how they would receive us. Suddenly an old man came out of one of the stores and shouted to us, 'Hizbullah, Hizbullah.' We were sure that we would immediately be told to leave. Perhaps it characterizes the great change that has swept across the Lebanese population and affected Hizbullah's status.
The incident dates from the days of Operation Grapes of Wrath, in April 1996, before the landing of the shell that killed approximately 100 people seeking shelter in a Unifil camp in Kafr Kana. Beirut was flooded with some 500,000 Lebanese refugees who were fleeing "Israeli" attacks; southern villages were evacuated, as was the city of Tyre, for the first time.
The "Israeli" strategy, which had already been used in Operation Accountability, relied on the outdated view that the expulsion of villagers to the capital city would put pressure on the Lebanese government, which would in turn pressure Syria to calm Hizbullah. The IDF* assured the public that this time it intended to get rid of the threat of katyushas once and for all. The "Israeli" government at the time had a grander ambition, to use the military operation to obtain better understandings than those obtained after Operation Accountability in 1993, but primarily, to use it as political lever that would enable it to withdraw from Lebanon.
Messages the "Israelis" sent via the Americans to Lebanon and Syria specifically stated that if after the operation, quiet prevails for nine months, it will then be possible to discuss an IDF withdrawal from Lebanon.
However, things developed differently. This brutal operation, with 650 sorties and around 25,000 mortar shells fired during its first week, created a Lebanon different from what the prime minister's advisers recognized. Hizbullah's information department and press office worked around the clock, operated radio and television stations and broadcast live the horrors of the war. At the same time, the Lebanese newspapers published announcements calling on citizens to contribute to and volunteer for the organization. "You can contribute toward the purchase of a bullet, Katyusha or missile. You can volunteer for us or purchase uniforms for fighters, you can donate money to the Hizbullah fund or funds supporting it," the ads and radio announcements said, making it sound like the Shirutrom telethon to raise funds for the "Israeli" army. One Lebanese woman donated tens of thousands of dollars, "but only for the purchase of katyushas that should be fired in my name against "Israel"." Another citizen wanted to donate $25,000 to buy katyushas, but imposed conditions that Hizbullah could not accept. "He wanted us to fire the katyushas every day in volleys of five," a Hizbullah commander told the Lebanese journalist. "We couldn't accept this condition because according to our rules, the enemy can't know how many rockets will be fired at him and what the scale of an attack will be."
But thanks to Operation Grapes of Wrath, Hizbullah was able to capture more than just the hearts of the Lebanese public in all its variety. The political echelon, and especially the prime minister at the time, Rafik Hariri, who prior to the operation referred to the organization as "a problem" and explained that Hizbullah might become a burden, changed their colors and adopted Hizbullah as a legitimate national resistance organization. Syria's policy also changed. In general Syria is, and was, in the habit of telling Hizbullah what it could not do, but did not get involved in the details of the organization's operations or activities, beyond the red lines it had established.
Around the time of the operation, Shimon Peres announced his decision to move up the elections from September to May, and Damascus saw that as an "Israeli" attempt to ruin the negotiations with it, because moving up elections, after all, means there is no negotiation partner until the election results are known.
Hizbullah's attacks in March 1996 could therefore, from Syria's perspective, serve perhaps as a necessary catalyst for negotiations with "Israel" because Syria then faced a complicated situation. Jordan and Egypt had already signed peace agreements with "Israel"; Turkey, two months prior to the operation, had signed with "Israel" what was to Syria a threatening military cooperation agreement, the negotiations with the Palestinians, although frozen due to the series of terrorist attacks, had not been canceled and representatives of the Gulf states were at the time in the midst of a modest flirtation with "Israel".
Syria was isolated and the strength of Operation Grapes of Wrath made it suspect that Lebanon might collapse under the pressure and undermine Syria's control there. Assad could not know that by the end of that week, he would be the real winner.
From "Israel's" perspective, the operation was not only an attempt to realize its hope for a new military order in Lebanon, but also a political necessity in order to restore the popularity of Shimon Peres, which had dissipated due to Hamas attacks. The "Israeli" public demanded a response to both the terrorist bombings and Hizbullah attacks. On March 4, 1996, Hizbullah killed five soldiers in the security zone. "Israel" did not respond because at the time, the Sharm el-Sheikh anti-terrorism conference was in progress. On March 20, Hizbullah killed another "Israeli" soldier. On March 30, Hizbullah fired katyushas on Kiryat Shmona after "Israel" attacked a Shi'ite village. After a Lebanese boy was killed in Baraasheit by an "Israeli" mine, Hizbullah fired katyushas at Kiryat Shmona, injuring six residents. The government could no longer withstand the public's calls and on April 11, it launched the operation that finally ended the understandings reached after Operation Accountability.
"Israel" was certain it was going to obtain a new set of understandings. U.S. President Bill Clinton, who was visiting Tokyo and Moscow at the time, urgently dispatched Secretary of State Warren Christopher and Dennis Ross. Peres announced that it was "still too early to conduct negotiations." After a few days, the U.S. relayed to Syria a message with "Israel's" conditions for negotiations. These included the disarming of Hizbullah, no attacks against IDF forces in the security zone, Syrian guarantees of quiet in the area, and if it lasted nine months, "Israel" would discuss a comprehensive withdrawal from Lebanon.
Syria rejected the conditions outright and was especially angry at Washington for not including UN Resolution 425, which called for an unconditional "Israeli" withdrawal from Lebanon.
At the same time, Syria and Lebanon received encouragement from France. Jacques Chirac, the first head of state to visit Lebanon since the end of the civil war, compared Hizbullah to the World War II French Resistance. His foreign minister, Herver? de Charrette, tried his hand at independent mediation and failed. "Israel" was in the midst of the euphoria of political and military victory and awaited a Lebanese and Syrian capitulation.
All along, Hizbullah did not stop firing katyushas at "Israel". Then on April 18, one "Israeli" artillery shell altered the military and political front. The Kana shell and some 100 casualties quickly became part of the Lebanese ethos. If Muslims in general and Arabs in particular coordinate the way they should, "Israel" wouldnt have dared to attack Lebanon and its foreign minister wouldnt have threatened to burn Lebanon and kill its children, the speaker said.
The "Israelis" know that their days are numbered in southern Lebanon. Thats why theyre more dangerous. The Arab leaders must awake after their people awoke, he added, referring to popular protests throughout the Arab World.
Kharrazi responded by commending the Lebanese resistance movement, whose activities honor the Muslim world as much as they honor Lebanon.
Lebanese officials, resistance leaders and the Lebanese people in general handled recent developments with utmost wisdom, he added. Commenting on French Prime Minister Lionel Jospins tagging of Hizbullah as terrorist, Kharrazi said: If the French official was aware of the positive aspects of the resistance movement, he wouldnt have made such shameful comments.
Regarding Palestinian students stoning Jospin in the West Bank on Saturday, the foreign minister said: The students told him their way that the only means to face the "Israelis" is resistance.
Upon his arrival at Tehrans airport, Berri said that despite the positive show of Arab support to Lebanon, the problem is that Arab and Islamic parliamentarians only meet as a reaction to an aggression.
Had there been coordination from the beginning, I can assure you that neither "Israel" nor any other country could attack an Arab or Islamic country, Berri said.
The speaker also expressed fear that the meeting of Arab foreign ministers would end with merely decisions expressing support. I speak on behalf of the Lebanese people: Lebanon does not need support because it has UN Security Council Resolution 425. We need words that can be turned into actions.
Asked if the strong Arab reaction in support of Lebanon indicated a new Arab position, Berri said that unfortunately, the official reaction followed that of the people.
Berri put a positive spin on Arab support following his attendance at the Union of Arab Parliamentarians conference in Algeria last week.
There was progress in terms of implementing Arab commitments toward Lebanon and not only voicing support, he said.
Berri also commented on "Israel's" latest power-station bombings, saying that the Jewish state intended to destroy the countrys infrastructure so that Lebanon would be crippled following a peace treaty.
This way Lebanon would not be able to compete with "Israel" in the region. "Israel" knows that Lebanon is capable of competing with it more than any other Arab country, he said.
Asked if Hizbullah would be allowed to remain in the south if "Israel" withdrew, Berri said: Hizbullah is part of Lebanon just like other political parties in the country. The resistance that is taking place is the peoples resistance.
