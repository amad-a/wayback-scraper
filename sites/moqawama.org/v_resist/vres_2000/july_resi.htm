In Long Fight With Israel, Hizbullah Tactics Evolved(1)

 Hizbullah, the guerrilla organization that forced Israel from the slice of southern Lebanon it had occupied for 22 years, grew from a small band of amateurish gunmen to a highly sophisticated tactical operation savvy enough to exploit a new weapon of modern warfare -- television.

"The use of media as a weapon had an effect parallel to a battle," the top Hizbullah commander in southern Lebanon, Sheik Nabil Qaouk, said in a rare interview with a Western journalist. He discussed in unusual detail how Hizbullah learned to improve its effectiveness, strategy and weaponry, and how to videotape its successes for distribution to the media.

"By the use of these films, we were able to control from a long distance the morale of a lot of Israelis," he said, underscoring how demoralization as much as defeat helped to drive the Israelis from southern Lebanon.

From once relying on teenage suicide bombers to crash cars into Israeli installations in the mid-1980's, Hizbullah tactics -- primarily ambushes, assassinations and roadside bombs -- became increasingly well planned and executed, military observers in the region say.

A turning point came three years ago this month with the ambush of a raid by elite Israeli naval commandos in which a dozen were killed.

The Israelis halted such raids for more than a year, trying to find out what went wrong, and then Hizbullah put out word that it had penetrated Israeli intelligence, the sheik and Western military observers said, further halting Israeli counterinsurgency operations.

The effect was to drive the Israelis into bunkers, conceding land and initiative to Hizbullah.

And the impact of their strikes was amplified by the fact that they were videotaped by hidden Hizbullah cameramen and that the tape quickly found its way onto Israeli television, sometimes even as the Israeli Army spokesman denied the incident had taken place.

The effect was what the military calls a "force multiplier," greatly undermining Israeli will.

"Seventy-five percent of Hizbullah's war was the videotapes," said an experienced official with the United Nations peacekeeping troops in the area.

The videotapes were rushed to Beirut, where copies were delivered to Lebanese television and Western news agencies like The Associated Press and Reuters, which made them widely available. They were, however, first shown on Hizbullah's own television station.

The videos were often shown on the nightly television news in Israel, helping bring home the cost of the occupation. They were a stark contrast to the standard fare served up by Israeli military reporters, whose coverage was severely restricted and censored by the army. Blurred and grainy as they sometimes were, the Hizbullah videos contributed to the rising public pressure in Israel to bring the troops home.

But the tape would not have been taken at all without the improved intelligence gathering and combat skills Hizbullah developed over the years.

"When they first started, they thought they could do it with a bunch of people on a hill yelling 'Allah-u akbar,' " a United Nations official in the area said of the Hizbullah fighters. "They would lose 40 in an operation. Now they are very sophisticated, very disciplined."

In the interview, Sheik Qauok said the guerrillas had been able to improve their effectiveness by studying each operation, learning from their mistakes and developing new uses for weaponry. He also outlined their reliance on sometimes stunningly low-tech methods to thwart Israels more numerous and more sophisticated forces.

"The resistance always focused on the weak points of the enemy," he said as he outlined a classic theory and practice of guerrilla warfare.

Military experts say that at any one time Hizbullah maintained only about 500 full-time fighters, although it could mobilize many more supporters. The Israeli troops in southern Lebanon usually numbered about 1,500, backed up by 2,500 more troops in their proxy Lebanese militia, which collapsed and fled when the Israelis abruptly pulled out of southern Lebanon on May 24.

Hizbullah officials say they lost 1,200 fighters over the years, and yellow roadside markers are being put up at the spots where they fell. Hundreds of Israeli soldiers were also killed.

There were two kinds of wars, Sheik Qaouk said, guerrilla war and conventional war. By alternating tactics, he went on, Hizbullah was able to maintain the initiative and, after a clash, melt away into a largely sympathetic populace.

"By limiting the firing, we were able to keep the cards in our hands," the sheik said. "We were able to do small, little battles where we had the advantage.

"Even the rockets the resistance used had to be used intelligently.

We would do an action only in the time and place where it had an impact."

The sheik and other military observers cited a number of Hizbullah innovations. These ranged from roadside bombs made of fake plastic rocks, which could be bought in Beirut garden stores for $15, to running farm animals across areas monitored by Israeli motion sensors. At the same time, Hizbullah had the ability to jam Israeli radar and closed-circuit television monitors.

Since it was never driven and did not show up on Israeli heat sensors, an old Soviet T-55 tank hidden in a cave and fired sporadically took the Israelis months to find, the sheik said. Hizbullah fighters discovered that wire-guided antitank rockets, which can, in effect, be steered in flight, could be directed into small openings in concrete bunkers.

"Even the man who invented these rockets did not know this," Sheik Qaouk bragged. The sheik smilingly refused to say if they had American-made TOW missiles, more accurate than the old Saggars they had been using.

Above all, the sheik said, he and his fighters "were always ready for martyrdom," but the Israeli soldier "probably wasn't ready to die for this."

"More important than everything," he said, "is that the Israeli soldier didn't believe in the war."

He also stressed the importance of a good intelligence network. The effectiveness of that network was demonstrated with the assassination on Feb. 28 last year of Brig. Gen. Eretz Gerstien, the top Israeli Army liaison officer to the proxy militia by a roadside bomb as his car passed in a military convoy. He was the highest-ranking Israeli officer killed during the occupation.

Similarly, last Jan. 30, the militia's second-ranking commander, Col. Aql Hashem, was killed by a bomb at his house in Marjeyoun.

July Saturday 29 -2000:


Resistance readies itself for new role
Hizbullah looks after the stability of the area without being the security authority ­ Qaouk(1)

With the imminent deployment of UNIFIL peacekeepers into the former occupation zone, the fighters of the Islamic Resistance will abandon their makeshift observation posts along the border and disperse among the local population of the South.
Having fought an open war with Israeli forces for almost two decades, Hizbullahs military wing is set to play a less visible role in the South in the coming months as UNIFIL peacekeepers and Lebanese Army troops assume control of security.
However, this does not entail the demise of the Islamic Resistance. Although Israels occupation is over, a number of outstanding issues ­ such as the release of Lebanese detainees and the return of Palestinian refugees ­ as well as the lack of a comprehensive peace in the region will ensure that the resistance remains on standby.
Israel is a well-armed terrorist state, Sheikh Nabil Qaouk, Hizbullahs southern commander, told The Daily Star. Its Lebanons right to hold onto all its sources of strength. The resistance is one of the main sources of strength ­ a strategic weapon for Lebanon, Syria and the Arabs.
Following the cessation of fighting in May, squads of Hizbullah fighters deployed at former crossing points along the frontier. With each squad consisting of about four or five fighters armed with assault rifles, there are perhaps 100 guerrillas ranged along the border. They are reportedly drawn from the 300-500 experienced and older fighters who form the backbone of the Islamic Resistance.
Although there have been no serious confrontations between the Islamic Resistance and UNIFIL along the border, on occasions the peacekeepers have been temporarily denied access to roads along the frontier by groups of fighters. However, the deployment of UNIFIL troops into the liberated areas will bring to an end the armed presence of Hizbullah border squads, according to a Lebanese military source.
To leave the border free of security is illogical. Hizbullah still has some observation posts and patrols along the border to let the Israelis know they are still there. The military presence of Hizbullah will vanish, however, in the near future from the UNIFIL area, the source said.
Then, he said, the Islamic Resistance would adopt a covert rather than overt presence along the border. Security is not just a matter of a soldier carrying a gun. Hizbullah will continue to have eyes in the area, along the border and in the villages, watching and seeing everything that goes on.
The fighters will confine their activities to intelligence gathering and monitoring developments along the border. Domestic security affairs in the villages and towns of the South will be left to the army and Internal Security Forces.
Qaouk explained that the Islamic Resistance was not a substitute for the states security apparatus. Hizbullah looks after the stability of the area without being the security authority. This is the governments role, he said. There is no conflict or contradiction between Hizbullah and the security authority. We have no problem in understanding each others role.
Richard Norton, professor of anthropology and international relations at Boston University, who is also probably the Wests leading expert on Hizbullah, argued that the discipline of Islamic Resistance fighters made their deployment a necessary interim measure.
For months, Hizbullah leaders have been concerned to avoid a paroxysm of personal vengeance episodes in the South, he said. From the onset, there was no interest within Hizbullah in substituting for the army. But without the Hizbullah deployment there would be a vacuum in the south. The ISF, police and mukhabarat presence is insufficient.
Unlike a regular army, the Islamic Resistance is a fluid, versatile guerrilla force living in residential areas with no permanent military positions, barracks or heavy equipment. Consequently, while the fighters may merge into the background during the present cease-fire, the nature of the resistance allows for a swift mobilization if the need arises.
Qaouk would not be drawn on Hizbullahs response if Israel were to launch an assault  against Lebanon. We are not concerned with addressing specific scenarios, he said. We believe only that Hizbullah was, is and will continue to be the arms to defend Lebanon.
After the incident at Markaba, Hizbullah reasserted its role in support of the victory, stability and internal unity, Qaouk said. We realize very well that Israel is trying to stain the image of our victory.
The core members of the Islamic Resistance have evolved over the past decade to be among the most proficient guerrilla fighters in the world, adept at the use of small arms, artillery, explosives, intelligence gathering, propaganda and communications.
Dismantling the resistance is seen by some as a waste of talent and resources. This has led to speculation that the group could become affiliated to the Lebanese Army, possibly as a paramilitary force operating along the border with Israel.
Qaouk was dismissive, however, arguing that the Islamic Resistance and the army already cooperated. Further links were unnecessary, he said.
The military source also cast doubts on such a move. Its just not feasible. The bulk of Hizbullahs fighters are educated people: doctors, engineers, university graduates who will have no need to continue a military existence, he said.
Norton also believed the fighters would have little difficulty settling back into a civilian existence. The mainline resistance members are drawn from the fabric of Lebanese society. These are men with family ties, homes, networks of social support and hopes and aspirations for Lebanon, he said. There is no serious problem looming in terms of reintegrating the Hizbullah fighters into Lebanese society, because they are already integrated.
