Time to call a spade a

 spade


    "The student demonstration at the CNN office in Beirut last week was not particularly aimed against CNN as an American news organization as much as it was an expression of frustration with the lack of accuracy in Western news coverage of Lebanese-"Israeli" hostilities in general.
The demonstrators were there to protest against the failure ­ even refusal ­ by Western news organizations to call a spade a spade.
Imagine the impact on world public opinion if the Lebanese resistance movement were called just that instead of the Iranian-backed Shiite Hizbullah as the Western media prefer to identify the group that is leading the liberation war against "Israeli" occupation of parts of Lebanon.
Professional journalists have an obligation to tell their audiences the facts. In this way, the truth can take care of itself.
That Hizbullah is a Lebanese resistance movement is an undeniable fact.
That it has been fighting an occupying power for more than 15 years is also a fact.
That the fighters are all Lebanese and not Iranians is a fact.
That it is fighting the "Israelis" on Lebanese ­ not "Israeli" ­ soil is also a fact.
That it is targeting "Israeli" military targets is another fact.
Doesnt it make sense for any professional journalist to mention these facts in their news coverage to produce a fair and balanced story about what is truly happening in Lebanon?
It is true that Syria and Iran are the main backers of the Lebanese resistance movement. "Israeli" government officials cannot stop accusing Syria and Iran of helping Hizbullah, but every time Hizbullah guerrillas launch a successful operation against "Israeli" targets in the occupation zone, "Israel" retaliates against Lebanon. Is Lebanon therefore a victim of mistaken identity?
Prime Minister Salim Hoss has made it clear that it is in Lebanons interest to enable the foreign media to operate freely in this country.
He spoke from experience. The prime minister surely remembers that had it not been for the presence in force of the foreign media in Beirut during the 1982 "Israeli" invasion, very few people abroad would have been able to know about the invasion promptly enough to start condemning it.
The same can be said about the massacres at the Sabra and Chatila refugee camps, which hit the air waves hours after they happened.
Tom Friedman, now a senior commentator at The New York Times, was the Beirut correspondent of that prestigious American newspaper during the "Israeli" invasion of Lebanon.
He nearly lost his job when he battled over the wires with his editor in New York over the use of the words indiscriminate bombing.
Friedman was in Beirut and he saw it happen. He insisted on calling a spade a spade even at the risk of losing his job.

1- The Daily Star, 25-2-2000.
