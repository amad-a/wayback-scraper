In fair territory
 
  
By Zvi Bar'el
  "Lebanon is required to ensure that attacks are carried out in keeping with the conditions of the agreements." That was how the joint oversight committee for implementation of the "Grapes of Wrath" cease-fire agreements summed up its meeting on Tuesday.

The committee did not convene to discuss the attack in which three Golani brigade soldiers werekilled next to the Tel Kab'a outpost, but rather to give its opinion concerning Hezbollah mortar fire and Lebanon's complaint that Israel damaged three civilian houses in the village of 'Arab Salim.

Mortar fire, like the latest deadly attack, falls within the framework of the agreements - what could be called "approved attacks." They took place within the security zone and did not harm civilian settlements. That's the way it is in southern Lebanon: Everything is orderly and organized. It's OK to kill as long as you observe the rules, otherwise you risk a scolding.

It's only when Israeli soldiers or Lebanese civilians are killed that we are reminded of this ceaselessly active battlefront. But daily life in Lebanon is no less exciting. For example, the United Nations Observers' headquarters ordered Nepalese soldiers to accompany farmers in southern Lebanon during the current olive harvest season. This step was intended to prevent the IDF* from shelling
villages and their olive groves. Nevertheless, the IDF attacked what were defined as "Hezbollah positions" every day last week.

Another seven attacks of this kind, in which dozens of mortar shells were fired, had taken place by this past Monday. Curfews and closure have been imposed on some southern Lebanese villages. The IDF expelled several families whose sons are suspected of aiding Hezbollah from the village of Hatzbaya. In other villages, the South Lebanese Army** (SLA) arrests all kinds of suspects and
transfers them to the Al-Khiyam prison.

Since there is no official occupation, there is also no organized legal system to defend the rights of those arrested. Citizens have two basic "rights": to pay SLA soldiers about $50 a month for protection, which translates into a job in Israel, or to support Hezbollah. SLA soldiers
can either desert or enjoy both worlds at once: serve in the army for a monthly wage and protection, while simultaneously passing information to Hezbollah.

Anyone who wishes to become better acquainted with IDF orders in southern Lebanon can read about them in the Hezbollah press. Following the incident in Hatzbaya, for instance, you could read that the IDF was ordered to reduce the number of supply convoys, that it changed its
escort procedures, that it assigned a minimal number of soldiers to each truck and that it issued directives concerning the distances to be maintained on infantry and motorized patrols.

After this week's attack at Tel Kab'a, Hezbollah sources let it be known that their forces knew the outpost well, that they had kept up continuous surveillance of it, and knew the routine and work procedures there. They did not hurry to activate the explosive charge, but rather, they waited for a
closely packed group of soldiers to pass within range, as they knew would eventually happen. It's easy to imagine that they are at this moment observing their next target, recording it with video cameras, gathering information from villagers or SLA soldiers, and deciding who will plant the
explosives.

It's interesting that the Hezbollah press no longer needs to publish its own glorious descriptions of Hezbollah successes. This week, it widely quoted Israeli television reporters, officers, soldiers and Knesset members. That was sufficient for a Hezbollah public relations victory.

No IDF commission of inquiry will be able to alter this reality in Lebanon. The procedure books are already filled to bursting with a profusion of directives. Anyone wanting to know how an incident like this could happen should direct his inquiry to Hezbollah. Its fighters could explain the obvious: every IDF soldier remaining in Lebanon is a target, and whoever is capable of planting explosives 300 meters from the border, 30 meters from an IDF outpost, could also plant one within Israeli territory. But there's no need to go to all that trouble when achievements can be easily had within the bounds of the Lebanese playing field, and when Hezbollah's sole aim is to oust the IDF.
 
Lynn Griver Gedera
Zvi Bar-el's article, "In fair territory" Nov. 20, 1998 lays out the dynamics of what is actually going on in Southern Lebanon. This week's loss of three soldiers is part of the routine this country has become accustomed to.

It is easy for the government and the Army officials to refer to women who take to the streets and demonstrate as a group of hysterical mothers of soldiers. In a very rational state, I read Zvi Bar-el's article along with many others this week-end. I only received further reinforcement that staying in Southern Lebanon continues to be a catastrophic mistake. On a very emotional and personal level, I watched the faces of my children and their friends as they sadly mourn the loss of a friend blown up by the Hezbollah.

Where is the accountability for these terrible losses? How can our soldiers be expected to have motivation after each Hezbollah victory is seen as a terrible mistake that calls for another nvestigation on our side? How often can they mourn for dead friends and go out and very possibly face yet another "mistake" themselves?

Yasser Arafat is now the Prime Minister's partner in peace. Everyone knows what a lengthy, painful process that has involved. Shouldn't we learn from previous experience how to talk to the enemy? Must we perpetuate the horrors of Southern Lebanon so the Army has somewhere to put practice into action?

** It is an "Israeli" nomination we absolutely reject for it contradicts with Lebanon's independence and sovereignty. This army is not but an "Israeli" agent working for realizing "Israel's" goals in Lebanon.
