What security zone?

There have always been two names for the area of south Lebanon currently not under the control of the Lebanese government. The "Israelis" dubbed it the security zone on the dubious ground that it provided a buffer area in which to prevent attacks by the resistance against "Israeli" soil. The Lebanese nomenclature is simpler and more accurate: it is occupied territory. With the latest resistance attack at Markaba ­ less than a minutes sprint from the border ­ the Jewish state should also consider a new name for the land they occupy in defiance of the United Nations Security Council ­ the insecure zone.

Late Wednesday two "Israeli" soldiers were killed by a roadside bomb planted by Hizbullah fighters less than half a kilometer from the international border between Lebanon and "Israel". Ten days ago three other "Israeli" soldiers were killed and four more wounded by a similar attack less than a stones throw away.

The so-called security zone clearly isnt. These were not casualties sustained in long-distance warfare. They were inflicted by a highly trained Hizbullah special forces squad which had penetrated deep into "Israeli"-held territory.

Nor can the ambushes be written off as luck or flashes in the pan. The superior daring and planning of Hizbullah over the past year especially has driven the "Israeli" troops to imprison themselves in concrete bunkers from which they rarely dare to venture.

In October 1997, two "Israeli" soldiers were killed and six wounded ­ again at Markaba and only 50 meters from the border. Three months later another three "Israeli" soldiers of the occupation force lost their lives in a Hizbullah assault on Blat which is less than a kilometer from the border.

The middle of this year saw possibly Hizbullahs most spectacular operation to date when 18 posts along a frontline of 50 kilometers from Sojod to the end of the western sector were subjected to simultaneous attacks from mortars and anti-tank rockets. To complete the humiliation of the occupying forces and their militia allies, the South Lebanon Army, a Hizbullah team stormed the SLA compound at Haddatha, blew up its ramparts and raised their flag while the occupants skulked in a secure underground bunker.

The following month, August, Hizbullahs real-life Rambo walked into the "Israeli" Armys well-fortified position at Sojod, had a fist fight with a member of an elite "Israeli" paratroop unit and escaped to rejoin his comrades.

The "Israelis" have all but abandoned ground operations in the south because they were sustaining an unacceptable level of casualties and now rely on using their expensive airborne weaponry in making slight changes to the contours of the landscape while their soldiers shelter behind tons of concrete, playing cards and telephoning their mothers.

But the question is not so much how much humiliation can the once-proud and mighty "Israeli" Army take so much as how long will it take its political leadership to realize the futility of remaining in our land.

If Hizbullah can penetrate to within 60 meters of the border to plant bombs and remain in eyesight of the position to detonate them, it could, if it wanted, wreak havoc on places like Kiriyat Shemona and Nahariyeh just inside "Israel". It doesnt because, with the rare exception of warning "Israel" not to attack civilian areas in Lebanon, it doesnt need to.

With the "Israeli" Army pinned down in abject fear of being blown up and an increasingly professional fighting force in Hizbullah and its resistance allies, Benjamin Netanyahus options are closing in. Even viewed from the "Israeli" standpoint it is not that the benefits of withdrawal outnumber the advantages of staying in occupation. There are no benefits for the "Israelis" to remain in south Lebanon.

It may be, therefore, that, given the desertion of the religious fanatics who sustained his ruling coalition, Mr. Netanyahu may now find himself being steered toward finding a face-saving formula for implementing Resolution 425 and withdrawing from Lebanon, thus saving himself much heartache and the mothers of any more dead "Israeli" soldiers from heartbreak.

This optimistic scenario has credibility only because the "Israelis" are in a corner. Riven at home, economically stagnant and facing the import of ever more politically unacceptable bodybags, it might be said that the Jewish state now needs peace more than the Arabs want it.

Though international and regional factors are also at play, the most public reason for putting "Israel" on the spot in south Lebanon has been the bravery, superb professionalism and, above all, repeated successes of the resistance fighters. We salute them.
  

Daily Star 28-11-1998
