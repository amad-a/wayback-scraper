Jewish states leaders weigh options in wake of humiliating defeats
 

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his general staff face considerable pressure to reverse the military losses and contain the fighting in the south.

But any decision has to take into account domestic sensitivities to casualties  which severely hampers the Israeli Armys ability to maneuver.

In the past two years, the Israeli Army has employed both offensive and defensive tactics in the zone. Last years offensive tactics involving commando units ambushing guerrillas along infiltration routes and raids north of the zone  forced Hizbullah to resort to long-range mortar bombardments. But it also increased Israeli casualaties. After the failed Insariyeh raid in September 1997 when 12 commandos were killed, War Minister Yitzhak Mordechai went back to the drawing board to look for alternatives

This led to the present holding pattern by the Israeli Army in which hilltop outposts have been fortified and patrols reduced. While this has lowered casualties during routine Hizbullah mortar and machine-gun attacks, it has left the soldiers more vulnerable whenever they do leave their reinforced-concrete bunkers. The soaring casualties over the past 10 days, seven soldiers killed, a third of the annual total, and 11 wounded would appear to prove the point. With fewer Israeli troops patrolling the zone, Hizbullah has more freedom to move and plant lethal roadside bombs.

Short of an immediate unilateral pullout, which does not seem likely, what can Israel do?

The first option, and according to most observers the least likely, is retaliatory air strikes against Lebanons infrastructure as advocated by Israels internal security minister, Avigdor Kahalani. For every soldier killed, there must be darkness in Beirut, he said Friday.

This sentiment is a familiar refrain from Mr. Kahalani, who has been calling for such an option for two years.

Uzi Landau, the head of the Knessets Security Committee, is similarly inclined. If we have the ability to strike, why not now? he asked.

The answer, as Mr. Netanyahu and the general staff are fully aware, is that hitting Lebanons infrastructure would result in large-scale Katyusha attacks across the border toward settlements in the northern Galilee, defeating, for Israel, the point of the occupation in the first place.

The second option is to regain the offensive by boosting troop numbers in the zone, increasing foot and armored patrols, making greater use of warplanes and helicopter gunships and targeting Hizbullah leaders.

Although increasing the military commitment could reduce Hizbullahs effectiveness, it could also lead to the prospect of incurring further Israeli casualties. As for targeting Hizbullahs field commanders in the south, there is little doubt that the Israelis would have already done so if they could find them.

General Mofaz is under extreme pressure. In the first six months of the year, eight soldiers were killed. But since General Mofaz assumed his post in July, 14 soldiers have died to only six Hizbullah fatalities.

The Army is going to try to limit our losses, but we cant guarantee that there will be no casualties on our side, he said.

The inability of Israeli troops to defend themselves against Hizbullah attacks, let alone take the offensive against the guerrillas, has sparked the most serious crisis to hit the Israeli government over its occupation of the south since the April 1996 war.

I think the war is over. Hizbullah has won, declared a senior Western security source. The Israelis have no choice but to shorten their lines and get out of there.

The source added that the atmosphere in the south is very similar to the period preceding the July 1993 and April 1996 wars, when mounting Israeli casualties triggered air and artillery blitzes.

Right now, it could go either way. Some Israelis want to hit back hard, others want to de-escalate the situation, he said.
