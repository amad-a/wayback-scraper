Resistance lifts veil of secrecy on training(1)
The resistance briefly drew aside the veil of secrecy over its training methods on Friday, allowing reporters a unique opportunity to witness a noisy and smoke-filled military exercise conducted by members of the multi-faith Lebanese Resistance Brigades.

Reporters were packed into vans with blacked-out windows for the 20-minute drive from Baalback to meet the fighters among the remote rock-strewn heights of the Anti-Lebanon mountains.

Carefully observed by seasoned Hizbullah instructors, the 24 fighters, clad in green uniforms, helmets and with faces hidden beneath smears of camouflage paint, took turns to dismember cardboard targets at the end of a makeshift range with an assortment of small arms.

Other combatants fired rocket-propelled grenades at distant targets further up the hillside: a deafening blast, a tiny ball of light flitting up the barren slope, a puff of gray smoke and an instant later the report of the exploding round.

Tires were set alight and the fighters ran through dense clouds of black smoke and crawled beneath barbed wire as the instructors fired live rounds above their heads.

The Brigades were conceived by Hizbullah Secretary-General Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah in September 1997 to allow non-Shiites the opportunity to participate in the resistance. The group launched its first operation in March 1998 and since then has chalked up 175 attacks.

We have people from all over Lebanon: Sunnis, Christians, Shiites, Druze even Armenians, said Maher, a veteran resistance fighter who chaperoned the reporters.

One of the fighters, a 23-year-old university student who sported a pony tail hairstyle, said he had joined to defend the nation. He said he had served with the Brigades since its inception two years ago and had been involved in 10 operations against the occupation forces.

But when asked his religion, he paused.

Im Lebanese, he said.

Cutting an unlikely figure among his comrades was a 44-year-old Maronite surgeon.

I was concerned by what I was seeing in the south and I wanted to make an example. If I can do it at my age then so can others, he said.

He admitted that his family was unaware of his part-time activities with the resistance.

I tell them Im going to France on business when Im called up for service, he said.

Rami, the commander of the Brigades, said that his fighters would soon begin staging more audacious attacks in the south.
Theres an effort to move them forward, when chances allow it, deeper inside the occupation zone to fight the Israelis directly, he said.

The climax of the live maneuvers was a simulated ambush against an Israeli patrol. Four cardboard cut-out figures were placed to one side of a track beneath a towering hill that, from a distance, bore a resemblance to the Israeli-manned Beaufort Castle.

One of the instructors laid roadside bombs beside the track as the fighters, clutching their weapons, moved into position.

This is a typical sort of attack we stage against the Israelis, explained Maher. He indicated two groups of fighters to the rear manning 82mm recoilless rifles and heavy machine guns. They will provide the back-up for the infantry, he said.

With the fighters in position, nestled behind rocks in the hillside, the order to launch the attack was given.

The roadside bomb exploded in a huge ball of fire, the heat of which swept over the reporters sited just 70 meters away. As the appalling din of the blast died away, the fighters opened up with their machine guns and rocket-propelled grenades. The cardboard targets were shredded in seconds as squads of fighters surged toward the track firing guns and hurling hand grenades.

To the rear, the fire support teams sent recoilless rifle rounds streaking across the wadi, hitting Beaufort Castle high above in bursts of chipped rock and dust, while the hills echoed to the rhythmic drumming of the heavy machine guns.
