Associated Press on south residents' support to Resistance
    " Just days after the front porch of Muslim Fadel's house was shattered by shellfire from "Israeli"-allied militiamen, workers turned up on what was left of his doorstep, ready to fix the damage".

"These weren't just any handymen, though. They were from the "jihad al-bina," a name best translated as "construction for the holy struggle's sake." And they were sent by the Shiite Muslim guerrillas who are waging war to drive "Israeli" troops out of south Lebanon.

The workers quickly plastered gaping holes in the front stairs and repaired a smashed sewer pipe.

They couldn't fix a decades-old grapevine whose gnarled roots were severed by shrapnel, but they did shore up the trellis its branches were entwined in.

"I'm grateful, of course," said Fadel, an olive farmer in his early 70s. "But what matters isn't them fixing my house. It's them sacrificing their lives ... I can't pay back what I owe them for that."

"Such sentiments are among the reasons it has proved so difficult for "Israel's" powerful modern army to prevail against a relatively small and lightly armed band of guerrillas in south Lebanon.

In the 14 years since "Israel" carved out a 440-square-mile enclave to try to guard against cross -border attacks, the guerrillas have enjoyed an important edge: The backing of local people.

"A case in point is the front-line village of Hadatha, six miles north of the "Israel"-Lebanon border, and only a few hundred yards from "Israeli"-controlled territory.

"The village is faced on two sides by outposts of "Israel's" allied militia, the South Lebanon Army.

Guerrillas from Hizbullah and the pro-Syrian Amal movement fire on these outposts -- just across a steep, rocky valley from the village -- whenever they can.

"And the SLA fires back -- even though the guerrillas are usually long gone, since their standard method is to fire a few quick rounds and escape before their position can be pinpointed.

This hit-and-run style puts Hadatha directly in harm's way. But rather than resenting the guerrillas, the villagers _ overwhelmingly Shiite Muslims, like the fighters themselves _ express enthusiastic support for the guerrillas' cause.

"On the main road through the village, 7-year-old Hassan Nasser and his friends played in the afternoon sunshine, waving wooden sticks they pretended were guns.

"Turning his eyes toward the SLA post on the opposite ridge, Hassan piped up, "Someday I'm going to go up there and get them!" The watching adults beamed approvingly.

"I'm 80 years old -- I'll join the resistance if they'll take me!" called out a woman drinking tea nearby.

"All the "Israelis" have to do is get out of our country," said Zeinab Taha, 56, clad in a headscarf and long, enveloping robe. "We won't chase them all the way back to Haifa" -- a northern "Israeli" coastal city -- "but we will drive them out of here."

Of course, in a climate of such vocal support, any villager who disagreed with the guerrillas' goals or tactics would be unlikely to speak up. But there are other, quieter gauges of the bedrock popular backing.

"All the villages along the northern edge of the "Israeli" zone have elected town councils and mayors affiliated with Amal or Hizbullah. All are communities that have taken the brunt of fighting.

"It's been an experience that was bound to make them more militant," said Commandant Kieran
McDaid of the Irish U.N. peacekeeping contingent that has its headquarters in nearby Tibnin.

U.N. forces can sympathize with the villagers' plight, because in some respects they share it. An Irish-manned outpost in Hadatha was hit by nine artillery shells last month in the same barrage that
damaged Fadel's house. Two peacekeepers were wounded.

No one is supposed to target the peacekeepers, but U.N. officials believe "Israeli"-allied militiamen took deliberate aim at them, perhaps because they blamed U.N. troops -- and villagers -- for the guerrillas using Haddatha to fire on an SLA outpost.

"Within the "Israeli"-held zone itself, opposition to the "Israeli" presence is a little less pronounced.

"Israel" has 2,500 SLA militiamen who support their families with "Israeli"-paid wages. Other villagers in the enclave benefit from the "Israeli" presence because they are allowed to cross into "Israel" and work. And suspected guerrilla sympathizers face arrest and expulsion by the "Israelis".

But inside the zone, the help of local informants is key to guerrilla efforts. Such inside information about "Israeli" movements helps Hizbullah carry out attacks like the devastating roadside bombing that killed an "Israeli" brigadier general and three other "Israelis" last month.

In Haddatha, villagers say their determination to back the guerrilla cause has only grown as the long conflict has dragged on.

"Hizbullah is not alone -- they have the support of the people," said school principal Ali Soumi. "We teach our children to fight the enemy."
