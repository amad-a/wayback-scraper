 Lahoud puts brave face on donors’ delay

‘Lebanon did not liberate its land with complaints and tears’ but with resistance(2)

President Emile Lahoud played down a decision by donor countries on Sunday to indefinitely postpone a conference aimed at raising some $1.2 billion for the government’s plan to rebuild the South.

“The decision does not devalue Lebanon’s victory over the "Israeli" enemy,” the president said in a statement after meeting with Prime Minister Salim Hoss at Baabda Palace.

Hoss reported from the United Nations on Saturday that donor-country representatives “said they prefer to wait for the new government to be formed in Lebanon,” before holding the crucial conference.

Hoss will resign after the newly elected Parliament is installed on Oct. 15.

During his short stay in New York to attend the 55th session of the UN General Assembly, Hoss tried to arrange the conference for Oct. 9 and Oct. 10 to raise money for southern reconstruction and development.

Lahoud remained upbeat in his statement on Sunday and commended UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan’s efforts to hold the meeting.

“Lebanon did not liberate its land with complaints and tears but with resistance action, forcing "Israel" to leave without restrictions or conditions,” the president added. “The liberation makes Lebanon too significant to restrain itself with conditions for aid or to make concessions regarding its dignity and sovereignty.”

The conference was scheduled as a follow-up to a Beirut meeting of ambassadors from potential donor countries in July. They set the early October dates for the second conference, which was supposed to be attended by foreign ministers.

Despite the postponement, “Lebanon still hopes to raise $150 million to finance part of the plan dealing with urgent projects for the South,” an unnamed government source said.

“Donor non-governmental organizations will meet in Beirut on Sept. 28. The government will lobby them for this bit,” the source added.

The meeting will be attended by UN special envoy Terje-Roed Larsen and EU Middle East envoy Miguel-Angel Moratinos, the source said.

Speaking to reporters at the airport upon his arrival from New York on Sunday, Hoss refused to comment on speculation that the conference was postponed by the United States to pressure Lebanon to send its army to the South.

“The government is spending money on some development projects in the South. Some Arab countries have sent some financial aid. Real work will remain pending until the conference is held,” he said. “The postponement does not help Lebanon in general or the liberated areas in particular.”

Hoss also denied that the postponement was a way to pressure Lebanon to permanently settle Palestinian refugees living on its territories.

“The issue of permanent settlement can’t be linked to any other issue. Lebanon refuses to settle the refugees permanently for two reasons: first, it’s committed to the Palestinian cause and the right of Palestinians to return to their homes,” he said.

“Second, both the Taif Accord and the Constitution ban such a move. Lebanon can’t violate this ban, which is fundamental for national harmony.”

The government fears that the conference of donor countries may be postponed again after the new government is formed because the US, the main donor country, will be busy with preparations for its presidential elections.

“A Western ambassador told government officials that donor countries don’t give aid to a country that ended its military dispute with another country until final peace is reached,” the source said.

The postponement came on the heels of US Secretary of State Madeleine Albright’s refusal to meet Prime Minister Hoss in New York, citing a full schedule. “The timing of both incidents show that Washington is pressuring Lebanon to mass its army in the South and disarm the resistance movement,” the source added.

“Claims by donor countries that the new government may have a different plan for the South are illogical,” according to the source. “Would the number of destroyed homes, for example, change with the creation of the new government?”

Regarding his UN meetings, Hoss told Tele-Liban on Sunday that he received promises from Larsen that Annan “is seriously working on the release of Lebanese detainees still held in "Israel". He is contacting world governments over the matter.”

He also reiterated that "Israel"’s pullout was not complete, a point he stressed during his address to the General Assembly. "Israel" only pulled back to the Blue Line, which was drawn by the United Nations to verify the withdrawal but does not coincide with the internationally recognized border.

Hoss was received at the airport by masses of supporters, who accompanied his motorcade to his home in Aisha Bakkar, chanting slogans about his success in expressing Lebanon’s views on the UN podium.

Deputy Speaker Elie Ferzli said he believed that donor countries “will not give Lebanon any aid before the a final solution is reached for the Arab-"Israeli" conflict.”

Speaking to the Voice of Lebanon radio on Sunday, Ferzli said: “I found it very strange when the conference was set for October. I didn’t believe that donor countries would give money to Lebanon in the wake of the "Israeli" withdrawal.”

 

Villagers cross Blue Line in brief ‘liberation’ of Shebaa (3)

Up to 150 villagers crossed the UN-delineated Blue Line near Kfar Shuba on Sunday and entered the "Israeli"-occupied Shebaa Farms, sparking a tense stand-off with dozens of "Israeli" troops.

The protest was the first time Lebanese have entered the Shebaa Farms district since 1967, raising the possibility of further cross-border demonstrations in the remote mountainous area.

The villagers, mainly from Shebaa and Kfar Shuba, carried Lebanese flags and banners as they surged through the Hassan Gate, the crossing in the "Israeli" security fence separating Lebanon from the Shebaa Farms and the occupied Golan Heights.

The demonstrators attempted to proceed south to Ramta but were stopped about 200 meters from the fence by around 30 "Israeli" soldiers and two Merkava tanks. The Israelis had arrived on the scene from the Jabal Summaq outpost, 1 kilometer to the South.

Shouting anti-"Israeli" slogans, the protesters planted their flags and stood opposite the "Israeli" soldiers. UNIFIL peacekeepers took up positions between the protesters and the soldiers. “It was very peaceful. There was no trouble,” said a UNIFIL officer.

After about 30 minutes, the demonstrators returned to the Lebanese side of the fence.

“This march is to express our support for the government’s stand that advocates the return of Lebanon’s sovereignty to the region and the liberation of our land from the "Israeli" occupation,” said Yehya Ali, head of the Arkoub Residents Committee.

“I call on the government to submit a complaint to the Security Council to protest "Israel"’s continued occupation of our land,” he added.

The farms stretch for about 16 kilometers from the village of Shebaa on the western flank of Mount Hermon in a southwesterly direction to just east of Noukhaile, close to the border between Syria and "Israel".

The area was owned by residents of Shebaa and Kfar Shuba. The residents claim that the real border between Lebanon and Syria lies 2 to 3 kilometers east of the existing border portrayed on contemporary maps.

The Shebaa Farms were captured by the Israelis during the invasion and subsequent occupation of the Syrian Golan Heights in 1967.

Lebanon’s claim to the Shebaa Farms is based on a 1951 agreement with Syria in which the area was ceded to Lebanese sovereignty.

The UN-delineated Blue Line places the Shebaa Farms under "Israeli" control. Lebanon has agreed to abide by the Blue Line but has voiced its reservations over three disputed sections of the line and continues to demand the return of the Shebaa Farms.

Qassem Hashem, the Marjayoun-Hasbaya MP who led Sunday’s protest, said the residents of Shebaa and Kfar Shuba had every right to enter the area.

“The Israelis are the violators, not us,” he said.

Hashem, a member of the Baath Party, claimed that he and the other villagers had not crossed the Blue Line.

“The Blue Line is unclear at this point because every time the UN establish the line’s location, the Israelis try to draw another line that suits them,” he said.

Because the Blue Line runs 250 meters from the "Israeli" security fence on the Lebanese side, Hashem and his comrades crossed the line even before they reached the Hassan Gate and the beginning of the Shebaa Farms.

 

Commemoration of "Israeli" Sabra-Shatila massacre(4)

Marking the 18th anniversary of the "Israeli" Sabra and Shatila massacre began on Saturday.

In commemoration of the slaughter that had been perpetrated in 1982 by the "Israeli" invading forces and the disbanded Lebanese Forces militia, a march set out from Beirut to the cemetery in which the victims were buried.

Besides Lebanese party representatives and political figures, an Italian parliamentary delegation took part in the march.

The Italian delegation visited Hezbollah's Secretariat HQ and met the party's Deputy Secretary General his Eminence Sheikh Naem Qassem.

We must never forget this day (of the massacre), said Luisa Gantini, one of the delegation members and head of the Italian Peace Movement.

Speaking during a visit to the Palestinian refugee camp in Ain el-Hilweh, Gantini said Sabra and Shatila was not the only massacre perpetrated against the Palestinian people at the hands of the "Israeli" occupation forces.

The Italian delegation was received warmly at the camp's entrance as military and scout squads were reviewed.

After Ain el-Hilweh, the delegation toured south Lebanon and paid respect at the Qana massacre cemetery.

Beirut Palestinians Recall Sabra, Shatila Massacre (5)

Palestinians in Lebanon recalled family and friends butchered in Beirut by Christian militiamen 18 years ago on Saturday in the worst atrocity of "Israel's" 1982 invasion of Lebanon.

They held rallies, protest marches and a photography exhibition to mark the Sabra and Shatila massacre -- a tragedy most Palestinian refugees say they cannot forget.

 Camp residents said their bitterness and sorrow would only be erased when they won the right to return to their homeland.

 Christian militiamen started a three-day killing spree in Sabra and Shatila on September 16, 1982, after being let in by "Israeli" soldiers who sealed off the two camps when they occupied the western sector of the Lebanese capital.

 The militiamen massacred more than 1,000 men, women and children in one of the worst atrocities of Lebanon's 1975-90 civil war while the "Israeli" soldiers ringing the camps stood by.

The corpses lie today in a patch of land between the camps normally used as a rubbish dump but cleaned up for the anniversary.

 But for the survivors the dead are still present.

 "That's Abu Mahmoud," said Aisha, gripping the arm of her teenaged daughter and pointing to a picture of an old man's bloated corpse dotted with flies.

 "I remember very well the day we found him and my father. I remember the black blood in the streets, the stench. It feels just like it was yesterday. My father had a feeling something was going to happen so he sent the women and children south for a few days. He died and we survived."

Refugees unwelcome (6)

Life came to a standstill at Shatila on Friday night as dozens of Palestinians chanted anti-"Israeli" slogans while marching through the sewage-soaked roads towards the cemetery.

 Some held candles stuck in plastic bottles. Others held banners demanding the right of return to the homes they were forced to leave during the 1948 creation of "Israel".

 "We don't want to be naturalised (Lebanese) nor do we want to go to a foreign country, we want to return to Palestine. Death to "Israel"," they shouted, walking past mounds of garbage and mangled cars.

 Aly Faisal, from a radical group that criticised Palestinian President Yasser Arafat's decision to delay the declaration of an independent Palestinian state this month, told the rally he wanted a country that would avenge the Sabra and Shatila killings.

 "Soon, we will have a state that will go to the International Court of Justice and sue "Israel" for the death of our kin," he said. "The time of reckoning will come soon."

 The United Nations estimates there are 365,000 Palestinian refugees in Lebanon. sentiments that set off the civil war.

 "Any Palestinian who tells you that he doesn't want to return to the homeland is lying," affirmed Issa Hamoud, a 36-year-old fighter who now repairs watches.

 Refugees remember massacre(7)

The usual piles of trash lined the sides of the street. Cinderblock homes riddled with bullet holes outlined a maze of muddy alleyways. Skinned calves hung on hooks attracting flies, just like any other day.

But this day was supposed to be different. Thousands gathered in the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps near Cite Sportive on Saturday to remind the world of the horrors of the 1982 massacre.

Teenagers and children carried banners, waved flags and played bagpipes as they led the annual march to the mass grave on the edge of the camps.

“I lost my whole family then. It left me with a hole so deep that I wish I was murdered with them,” said Mohammed Abu Rodyna, who was only four when the massacres took place. At 22, his hair is already graying and his face sagging with wrinkles.

This was where, 18 years ago, decaying bodies bloated under the sun and thousands of empty cartridge cases lay in the streets. Rubble from bulldozed homes concealed dead bodies inside.

It was a three-day murder spree, starting on Sept. 16, in which 2,750 men, women and children were slaughtered, according to the International Committee of the Red Cross.

Hundreds of Christian militiamen were allowed to enter the camps by the "Israeli" Army to flush out Palestinian guerrillas who stayed following the Palestine Liberation Organization’s departure two weeks earlier.

Completely surrounded by "Israeli" tanks and troops, the Palestinian and Lebanese residents had nowhere to go as hundreds of militiamen entered the camps armed with guns, axes and knives.

But on Saturday, children skipped and chattered to each other as they prepared for a ceremony they have celebrated every year of their lives.

“We always talk about the massacres, about how it happened. We know it as well as we know our own names,” said 16-year-old Walid Balkis. “We participate today to send a message to the world outside and to show "Israel", our enemy, that we’re still determined and standing on our feet,” he said. Walid was not even born when his grandfather, Ali, was killed.

But this year somehow felt different. A sense of hope filled the air and hundreds more than usual turned out to mark the 18th anniversary of the massacre. “We’re more unified this year because we feel like Palestine is slipping through our fingers,” said Sanaa Khaled Akkar, the wife of a victim of the massacre, hinting at the faltering Middle East peace process.

“It’s a very critical time to show support and talk about our right to return to our homeland” she said. “We want to show the world that we haven’t forgotten our land. What future do our children have here in Lebanon?”

The camps’ residents were also encouraged by an Italian delegation’s efforts to create a memorial for the massacre and lobby support in the Italian Parliament for their right of return.

“It’s been 18 years and what have they done for us? This is normally a garbage dump,” said Loutfieh Shammas as she stood on the reddish sand that covers thousands of graves. “I’m glad the Italians are here. We want to preserve the memory of our loved ones and we feel others sympathize with us,” she said.

The march, normally ending with a prayer above the mass grave, ended instead with a series of speeches “We hope we can stand shoulder to shoulder with the Lebanese government in finding a solution for the refugees,” said Ali Faisal, spokesman for the Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine. “Without the right of return … we’d have a very explosive situation.”

 

Massacre of Palestinians at Lebanese refugee camps remembered (8)

Palestinians and Lebanese commemorated the 18th anniversary Saturday of the massacre of 1,500 Palestinians at the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps in Beirut.

 An Italian member of the European parliament attended the commemoration at the site, now a rubbish dump but given a makeover for the occasion. 

The massacre of the elderly, women and children in the camps was carried out over three days by the Christian Lebanese Forces militia while the "Israeli" army was deployed just outside the camps.

 An "Israeli" parliamentary commission found the Jewish state responsible for not preventing the massacre, which happened in the heat of the "Israeli" invasion of Lebanon.

 Relatives of the victims point an accusing finger at Elie Hokeika, now an outgoing member of parliament but then the intelligence chief for the Lebanese Forces.

 He denies all responsibility for the massacre. 

For the first time since the massacre, a foreign delegation attended a rally in memory of the victims buried in a common grave. The Italian delegation was led by European parliamentarian Luisa Morgantini.

 The burial site in wasteland covered with ochre-coloured sand and surrounded by a wall and a group of small houses has recently been whitewashed by the local authority.

 There is no inscription at the cemetery where all year round market traders dump rotten fruit and vegetables and where waste water from local houses runs and gives off nauseating smells. On the dry part of the cemetery, children play soccer.

 Young Palestinians draped in Palestinian flags headed Saturday's procession from Shatila, which was followed by a troupe playing Scottish bagpipes, a throwback to the period of the British mandate in Palestine, from 1920 to 1948.

 Lebanese, Palestinians and members of the Italian delegation then gathered in the cemetery for speeches. Among the speakers was Abu Mahe Yamani, one of the founders of the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, who condemned "the horrible massacre carried out by Zionists and their agents."

 Morgantini said: "it is totally unacceptable that the cemetery is in such a state; it should be turned into a mausoleum and sealed off to stop it being profaned."

 She told AFP that she planned to launch a fund-raising campaign so that "the victims (of the massacre) can rest in peace."

 Away from the official ceremony, relatives recalled those three nightmare days from 1982.

 Jajje Benuatti, 70, carries pictures of his four sons "killed by the Christian militia 18 years ago," inscribed on the photos.

 Abu Sami Hrur, 28, who lost his father, his mother and four brothers and sisters in the massacre, said he only escaped by hiding in a dog kennel.

 Adnan Moqdad, a Lebanese Shiite, says he lost 43 people close to him in the massacre.

 "The heads of my 80-year-old parents were cut off. The streets of Shatila were littered with the bodies of decapitated old people, disemboweled women, young men riddled with bullet wounds and children with their heads smashed," he recalls.

 Following the "Israeli" pullout from Beirut which followed the massacre, the Palestinians put up a commemorative plaque around the mass grave.
