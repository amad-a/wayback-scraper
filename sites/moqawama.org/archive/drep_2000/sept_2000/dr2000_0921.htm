Fadlallah: US-”Israeli” plan behind ‘political confusion’…(1)

The religious leader Sayyed Mohammad Hussein Fadlallah accused the United States on Wednesday of being behind much of the recent “political confusion” in Lebanon and said the postponement of a conference of donor countries, scheduled to have been held in Beirut next month, was “in harmony” with a US-”Israeli” plan.
Speaking at a weekly press briefing, he called on the Lebanese “not to believe America and its promises” and urged politicians of various shades to join hands for the country’s national interest, both economic and political.
He was referring to the past week’s anti-Syrian protests by supporters of the banned Lebanese Forces in both Beirut and Mayfouq, Jbeil during which calls for ending neighboring Syria’s military role in Lebanon were made.
Reports speculated that the delay was meant to pressure Lebanon to deploy troops in strength throughout the South and along the border with “Israel”.
Fadlallah said that since the preliminary meeting of representatives of donor countries in July, he had cautioned that “what is awaiting Lebanon is further promises which won’t be fulfiled.”
He added that implementation of these promises would not take place “except via American-”Israeli” conditions which include the question of army deployment in the South ... and not out of keenness for Lebanon’s stability and sovereignty, as alleged.”
The leader added  that “We believe this postponement is in harmony with the American-”Israeli” plan drawn up for Lebanon following the liberation (of the South).”
Fadlallah continued: “We believe the US is behind much of the political confusion in Lebanon lately, notably enflaming the political atmosphere against Syria.”
He also accused the US of seeking to maintain discord in Lebanon through a deterioration of the economic and political situation and attempts to disrupt ties with Syria, “as if America tells us: ‘either you heed our conditions or expect repeated confusion and pressure’.”

“Israel’s” withdrawal signals Bastara’s bittersweet return…(2)

“Israel’s” troop withdrawal to behind the United Nations-delineated Blue Line has resulted in a sliver of the Shebaa Farms coming under Lebanese jurisdiction, along with the only family to have remained on the occupied territory since 1967.
For over three decades, the extended Zohra family has struggled to survive in conditions of extreme poverty, isolated from their fellow Lebanese, without such amenities as electricity and running water and surrounded by the apparatus of “Israeli” occupation.
The family lives in two single-room stone hovels in the hamlet of Bastara, beside an abandoned “Israeli” outpost towering above the plain of northern Galilee and the hills of southeast Lebanon.
Bastara, one of the 14 Shebaa Farms, was included in an “Israeli” incursion zone, which saw the border fence pushed deeper into Lebanon about 10 years ago to annex part of the high ground with views stretching deep into the south.
Last Sunday, residents of Kfar Shuba and Shebaa villages entered the former incursion zone through the Ramta gate and drove 2 kilometers along the old “Israeli” patrol road to Bastara. For Layali Zohra, 17, they were the first Lebanese she had ever seen at her home.
“We were very happy to see them here,” she says, smiling shyly as she helps her aunt, Inam, kneed laundry in a basin of filthy water.
The home of Qassem Zohra, Layali’s father, is tiny. The only natural light filtering into the gloomy interior comes from the open door and a small glassless window.
“We’re tortured here,” says Ghalia Zohra, Layali’s aunt. “There’s no water. No electricity. We have to travel to Kfar Shuba each day to get water and buy food.”
All 11 members of Qassem’s family sleep on the earthen floor. Lining one wall are black plastic bags containing the family’s clothes and sacks of lentils, rice, salt and wheat ­ their staple diet.
An open fire in the corner fills the room with acrid smoke. A large cauldron of boiling lentils rests on stones surrounding the fire. The ceiling of corrugated tin is black with soot. Swarms of flies and wasps surround the entrance, attracted by piles of goat dung. Qassem has gone to Kfar Shuba, 6 kilometers north, to fetch the supply of water, which until recently had to be undertaken by mule.
To reach Bastara one must drive south out of Kfar Shuba along a gravel road to the Ramta Gate, which marked the limit of the former incursion zone. The final stretch was impassable to vehicles until two weeks ago, when the track was widened.
Once through the gate, there is the bizarre experience of driving on an “Israeli”-built road with signs in Hebrew, all the while peering through the three-meter high electrified fence into south Lebanon. It is easy to forget that the road is on Lebanese territory.
The road dips back onto the “Israeli” side of the Blue Line a few hundred meters east of the Zohra’s home. A barricade of bulldozed earth blocks the road. Lebanese flags and banners left by last Sunday’s demonstrators are still tied to the fence beside the barricade. “Stop Israelian attacks on UNIFL in Arkoub,” reads one banner ­ an out-of-date and mis-spelled message signed by the Arkoub citizen’s committee.
A Merkava tank, its barrel pointed menacingly at the barricade, is barely visible among the scrub and boulders on the other side of a small wadi. A soldier standing on the Merkava peering through a pair of binoculars yells a greeting and waves at the unexpected visitors to the frontier.
The “Israeli” soldiers were not always this friendly. During the occupation, Qassem and his uncle Mohammed, who lives in an identical stone building nearby, were subject to the whims of “Israeli” troops manning the Ramta crossing.
“We’d turn up there in the morning and they would keep us until the afternoon before letting us through,” says Mohammed.
Their only source of income was to sell olives and fresh goats’ milk in Kfar Shuba and Halta. “Very often the milk would go sour in the sun as we waited to be let through,” he says. “We would throw it at the fence and return home.”
Mohammed unleashes years of pent up frustration at the hardships he and his family have endured. “When they put the fence up 10 years ago and built this position,” he says indicating the security fence and abandoned outpost a few meters away, “they destroyed 500 of my olive trees. I only have two left now.”
He says that the “Israeli” had attempted to drive his family out of the area at the time by stealing his goats.
“They took 200 of them over two days,” Mohammed recalls.
He hurries into his house and returns a minute later clutching a yellowed photocopy of a statement he made to the police in Hasbaya. “I complained to the UN and the police but no-one did anything.”
Mohammed says he refused to be intimidated by the “Israeli” and remained in Bastara, despite the construction of an “Israeli” outpost beside his house.
“They kicked us out of my old home in Mazraat Qafwa and blew up the buildings when they invaded (in 1967). I owned land here in Bastara so I brought my family here. I was not going to move out again,” he says.
There are no Lebanese still living in the Shebaa Farms that remain under “Israeli” control. Most of the farmsteads have been occupied by Jewish immigrants from Ethiopia.
Mohammed’s grizzled face breaks into a smile as he recalls the district before 1967 when there had been no “Israeli” soldiers or steel mesh fences restricting their movement.
“The farms ran from Banias (in occupied Syria) along Wadi Aasal to Jabal al-Sheikh (Mount Hermon). We were all farmers and we harvested the land,” he says.
Having ceded their incursion zone, the “Israelis” are constructing a new border along the path traced by the Blue Line. The clank of heavy machinery is carried on the warm breeze from the southeast as “Israeli” engineers cut new roads through the mountainside.
But the Zohra family’s return to the authority of the Lebanese state has not been without its disadvantages.
“Before the liberation we used to be able to go to the rest of the farms. Now the “Israeli” shoot at us when we try to enter them,” says Mohammed.
The end of the occupation has raised hopes among the family that the state will provide them with basic amenities.
“We’ve been completely cut off here for years,” Inam says. “All we need is some electricity and water so that I can buy a washing machine then life here would be beautiful.”
