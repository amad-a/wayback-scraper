Fisk gets caught up in Middle East cyber-war

The shockwaves from the cyber-war being waged in the Middle East appear to have spread outside of the region.
Over the course of the last week, the website of the British newspaper The Independent (www.independent.co.uk) has been flooded with thousands of angry e-mail messages and the newspaper itself all but drowned in a deluge of telephone calls and letters.
Unlike the recent attacks on websites in “Israel” and in Lebanon, which have been the result of hackers proving that technology can be political, the onslaught at The Independent was led by readers who believed ­ mistakenly ­ that the newspaper’s long-time Beirut-based Middle East correspondent, Robert Fisk, was about to be silenced or perhaps fired.
Like all great Middle Eastern stories, this is a tale that is part truth, part fiction and heavy on (mis)perception.
Earlier this month, a US-based academic, perhaps as a joke or perhaps voicing a genuine concern, sent out an e-mail suggesting that given Fisk’s reputation for not toeing the “Israeli” line, and his sometimes controversial stance on Middle Eastern affairs, he might come under pressure to stop writing about the bloodletting in the Palestinian territories.
On Oct. 18, a US-based organization called the Islamic Association for Palestine reissued the warning, which it sent out to various activist organizations and message boards.
Within days, what had started out as a simple observation became a global call-to-arms.
Somewhere along the way, the wording in the association’s message was changed, “might be pressured” became “is being pressured.” As a result of this confusion, more than 3,000 people decided to let The Independent have a piece of their minds.
In the heat of the moment, passions fueled by the deteriorating and increasingly desperate situation in the West Bank and Gaza Strip few stopped to question if the rumor that The Independent was about to silence one of its longest-serving, award-winning journalists was true.
In hindsight, it seems hard to believe anyone would have given the rumor much credence. But these are unusual times.
The Independent did not help matters when it included on the newspaper’s website a discussion forum entitled Is Robert Fisk Biased?
To anyone aware of the association’s message, this was proof that the claims had substance and that a witch-hunt was on. Action became imperative.
Fouad Fawwaz was one of those who acted.
“When I got the first IAP message, I didn’t think anything of it,” said the 33-year-old, who describes himself as an “armchair activist.”
“I read The Independent because I value Fisk’s coverage, and I always believed they did too. I couldn’t believe they would try to shut him up.”
But when he discovered the discussion forum, Fawwaz believed that he had misjudged the situation.
“I couldn’t understand why there would be this forum unless it was true that The Independent was trying to clamp down on Fisk,” he said. “It looked like trial by e-mail.”
According to Leonard Doyle, foreign editor at The Independent, no one on the paper knew what was going on until they were hit by the wave of protests.
“The Independent website is a completely separate entity to the newspaper,” he explained. “It’s under different editorial guidelines and not, strictly speaking, part of the newspaper.”
Doyle, whom Fisk described as “outraged” at the existence of the forum, ascribed the forum’s creation by an Independent staffer to “naivete and a lack of comprehension of Fisk’s role in the journalistic world, particularly as it relates to the Middle East.”
If it is difficult to believe that a major newspaper would allow this kind of slip-up at such a sensitive time, it was not only The Independent who were caught off-guard. As the online debate raged, Fisk remained oblivious.
He does not read the online version of The Independent and says he does not even have an e-mail address. But as the letters began to flood in, news reached friends in Beirut.
“Lots of helpful and kind people have rung up asking if I have any problems (with The Independent),” Fisk explained by phone, sounding somewhat weary. “What happened is someone made a joke and then it got taken seriously. It has no relation to reality.”
Real or not, rumors die hard and if the claim sent a spark through the activist community, it had a similar effect on Fleet Street.
Earlier this week, in a bid to silence chattering tongues, Independent editor Simon Kellner was quoted in Times of London journalist Giles Coren’s column as saying that he had no idea where the campaign had begun.
Kellner went on to describe Fisk as “a jewel in our crown” and that contrary to rumors, Fisk “isn’t going anywhere.”
Thursday afternoon the forum was finally closed down, 10 days after it was started, but not before some inflammatory postings were made. Evidently some people do believe Fisk is biased.
The Independent is considering the possibility of taking legal action against some of the less inhibited participants.
For Fisk, on his way to report on the West Bank, closure has not come a moment too soon.
“I spent a day on this when I should have been working,” he said. “This garbage is taking people’s attention away from the real issues in the Middle East”…(3)

Attacks on Jews worldwide 'worst since Nazi Germany'…(8)

Jewish leaders are asking the United Nations to condemn a recent wave of anti-Semitic attacks around the world which, they say, have reached levels unprecedented since the rampages of Nazi supporters against Jews in Germany in 1938.

The Simon Wiesenthal Center in Los Angeles has presented Kofi Annan, the UN secretary-general, with a catalogue of 200 anti-Semitic incidents that have occurred in scores of countries since violence returned to the Middle East at the end of September, including in Britain.

The organisation, which keeps memories of the Holocaust alive to help eliminate anti-Semitic sentiments more than half a century later, was also highly critical of the UN for failing to recognise the attacks. It stopped barely short of suggesting that the UN itself had inadvertently encouraged them.

"There has been nothing but total silence on the unprecedented anti-Semitic attacks," commented the founder of the Wiesenthal Center, Rabbi Marvin Hier, after handing the list of incidents to Mr Annan in New York on Thursday night.

"We don't believe there has ever been, except going back to 1938 to Kristallnacht, as many synagogues desecrated and attacked in such a short time."

Most of the incidents, ranging from the scrawling of graffiti to the stoning of worshippers have happened in France, the Center said. But they have also been seen in Britain, Canada, the US, Germany, Russia, Brazil, Italy, Spain and elsewhere. According to the list 93 synagogues in different countries have been vandalised since 28 September.

A synagogue was broken into in Efrat on the West Bank yesterday by vandals who daubed walls with swastikas, police in Al-Quds said last night. "This is the first time such a thing has happened," said the mayor of Efrat, Eitan Golan.

Rabbi Hier noted that the worst of the attacks had broken out after the UN Security Council responded to the worst of the violence in the West Bank by condemning “Israel”.

Many Jewish leaders consider the UN to be biased towards Palestinian interests while being unwilling to speak up for Jews when they are threatened. The UN Charter, Rabbi Hier said, demanded that it speak for both sides.

Mr Annan, however, has been striving to end “Israel’s” comparative isolation within the UN. His efforts appeared to have paid off when he was invited to the region recently to help mediate between “Israel” and the Palestinian Authority.
