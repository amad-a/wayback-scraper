Recovered hill with a view invaded by Israeli cameras(1)

The joint United Nations and Lebanese Army border verification team on Wednesday discovered a possible Israeli violation of the Blue Line on the summit of the imposing Abbad hill near Houla, their first since beginning an inspection along the frontier three days ago.
A small observation post manned by several Israeli soldiers is located suspiciously close to the Blue Line, causing UN observers and Lebanese Army officers to spend over an hour assessing whether the position breached the line of withdrawal.
Earlier, the team regained around 1,500 square meters of Lebanese territory when a gate leading to a former Israeli incursion zone near the settlement of Manara was forced open.
The joint team inspected seven locations along the border Wednesday from Blida to Abbad hill opposite Houla. The operation went smoothly until the verification team scaled the strategic hill at Abbad, the site of one of the most important and largest Israeli outposts during the occupation.
The convoy of eight UN vehicles, including an ambulance, wound its way up a steep rutted track which ended at a military gate along part of a former Israeli incursion zone. The incursion once ran along a ridge from Addaisseh, 5 kilometers north of Abbad hill, to Manara, 1 kilometer to the south. The gate was pulled open and a Polish sapper swept the dusty earth leading to the asphalted former patrol road for landmines.
From the ridge, 800 meters above the plain of northern Galilee, the settlement of Kiryat Shemona was reduced to childlike proportions. The Golan Heights, to the east of Galilee, was barely visible through the haze. Another settlement ­ Margaliot, a collection of trim red-roofed houses ­ nestled halfway up Abbad hill. Given the all-clear, the convoy followed the old patrol road to the shattered remains of the heavily-fortified outpost on the summit of Abbad.
The Blue Line slices through the outpost, leaving more than two-thirds inside Lebanon. The other third falls below the skyline in Israel, blocking the sweeping views of south Lebanon that Israeli troops were able to enjoy during the years of occupation.
And therein lay the problem. To overcome the blindspot, the Israelis had constructed an observation post beside the concrete and stone border marker on the summit of the hill. The post was smothered with a camouflage net. Three television cameras rotated slowly on the roof, surveying vast tracts of Lebanese countryside.
The verification team clambered over earth barricades and approached the observation post, all the while watched by several Israeli soldiers.
Brigadier General Jim Sreenan, the head of the UN team, spoke to an Israeli officer, apparently asking him and his men to move aside so that the Lebanese delegation, headed by General Amin Hoteit, could conduct their measurements. Hoteit and the other Lebanese officers studiously ignored the presence of Israeli troops, only a few meters away.
Despite spending an hour to check whether the post breached the Blue Line or not, the UN team member said that Sreenan would have to travel to the Israeli side of Abbad hill to conduct further measurements.
He could not confirm whether the observation post violated the Blue Line, pending further examination. The team will continue inspecting the border Thursday.
Earlier, Lebanese soldiers had used bolt cutters to open
a padlocked gate beneath the settlement of Manara, allowing the team to enter a part of Lebanon that had been restricted to all Lebanese for almost two decades.
This is like opening the gates to Heaven, Hoteit said, as he marched through.
The general refused to comment on details of the operation during the day, saying, no words, just action.
Israeli soldiers ran down the new border road as the team crossed into the former incursion zone. As elsewhere along the Blue Line, Israeli troops silently watched the Lebanese conduct their measurements.
The recovery of the incursion zone entailed for Israel the loss of one of the most popular tourist spots in the northern part of the Jewish state.
Beside the squat, drab box-like houses of Manara is a viewpoint that allowed curious Israelis to gaze into the occupation zone. Ice creams and drinks were sold to onlookers. It was from this spot that reporters saw an Israeli tank fire a shell into a parked car on the road below, killing Abed Taqqoush, a long-time driver for the BBC and the last civilian fatality of the occupation.
The Blue Line, however, places the viewpoint on the Lebanese side of the line, creating a security dilemma for Israel due to the lack of space to build a new border fence and patrol track.
But any enterprising Lebanese who would like to open his own ice cream parlor at the viewpoint will first have to negotiate his way through a minefield some 300 meters deep running the length of the former incursion.


A number of fleeing collaborators return to Lebanon along with families(2)

Seven Lebanese families of collaborators who had fled to "Israel" as its 22-year occupation ended returned to Lebanon on Wednesday, the police said.

The 22 people, including seven children, crossed the border at the western point of Ras Naqoura in vehicles belonging to the UN Interim Forces in Lebanon, who delivered them to police in the port city of Naqoura, police said.

The families were all originally from Debl in the former Israeli-occupied border zone.

Their return raises to 250 the number of Lebanese who have returned from occupied Palestine since fleeing there at the end of the Israeli occupation on May 24, according to a police tally.

About 6,000 Lebanese, many of them members of the Israeli occupation's proxy militia, fled south as "Israels" occupation ended.

On Tuesday, hundreds of the collaborators in occupied Palestine and their family members demonstrated in Ashkelon and Netanya against the poor treatment they are receiving from the Israelis saying, "We have been thrown to the dogs!"

Demonstrators exclaimed that they have been "forgotten in small stuffy rooms".

"We have become refugees in a foreign land," explained one collaborator in Ashkelon, who added that conditions for him and his family are unacceptable.

Another militiaman expressed his absolute disgust, adding that he served as "Israels" ally in the occupation's militia for 15 years, and is now a refugee with no hope for a better tomorrow.

On the other hand, an Israeli senior military source said the Zionist entity is spending 225 thousand US dollars (NIS 1 million) daily to cater to the needs of the collaborators living in occupied Palestine.

The mayor of the Shlomy colony had refused to receive the Lahd militiamen in his town and asked the Zionist cabinet to take them to a colony in the Tiberias area.


154 collaborators men go before court(3)

The trial of the 18th batch of former Israeli-proxy militiamen began on Wednesday with defense attorneys appealing for clemency for all 154 defendants. Military Judge Maher Safieddine, who presided over the session, was expected to issue indictments later the same day.


In Long Fight With Israel, Hizbullah Tactics Evolved(4)

 Hizbullah, the guerrilla organization that forced Israel from the slice of southern Lebanon it had occupied for 22 years, grew from a small band of amateurish gunmen to a highly sophisticated tactical operation savvy enough to exploit a new weapon of modern warfare -- television.

"The use of media as a weapon had an effect parallel to a battle," the top Hizbullah commander in southern Lebanon, Sheik Nabil Qaouk, said in a rare interview with a Western journalist. He discussed in unusual detail how Hizbullah learned to improve its effectiveness, strategy and weaponry, and how to videotape its successes for distribution to the media.

"By the use of these films, we were able to control from a long distance the morale of a lot of Israelis," he said, underscoring how demoralization as much as defeat helped to drive the Israelis from southern Lebanon.

From once relying on teenage suicide bombers to crash cars into Israeli installations in the mid-1980's, Hizbullah tactics -- primarily ambushes, assassinations and roadside bombs -- became increasingly well planned and executed, military observers in the region say.

A turning point came three years ago this month with the ambush of a raid by elite Israeli naval commandos in which a dozen were killed.

The Israelis halted such raids for more than a year, trying to find out what went wrong, and then Hizbullah put out word that it had penetrated Israeli intelligence, the sheik and Western military observers said, further halting Israeli counterinsurgency operations.

The effect was to drive the Israelis into bunkers, conceding land and initiative to Hizbullah.

And the impact of their strikes was amplified by the fact that they were videotaped by hidden Hizbullah cameramen and that the tape quickly found its way onto Israeli television, sometimes even as the Israeli Army spokesman denied the incident had taken place.

The effect was what the military calls a "force multiplier," greatly undermining Israeli will.

"Seventy-five percent of Hizbullah's war was the videotapes," said an experienced official with the United Nations peacekeeping troops in the area.

The videotapes were rushed to Beirut, where copies were delivered to Lebanese television and Western news agencies like The Associated Press and Reuters, which made them widely available. They were, however, first shown on Hizbullah's own television station.

The videos were often shown on the nightly television news in Israel, helping bring home the cost of the occupation. They were a stark contrast to the standard fare served up by Israeli military reporters, whose coverage was severely restricted and censored by the army. Blurred and grainy as they sometimes were, the Hizbullah videos contributed to the rising public pressure in Israel to bring the troops home.

But the tape would not have been taken at all without the improved intelligence gathering and combat skills Hizbullah developed over the years.

"When they first started, they thought they could do it with a bunch of people on a hill yelling 'Allah-u akbar,' " a United Nations official in the area said of the Hizbullah fighters. "They would lose 40 in an operation. Now they are very sophisticated, very disciplined."

In the interview, Sheik Qauok said the guerrillas had been able to improve their effectiveness by studying each operation, learning from their mistakes and developing new uses for weaponry. He also outlined their reliance on sometimes stunningly low-tech methods to thwart Israels more numerous and more sophisticated forces.

"The resistance always focused on the weak points of the enemy," he said as he outlined a classic theory and practice of guerrilla warfare.

Military experts say that at any one time Hizbullah maintained only about 500 full-time fighters, although it could mobilize many more supporters. The Israeli troops in southern Lebanon usually numbered about 1,500, backed up by 2,500 more troops in their proxy Lebanese militia, which collapsed and fled when the Israelis abruptly pulled out of southern Lebanon on May 24.

Hizbullah officials say they lost 1,200 fighters over the years, and yellow roadside markers are being put up at the spots where they fell. Hundreds of Israeli soldiers were also killed.

There were two kinds of wars, Sheik Qaouk said, guerrilla war and conventional war. By alternating tactics, he went on, Hizbullah was able to maintain the initiative and, after a clash, melt away into a largely sympathetic populace.

"By limiting the firing, we were able to keep the cards in our hands," the sheik said. "We were able to do small, little battles where we had the advantage.

"Even the rockets the resistance used had to be used intelligently.

We would do an action only in the time and place where it had an impact."

The sheik and other military observers cited a number of Hizbullah innovations. These ranged from roadside bombs made of fake plastic rocks, which could be bought in Beirut garden stores for $15, to running farm animals across areas monitored by Israeli motion sensors. At the same time, Hizbullah had the ability to jam Israeli radar and closed-circuit television monitors.

Since it was never driven and did not show up on Israeli heat sensors, an old Soviet T-55 tank hidden in a cave and fired sporadically took the Israelis months to find, the sheik said. Hizbullah fighters discovered that wire-guided antitank rockets, which can, in effect, be steered in flight, could be directed into small openings in concrete bunkers.

"Even the man who invented these rockets did not know this," Sheik Qaouk bragged. The sheik smilingly refused to say if they had American-made TOW missiles, more accurate than the old Saggars they had been using.

Above all, the sheik said, he and his fighters "were always ready for martyrdom," but the Israeli soldier "probably wasn't ready to die for this."

"More important than everything," he said, "is that the Israeli soldier didn't believe in the war."

He also stressed the importance of a good intelligence network. The effectiveness of that network was demonstrated with the assassination on Feb. 28 last year of Brig. Gen. Eretz Gerstien, the top Israeli Army liaison officer to the proxy militia by a roadside bomb as his car passed in a military convoy. He was the highest-ranking Israeli officer killed during the occupation.

Similarly, last Jan. 30, the militia's second-ranking commander, Col. Aql Hashem, was killed by a bomb at his house in Marjeyoun.
