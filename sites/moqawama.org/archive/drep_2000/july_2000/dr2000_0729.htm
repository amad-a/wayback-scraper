UN troops move to border

Beirut: All violations must be rectified ahead of deployment(1)

UNIFIL troops tentatively took up positions on two hills along the border with Israel on Friday but a full deployment of peacekeepers into the liberated areas will not occur until Israels violations of the Blue Line have been rectified.
The initial deployment consisted only of two platoons, one Ghanaian and the other Irish, dispatched to Manara and south of Yaroun.
Prime Minister Salim Hoss said that no further peacekeeping troops would be sent to the former occupation zone until all Israels violations of the Blue Line of withdrawal had ended.
Lebanon insists that all violations be removed before UNIFIL deploys, Hoss said.
This is our fundamental standpoint and it was agreed upon by the United Nations, which had rectified past violations and is still working on removing the new Israeli violations, he added.
Brigadier General Amin Hoteit, the head of the Lebanese delegation to the border inspection team, denied that UNIFIL had begun deploying.
All there is to it is that a few UNIFIL peacekeepers are accompanying the teams of inspectors to facilitate their mission. Deployment will not commence before the Lebanese team announces the end of the Israeli violations, he said.
Some 30 Ghanaian peacekeepers moved at dawn from the sector they have manned since 1978 to a new post on the border beside the Israeli settlement of Manara.
Two Ghanaian soldiers, dripping with sweat beneath their heavy flak jackets and helmets, erected a flagpole in the stony soil. The blue UN flag snapped in the warm breeze.
This is an historic day, said a Ghanaian captain as he stood on the edge of the viewpoint beside Manara, his new outpost, and gazed at the desolate, brown rolling hills and wadis of south Lebanon.
Bewildered settlers of Manara slowed their cars as they passed by to stare at the unfamiliar scene of UNIFIL soldiers parking their armored personnel carriers beside the road. Other settlers snapped photographs of the Ghanaian peacekeepers and Lebanese reporters. The two groups, Lebanese on one side and Israeli settlers on the other, stared at each other in awed silence. The atmosphere was similar to a teenage dance, with the boys and girls lined up on opposite sides of a dance hall, too shy to speak to each other.
A pair of curious Israeli soldiers questioned three Ghanaian soldiers on their duties with the peacekeeping force. The UNIFIL men responded uneasily. Other Ghanaian soldiers began uncoiling barbed wire along the Blue Line, carefully watched by the Israeli soldiers. Before the Israeli withdrawal two months ago, the Manara viewpoint fell inside Israel. Israeli tourists would eat ice creams and sip soft drinks while listening to distant explosions and watching plumes of smoke rise in the distance as Israeli troops traded daily blows with the resistance.
From this point, Israeli and foreign reporters had watched and filmed the death of Abed Taqqoush, a driver for the BBC, who was killed by an Israeli tank shell less than 24 hours before the occupation ended.
One of the anomalies of the UNs so-called Blue Line, however, was to relocate the viewpoint inside Lebanon, bringing the border to the edge of a civilian road running around Manara. The Israelis, alarmed at the prospect of armed Hizbullah fighters coming with in a meter or two of Israeli civilians, attempted to construct a 200 meter long military patrol track on Lebanese soil as a buffer.
But the deployment of Ghanaian soldiers at the disputed spot appears to have brought an end to any further construction activity.
Blobs of blue paint marked the frontier in the absence of a fence. A member of the Lebanese border verification team said that the violation at Manara had been rectified but Lebanon would not be fully satisfied until a permanent barrier had been erected.
There is nothing stopping the Israelis from crossing over at the moment, he said. There are several other positions like this where we also want some sort of obstacle.
The officer said that they were located at Labboune, near the coast, Misgav Am, above Odaisseh, and Abbassieh near Ghajar in the east.
A platoon of Irish peacekeepers set up camp shortly after dawn at Boundary Pillar 18 on the border two miles south of the village of Yaroun, the scene of the second Israeli violation of the Blue Line. Reporters were unable to approach closer than 2 kilometers due to the presence of landmines.
An Irish patrol watched their comrades from a football pitch on the outskirts of Yaroun.
Well stay here and keep an eye on them, one soldier said.
Looking through binoculars, the Irish soldiers could be seen talking to Israeli troops standing on the edge of their outpost.
A UNIFIL helicopter circled the outpost at 1.30pm as General Amin Hoteit, the head of the Lebanese delegation to the border verification team, checked to see whether the violation had been rectified. The helicopter departed after a few minutes.


Lebanon to ask World Court for Israeli compensation(2)

The Lebanese government said Friday it was preparing to sue Israel before the International Court of Justice to seek compensation for damages caused by Israels 22-year occupation of south Lebanon, which a government study earlier estimated at eight billion dollars.

Lebanese Prime Minister Salim Hoss presided over a meeting Friday of specialists who are preparing the suit to be filed in The Hague.

An official source said Lebanon will seek compensation for both direct and indirect damage caused by Israeli military operations and by the Israeli army's occupation of southern Lebanon, which ended May 24.

A government study published in January said Israel was responsible for eight billion dollars worth of damages in Lebanon.


Hizbullah warns against continued Israeli detention of Lebanese(3)

 Hizbullah has reiterated warnings it will use all means possible to liberate nineteen Lebanese still held captive in Israel. Hizbullah MP Abdallah Qasir told families of detainees who held a rally infront of UN House in downtown Beirut, the patience of the resistance movement is eroding. Demonstrators called for the release of all Lebanese held in Israel. The head of the Follow-Up Committee for the Release of Lebanese Detainees in Israeli Prisons Mohammad Safa said activists will hold an open-ended sit-in in front of the UN headquarters in downtown Beirut until all hostages are released. Activists handed UN delegates a letter to Secretary General Kofi Annan demanding the immediate release of all Lebanese captives. They are being held without trial or charge as bargaining chips for information on missing Israeli serviceman Ron Arad. An Israeli court recently extended by three months the detention of the two most prominent detainees, Sheikh Abdel Karim Obeid and Mustafa Dirani, claiming their release posed a quote "threat to Israels security." 


Resistance readies itself for new role
Hizbullah looks after the stability of the area without being the security authority ­ Qaouk(4)

With the imminent deployment of UNIFIL peacekeepers into the former occupation zone, the fighters of the Islamic Resistance will abandon their makeshift observation posts along the border and disperse among the local population of the South.
Having fought an open war with Israeli forces for almost two decades, Hizbullahs military wing is set to play a less visible role in the South in the coming months as UNIFIL peacekeepers and Lebanese Army troops assume control of security.
However, this does not entail the demise of the Islamic Resistance. Although Israels occupation is over, a number of outstanding issues ­ such as the release of Lebanese detainees and the return of Palestinian refugees ­ as well as the lack of a comprehensive peace in the region will ensure that the resistance remains on standby.
Israel is a well-armed terrorist state, Sheikh Nabil Qaouk, Hizbullahs southern commander, told The Daily Star. Its Lebanons right to hold onto all its sources of strength. The resistance is one of the main sources of strength ­ a strategic weapon for Lebanon, Syria and the Arabs.
Following the cessation of fighting in May, squads of Hizbullah fighters deployed at former crossing points along the frontier. With each squad consisting of about four or five fighters armed with assault rifles, there are perhaps 100 guerrillas ranged along the border. They are reportedly drawn from the 300-500 experienced and older fighters who form the backbone of the Islamic Resistance.
Although there have been no serious confrontations between the Islamic Resistance and UNIFIL along the border, on occasions the peacekeepers have been temporarily denied access to roads along the frontier by groups of fighters. However, the deployment of UNIFIL troops into the liberated areas will bring to an end the armed presence of Hizbullah border squads, according to a Lebanese military source.
To leave the border free of security is illogical. Hizbullah still has some observation posts and patrols along the border to let the Israelis know they are still there. The military presence of Hizbullah will vanish, however, in the near future from the UNIFIL area, the source said.
Then, he said, the Islamic Resistance would adopt a covert rather than overt presence along the border. Security is not just a matter of a soldier carrying a gun. Hizbullah will continue to have eyes in the area, along the border and in the villages, watching and seeing everything that goes on.
The fighters will confine their activities to intelligence gathering and monitoring developments along the border. Domestic security affairs in the villages and towns of the South will be left to the army and Internal Security Forces.
Qaouk explained that the Islamic Resistance was not a substitute for the states security apparatus. Hizbullah looks after the stability of the area without being the security authority. This is the governments role, he said. There is no conflict or contradiction between Hizbullah and the security authority. We have no problem in understanding each others role.
Richard Norton, professor of anthropology and international relations at Boston University, who is also probably the Wests leading expert on Hizbullah, argued that the discipline of Islamic Resistance fighters made their deployment a necessary interim measure.
For months, Hizbullah leaders have been concerned to avoid a paroxysm of personal vengeance episodes in the South, he said. From the onset, there was no interest within Hizbullah in substituting for the army. But without the Hizbullah deployment there would be a vacuum in the south. The ISF, police and mukhabarat presence is insufficient.
Unlike a regular army, the Islamic Resistance is a fluid, versatile guerrilla force living in residential areas with no permanent military positions, barracks or heavy equipment. Consequently, while the fighters may merge into the background during the present cease-fire, the nature of the resistance allows for a swift mobilization if the need arises.
Qaouk would not be drawn on Hizbullahs response if Israel were to launch an assault  against Lebanon. We are not concerned with addressing specific scenarios, he said. We believe only that Hizbullah was, is and will continue to be the arms to defend Lebanon.
After the incident at Markaba, Hizbullah reasserted its role in support of the victory, stability and internal unity, Qaouk said. We realize very well that Israel is trying to stain the image of our victory.
The core members of the Islamic Resistance have evolved over the past decade to be among the most proficient guerrilla fighters in the world, adept at the use of small arms, artillery, explosives, intelligence gathering, propaganda and communications.
Dismantling the resistance is seen by some as a waste of talent and resources. This has led to speculation that the group could become affiliated to the Lebanese Army, possibly as a paramilitary force operating along the border with Israel.
Qaouk was dismissive, however, arguing that the Islamic Resistance and the army already cooperated. Further links were unnecessary, he said.
The military source also cast doubts on such a move. Its just not feasible. The bulk of Hizbullahs fighters are educated people: doctors, engineers, university graduates who will have no need to continue a military existence, he said.
Norton also believed the fighters would have little difficulty settling back into a civilian existence. The mainline resistance members are drawn from the fabric of Lebanese society. These are men with family ties, homes, networks of social support and hopes and aspirations for Lebanon, he said. There is no serious problem looming in terms of reintegrating the Hizbullah fighters into Lebanese society, because they are already integrated.


Arrest warrants issued to 10 collaborators(5)

Chief Military Examining Magistrate Riad Talih has issued arrest warrants for 10 collaborators accused of collaborating with an enemy power. If found guilty, the 10 could get up to 20 years in jail with hard labor. Over 2,000 surrendered Israeli-allied militiamen have been interrogated pending trial before a military court.
