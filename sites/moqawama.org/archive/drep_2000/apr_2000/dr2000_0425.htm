Hezbollah: Lahd's militia is Israeli, its stay means continuation of occupation (1)
Hezbollah Secretary General his Eminence Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah said the militia of agent Antoine Lahd is "an Israeli case, and Hezbollah will deal with it accordingly if it stays in the south after the Israeli pullout."

Sayyed Nasrallah told the Iranian Islamic Republic's News Agency (IRNA), "We don't consider Lahd's militia a Lebanese case or militia, for it is an Israeli case."

The stay of this militia in its outposts and barracks is a continuation of the Israeli occupation, he said, adding, "Israel will be completely responsible for all the acts that agent Lahd's militia may do."

Any Lahdi aggression on our towns and villages is an Israeli aggression and we will deal with it accordingly, he said.

The Hezbollah secretary general stressed that the collaborators in Lahd's militia are "Lebanese only in the identity cards they carry, but the militia's formation, project and goals are Israeli."

If the militia stays after the withdrawal it will do so to achieve Israeli goals and not to serve Lebanese calculations, Sayyed Nasrallah concluded.

 

Isreali shelling damages mosque, home in southern Lebanon

Shelling from the Israeli-occupied zone of southern Lebanon damaged a mosque and a house near the port city of Tyre early Monday, police said.

Two shells struck the Haddatha mosque and a neighbouring house, the police said, adding that there were no casualties.

The police said some 20 shells in all were fired at areas on the edge of the western sector of the occupied zone.

Meanwhile an official with the UN multinational peacekeeping force in Lebanon, UNIFIL, said an Irish post south of Tyre had been damaged by a shell fired from the occupied zone on Sunday.

There were no injuries reported but windows were shattered in the barracks at Haddatha, which houses 18 UNIFIL troops from Ireland, the official said, adding that UNIFIL had protested to the Israelis.(2)

Partial Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon unacceptable: Syrian FM (4)

Israel's pledged withdrawal from southern Lebanon must be total and unconditional, and any attempt to wriggle out of the commitment will be doomed to failure, Syrian Foreign Minister Faruq al-Shara said here Monday.

The Israeli pullback must be "total and absolute, from the Lebanese seas, land and skies," he told journalists after meeting Lebanese President Emile Lahoud.

Otherwise, "it would mean that Israel wants to perpetuate the current situation, only worse, having proposed something that it would not have carried out. That would be unacceptable."

"If they want something else, they will not succeed, even with the backing of the great powers. They will fail as they did in the 1980s," he warned, in a reference to the experience of the multinational force deployed in Beirut in an attempt to keep the peace after the Israelis withdrew in 1982.

Suicide commandos of Islamic Jihad blew up the headquarters of the US contingent of the force in October 1983, killing 241 marines, and destroyed a French post killing 56 troops.

The force, which also contained British and Italian troops, finally withdrew a year later, with little to show for its efforts.

Israel officially announced last week that it would withdraw its troops from southern Lebanon, where they have occupied a border strip since 1978, by July, whether or not it has reached a peace agreement with Beirut or Damascus, which is the main powerbroker in Lebanon.

Shara's visit to Lebanon came on the eve of a trip he is to make to France, where he will have talks with President Jacques Chirac and Foreign Minister Hubert Vedrine on Syria's position towards the peace process.

He said the interests of Syria and Lebanon were identical, both domestically and as far as the region was concerned.


Lebanese ex-prisoners face up to freedom after long years in Israeli jails (5)

The first morning after Israel released him from 13 years in prison Lebanese Abbas Srour woke up at dawn, prayed and started mechanically to pace his small room.

Only half an hour later did it occur to him that he could actually open the door and walk out as he pleased.

"For years, I was used to life as a hostage. I had to ask permission to go to the bathroom. I still can't believe I am a free man," said Srour, 37, with a sad smile under his thick beard.

"Years of detention since the age of 24 and being subjected to all kinds of inhuman physical and psychological torture do that to a person," he said, as a circle of well-wishers at his Beirut home nodded in silence.

Srour, his younger brother Abdel Hassan, and 11 other Lebanese were freed last week and returned home after Israel's supreme court ruled their detention illegal.

All were being held as bargaining chips to pressurize Hezbollah  to provide information about Israeli servicemen missing in action in Lebanon, including air force navigator Ron Arad.

"Arad's mother keeps the world's attention focused on his fate. But what about my old and sick mother? Six of her sons were jailed unjustly by Israel, including my 22-year-old brother Khalil who remains in Khiam," said Srour.

Hassan Hijazi was only 16 in 1986 when Israeli officers and their allied South Lebanon Army (SLA) militiamen came to seize him from his family home in Mays al-Jabal in the Israeli-occupied border zone in southern Lebanon.

When they could not find him, they took two of his brothers instead until he surrendered himself a few days later.

He was tried by an Israeli military court which sentenced him to three years in jail, after which he remained in administrative detention for 10 years during which he was transferred to 10 different prisons in Israel.

His crime? The teenager said he had written anti-Israeli graffiti on a wall in his tiny village.

Hijazi, the Srour brothers and the others, all tell the same tales.

Most of them were first detained at the notorious Khiam prison, in the border zone, run by the Israeli-allied South Lebanon Army (SLA) militia under Israeli supervision.

Conditions at the jail have been frequently condemned by human rights groups, including Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, who point to the torture and ill treatment of Lebanese arbitrarily detained there.

"For five months, I was subjected to interrogations during which I was severely beaten, I was whipped and tortured with electricity," said Hijazi.

The freed detainees said they were then transferred to a military jail called Sarafand in an unknown location in northern Israel where they were placed in tiny solitary cells painted either red or black.

"They prevented us from sleeping with a blinding spotlight and by forcing us to jump up, wear a hood and put our hands over our heads every time they entered our cells," said Abdel Hassan Srour.

"They sometimes used to do that 10 times in one night and if we were not ready, they beat us," said Srour, who was a Hezbollah member.

Then they were tried by military courts, but remained in detention as "bargaining chips" long after their sentences expired -- in some cases for an extra 10 years. Four of them were never put on trial at all.

That was what happened to Hussein Ahmad Ramiti and Hussein Bahij Ahmad.

In 1987, the two men were driving up the Monte Verde mountain road, a region controlled by Christian militias during the 1975-1990 civil war, when they were kidnapped by the now-disbanded Lebanese Forces.

"I was systematically beaten and tortured for a month and a half. Then, in 1990, I was taken by sea to Israel where I was held for 10 years without trial," said Ramiti.

If the detainees are proud to have received a hero's welcome in Lebanon and feel somehow vindicated by the planned Israeli pullout from southern Lebanon by July 7, they remain uncertain and disorientated about their future.

"I was taken at 16. I was a child and there is little I can do now. My life is completely shattered," said a frail bespectacled Hijazi.

"I don't even know how to talk to my children, especially my 13-year-old son Ragheb whom I have only just met because he was born 16 days after my detention," said Abbas Srour.

 

Lahd's militia detains resident for refusing to collaborate (6)
Reports from the occupied zone said the collaborators of the Israeli occupation in Antoine Lahd's militia have detained Ramez Abu Aliwa, 28, from his hometown of Bint Jbeil and took him to al-Khiam detention center.

The reports said agents Wisam Safi Mroue and Ali Fawzi al-Sagheer led the arrest operation which took place because Abu Aliwa had refused to collaborate with the militia and provide the agents with information on what goes on in the town.

The Israeli occupation forces had killed Abu Aliwa's father in 1978 as he was attending his cattle in the town's woods.

 

Israelis move away from border as south Lebanon withdrawal approaches (7)

Michelle Stoch and many of her neighbors are packing to move out of this hamlet on the northern border because they fear it will be too dangerous after Israeli troops withdraw from south Lebanon in less than three months.

"We just don't know what will happen, so we are playing it safe. Every time I hear the explosions my heart jumps. That is not why we came here," said Stoch while holding her five-week-old baby on her shoulder.

Hezbollah guerrillas fighting Israeli troops in the Jewish state's south Lebanon "buffer zone" just over the border have stepped up their attacks since Israel notified the United Nations it will leave Lebanon by July 7.

While Israeli positions in south Lebanon have absorbed the brunt of the Hezbollah's attacks, the border communities have also been damaged both physically and psychologically.

"I am sure countless women before me have been faced with the decision to hold their ground or to flee, and I am of the fleeing flock," Stoch said from her home, which is only meters (yards) away from the barbed-wire fence that separates Margaliyyot from south Lebanon.

The Israeli government has said it no longer needs to control a sliver of Lebanon to defend its own northern territory and that the withdrawal will bring peace because Hezbollah fighters will no longer have a reason to attack.

But residents here said they were increasingly unconvinced of the government's logic.

"It is hard to tell what is going to happen after the withdrawal, but judging from the increase in activity in the last few months, it seems that the fighting will continue," said Shalom David, who maintains a chicken coop in Margaliyyot.

"If they were already able to do so much damage with Israeli soldiers in south Lebanon, just imagine what they could do without them there," he added.

While the military has promised to beef up security for the frontier towns and has begun construction to shift a heavily used road further from the border, residents said there is no way that the work can be completed by July.

"If the military won't finish preparations and build shelters, I will leave. The military has said it would build more shelters, but they have not," David said.

Orna Karen, who lives a stone's throw from where a Katyusha rocket destroyed a chicken coop earlier this month, said it was difficult for her to move to a town out of artillery range because no one is willing to buy her house.

"Most people here are looking for other places to live. We are looking for a new house and the minute we find something we will take it," she said.

So far this year, seven Israelis have been killed and 20 injured, according to an AFP tally.
