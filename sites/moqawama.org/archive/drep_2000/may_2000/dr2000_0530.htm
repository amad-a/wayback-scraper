Nasrallah: A political master triumphant in Lebanon…(2)

" With a gift for oratory and a youthful charisma, Sheikh Hassan Nasrallah, the leader of Hizbullah, is a shrewd politician who stands victorious from "Israel's" withdrawal from Lebanon.

Elected in 1992 as head of Hizbullah when he was only 32, Nasrallah has invested his life in the fight against "Israel", which withdrew from southern Lebanon on Wednesday after a 22-year occupation.

Wearing a turban and black-rimmed eyeglasses, Nasrallah is a master orator who can delight his audiences with his eloquent literary Arabic, dashed with just enough popular usage to avoid sounding too solemn.

A smile on his bearded face, Nasrallah often shakes off his ring of security guards, seeking personal contact with crowds. His personal attention to his followers has won him the affection of thousands of young Islamists.

Born in depressed Quarantaine, a suburb north of Beirut, a young Nasrallah moved to southern Lebanon in 1976 after Christian militias "cleansed" the Shiite stretch of land that diced Beirut from Mount Lebanon. He became a partisan of the movement Amal, founded by Mussa Sadr, who disappeared in 1978 after leaving for trip to Libya.

Later in 1976, Nasrallah set off for three years in Najaf, the Iraqi holy city that was the most important Shiite center before Iran's Islamic Revolution. Nasrallah studied under disciples of Ayatollah Mohammed Baker el-Sadr, the founder of the Islamist party Ad-Dawaa who was assassinated by Saddam Hussein's government in 1979.

The same year, Nasrallah returned to Lebanon, where he was named Amal's official for the Bekaa Valley and later a member of the group's political office. But Nasrallah quit Amal in 1982, judging the movement's response to the "Israeli" occupation too timid.

Nasrallah helped form Hizbullah, or Party of God, founded with the help of 300 Guardians of the Revolution imported from Iran. He was immediately made Hizbullah's man in the Beqaa and in 1985 became the representative for Beirut.

In 1992, Nasrallah was elevated to Hizbullah's helm, replacing Abbas Mussawi, killed in southern Lebanon by "Israeli" helicopters. Nasrallah was re-elected in 1993 and 1995.

The father of two girls and three boys, Nasrallah's popularity reached a climax a year and a half ago with the way he handled the death in combat of his warrior son Hadi, 20.

On Nasrallah's instructions, Hadi's death was announced only by a routine statement from the "Islamic resistance," the same treatment accorded other martyrs in the fight against "Israel".

Hadi's body was eventually brought back to Lebanon in exchange for the remains of one of 12 "Israeli" soldiers killed in a Hizbullah ambush.

Nasrallah knows how to take on a vociferous posture, angrily warning northern "Israeli" residents of reprisal attacks after "Israeli" bomb raids and threatening death for "collaborators" unless they promptly desert.

Once the "Israelis" left, Nasrallah explained that his threats were part of the psychological war that led to the dissolution of "Israel's" proxy militia.

Nasrallah, whose movement is active in Lebanese politics, maintains good relations with Lebanese President Emile Lahoud and Prime Minister Salim Hoss.

But when Hizbullah marched triumphantly into the former "Israeli" zone, Nasrallah urged modesty.

"This victory," he told a crowd, "is as much the fruit of the resistance of the movement Amal, secular parties and the Lebanese resistance of all honest people in Lebanon, be they Muslim or Christian."

UN envoy and Lebanon at odds over 425
ISF presence on border beefed up to defuse tension…(6)

"A fresh round of talks between Lebanese officials and UN Special Envoy Terje Roed-Larsen on Monday highlighted the differences in interpreting the implementation of Security Council resolution 425.
As the two sides met to bridge the gaps, the government beefed up the presence of Internal Security Forces in the South and instituted new procedures to defuse tensions on the international border by requiring special passes for visitors.
During the talks at Baabda Palace, President Emile Lahoud told Larsen that "Israel" must withdraw from the Shibaa Farms, free all remaining Lebanese detainees in "Israeli" prisons and hand over the bodies of nine resistance fighters still held by the "Israelis".
Lahoud insisted that Lebanon would only accept the international border as a basis for the Jewish state’s withdrawal from occupied territory, and rejected “the so-called practical lines,” according to a statement from the Presidential Palace.
“We don’t accept the logic of the so-called ‘practical lines’ since Lebanon considers the international border the criterion for "Israel's" withdrawal according to Resolution 425 and anything other than that means the resolution has not been implemented,” Lahoud said.
The practical lines refer to the demarcation between UNIFIL, responsible for areas occupied by "Israel" in 1978, and UNDOF, which has manned the buffer zone between Syrian and "Israeli" forces on Syria’s Golan Heights since 1974.
Larsen and UN officials met Lahoud at Baabda Palace in the presence of Prime Minister Salim Hoss, the head of the Surete General, Major General Jamil Sayyed, the director-general of the Foreign Ministry, Zuheir Hamdan, and a ministry ambassador Yehia Mahmasani.
Larsen reiterated that the only way to settle the controversy over the farms would be for Lebanon and Syria to submit a joint document to the UN Security Council, defining their border. A political source disclosed that contacts between Beirut and Damascus would be established as soon as possible to work on the matter.
The Lebanese delegation also demanded that the withdrawal include the outposts along the border opposite Shibaa and the removal of the incursion zones where the "Israelis" have pushed the border fence into Lebanon. "Israel" has already removed most of the incursion zones and is in the process of dismantling the border posts by Shibaa.
As for the detainees, the source said Larsen expressed hopes that the release process would be accelerated.
The Lebanese delegation also reiterated its position on the need to settle the issue of Palestinian refugees, arguing that Beirut could not be held responsible for incidents triggered by Palestinian elements in Lebanon.
Larsen reportedly answered that all parties should try to avoid the occurrence of undesired incidents. But he was told that such events could occur accidentally, reiterating that Beirut would reject all responsibility.
The government on Monday sent ISF and intelligence personnel to the liberated villages. In all, about 1,000 men were deployed in the various villages.
Despite calls for deploying the army in the liberated area, officials maintain that no such move will be taken before "Israel's" pull-out is verified by the UN.
The political source argued that sending the army into the South would have meant putting it at the center of conflicts between parties enjoying a presence on the ground, and would have been asked to support one side against the other.
Nevertheless, Larsen continued his upbeat assessment of progress on implementing resolutions 425 and 426 and called on all sides to exercise restraint to ensure that no “international incidents” take place on the border.
The Norwegian envoy singled out "Israel" for its share of responsibility in clashes that injured several Lebanese civilians over the weekend.
“I raised this matter with Prime Minister Barak yesterday, and advised him to instruct his soldiers to exercise restraint,” Larsen said.
“Freedom of assembly and expression are basic rights,” the Norwegian diplomat continued.
“At the same time, it’s very important that all responsible parties concerned focus their efforts on ensuring that the exercise of these rights does not lead to situations that could produce an international incident. With "Israel" out of Lebanon, such border incidents can all to easily take on an international character,” he added.
Larsen said he hoped the UN’s border verification teams will have completed their task “within the next few days.

Lebanese Suffer the Legacy of "Israeli" Land Mines…(8)

" Villagers buried the pieces they could find of six-year-old Ala'a Adnan Madi on Monday, a gruesome reminder of the legacy of land mines left behind by "Israel's" 22-year occupation of Lebanon.

Madi and her cousin, 14-year-old Lama Hamadi, were in a packed car driving down a valley on Sunday when it struck one of the uncounted mines sewn by "Israel" and its local allies.

Madi was ripped to pieces and the car was hurled through the air. Hamadi also died. The five others injured included Madi's father, who was in critical condition.

Land mines have been scattered across Lebanon through 15 years of civil war and 22 years of "Israeli" occupation. The Lebanese army, assisted by various foreign organizations, has been working steadily since the civil war ended in 1990 to clear large swathes of the mountains.

But the "Israeli" withdrawal last week abruptly opened up heavily mined areas where Lebanese have not been free to wander in two decades. Lebanese rushed south to see the newly liberated land.

The first casualty came only hours after the "Israelis" left, with a 15-year-old boy losing his leg when he stepped on a mine. That was followed by a 10-year-old boy losing a foot and two men in their twenties each losing a leg.

"The whole of south Lebanon has been mined over the years," said Timur Goksel, spokesman for UNIFIL, the U.N. peacekeeping force in Lebanon. "But now there is a totally new problem."

Two U.N. experts in mine clearing arrived on Monday to work with the Lebanese government on removing the mines and mounting an urgent program to warn the public to avoid the danger zones.

"The area of UNIFIL is totally safe," Goksel told Reuters. Since they arrived in 1978 hoping to oversee the withdrawal of "Israeli" forces, the peace keepers have removed 15,000 mines and other explosives from their area.

"But the front lines and down to the borders are full of mines," he said.

Land mines were scattered indiscriminately around the hilltop fortresses maintained by the "Israelis" and their proxy militia.

More were planted along infiltration routes used by guerrillas, mainly in the wadis around the combat zone. It was one of those, planted on a dirt track between the villages of Kfar Roummane and Arab Salim, that killed the two girls on Sunday.

The U.N. troops first priority is clearing mines in areas where it must deploy near the "Israeli" border once the "Israeli" withdrawal is formally confirmed. Those minefields are generally marked and diplomats said "Israel" had guaranteed to provide detailed maps.

"These are marked by barbed wire and maps by "Israelis" but the rest is a free-for-all," Goksel said. "There are thousands of people touring the area and they are not very well informed about the situation."

The nation's big gamble…(11)

Moshe Arenz

""Israel's" Rout," runs the headline of this week's article in The Economist reporting on the IDF's* withdrawal from southern Lebanon. That is the general tenor of the reporting in the world's media, spiced with shouts of glee and victory in the Arab press. In this age of mass communications, it is perception that has become reality. What people see on television and read in the press is what most believe to be fact. There is no doubt that many in the Arab world believe that "Israel" has suffered a major defeat at the hands of a few hundred Hizbullah guerrilla fighters and many will conclude that pressure, when applied to "Israel" with adequate tenacity over an extended period of time, led to "Israel" giving in to Arab demands. If the Palestinians, encouraged by these reports, decide to take a leaf out of Hizbullah's book, our relations with them are headed for stormy weather.
Prime Minister Barak decided to gamble. Without assurance that either the Syrians, the Lebanese or UN forces would guarantee security along the "Israel"-Lebanon border, he decided to withdraw the IDF from the security zone in southern Lebanon. The towns and villages of the Galilee were placed on the front line, in the hope that the withdrawal would put an end to hostile actions against "Israeli" targets. The gamble may or may not pay off - we won't know for many months. In the meantime, it's going to be a tense period for all of "Israel", but particularly so for "Israelis" living in the North. Getting up in the morning with Hizbullah flags flying across the road is going to take some getting used to.
Most important political decisions, especially when they bear on the country's security, involve an element of risk: A risk that generally cannot be calculated, even though the term "calculated risk" is so often misused. It is the gravity of the worst-case scenario that makes the difference between a risky decision and a gamble. In the case of the withdrawal from southern Lebanon, the worst-case scenario is pretty grim.
Hostile action against civilian targets in the North at point-blank range is likely to incur civilian casualties. This is not a zero-probability event, as the television coverage in the recent days of hate and venom on the Lebanese side of the border makes abundantly clear. Barak's threats of dire retribution should this occur, properly directed against the Syrians, the real masters of Lebanon, may not as yet have had the desired effect. The Lebanese Army has not been ordered into the South so far, and the presence of Hizbullah and other unruly elements on the "Israeli" border makes for a pretty explosive mixture. Hafez Assad must be fully aware of this. "Let the "Israelis" sweat," he probably says to himself.
Just exactly what our response should be to sporadic incidents initiated by unidentified individuals from the Lebanese side of the border is a problem in and of itself. The degree of our ability to deter hostile activity is likely to be put to the test in the months to come.
The IDF's withdrawal to the international border is not irreversible. Far-fetched as it may seem at the moment, should it turn out that the present deployment cannot assure the safety of the people living in the North, the IDF may again establish a security zone. But after our betrayal of our allies in South Lebanon, we are not likely to find allies among the Lebanese again. The scenes of our allies who were abandoned by us, the wanton ransacking of their belongings left in the safekeeping of the IDF, the SLA** prisoners taken at gunpoint by Hizbullah to judgment in Beirut will surely be engraved in the minds of all Lebanese for a long time. On the day of the IDF withdrawal it became clear that not only had they been betrayed by us, but they had also been abandoned by us.
The government still owes the people of "Israel" an explanation for what happened. The collapse of the SLA was not unexpected. Actually, it was surprising that they continued to function as an effective fighting force during the past year, after hearing almost daily that it was the "Israeli" government's intention to withdraw from the security zone by July 7th, while no adequate arrangements had been made for them. When in the past few weeks the UN demand for the disarmament and dispersal of the SLA became known, and "Israel" made known its intention to meticulously adhere to all UN conditions, the officers and soldiers of the SLA knew that their fate was sealed - they had been abandoned by their "Israeli" allies.
Honorable behavior may not seem like first-priority stuff when it comes to major decisions. But in the long run, in a nation's history, it may well turn out to be of cardinal importance

Lebanese economy gets a boost from "Israeli" pullout…(14)

" The "Israeli" withdrawal from Lebanon has given a boost to the country's economy, strengthening the Lebanese pound after nearly two months of pressure, and reducing the cost of borrowing.

"The Central Bank of Lebanon has not had to intervene on the forex market to support the pound since Wednesday," a money-changer said Monday, referring to the date of the pullout.

Rates for interbank lending have also plummeted, falling to between seven and 7.5 percent, compared to 20 percent two weeks earlier, a banking expert said.

The Lebanese currency had risen to 1,508 pounds to the dollar Monday up from a low point of 1,514 pounds.

One bank director attributed the pound's appreciation to the "psychological impact" of the "Israeli" retreat.

"Speculative demand for the pound, set by relative uncertainty over the "Israeli" withdrawal, has disappeared and the market has found its balance, and the current commercial demand of about 10 million US dollars a day can be satisfied by the supply from banks and individuals," the director said.

"The governor of the Bank of Lebanon ought to write a thank-you letter to the "Israeli" prime minister, whose early pullback saved the Lebanese pound from strong pressure it was subjected to," he joked.

To maintain the pound's stability, the Central Bank earlier had to release some 800 million dollars from its net reserves, which were at five billion dollars at the beginning of April, to satisfy demand for dollars, banking sources said.

Central Bank governor Riad Salameh told AFP May 8 that he was determined to keep Lebanon's currency stable.


"Israel" - Lebanon's number one tourist attraction…(15)

" Forget the Roman temples of Baalback, the ancient port city of Byblos, even the majestic Forest of the Cedars; there seems to be just one tourist attraction in Lebanon today - "Israel".

Lebanese from across the country have been on a painstaking sightseeing mission to see one thing.

Not because they love it, but because they want to see what school texts have always taught them was their "enemy" that had occupied a border zone in southern Lebanon for 22 years until Wednesday.

"We took a day off from work and came all the way from Tripoli in the north to see what "Israel" looks like," said Samira Ahdab, wearing dark sunglasses and a safari hat under the blazing sun.

Like all visitors, Ahdab poses with her friends to take pictures with the uniform red-tile houses in the "Israeli" northern settlement of Metulla in the background.

"They are real savages because they have devastated our dear south and when we look at "Israel", we only see neat houses with blooming orchards. Now I have seen with my own eyes what an enemy can do," she said.

Akram Habib, a businessman from the capital, even telephones his brother all the way to the United States to proudly announce in a loud voice: "I am now standing on the last inch of Lebanon. I can see "Israel"!"

They are among thousands of Lebanese men, women and children who have been flocking in the last few days in cars and busloads to the Fatima Gate leading to "Israel" from the southern Lebanese village of Kfar Kila.

Some wear black chadors, others shorts and t-shirts with printed pictures ranging from Michael Jackson to Brazilian soccer star Ronaldo or Hizbullah chief Sheikh Hassan Nasrallah.

But nearly all carry cameras around their necks.

In a very entrepreneurial spirit, colorful vans were quick to park behind roadblocks preventing vehicles from reaching the crossing point to sell refreshments, candies, sandwiches, ice-cream and camera films.

Behind the vans, stands a large green plastic Katyusha rocket launcher, apparently left by the Hizbullah which spearheaded the guerrilla war to force "Israel" out of southern Lebanon.

The visitors walk along the long road parallel to the border fence to the metallic door which was once the main passageway for "Israeli" troops, their allied militia, and the 2,500 or so Lebanese who crossed everyday to work in hotels, factories or plantations in "Israel".

Then the crowd thickens in front of a small two-storey building, protected by tall metallic shields, on the other side of the border.

"Here is an "Israeli"," a woman shouts, pointing at the helmetted head of a soldier appearing furtively behind one of the windows on the upper floor of the building.

Separated by wire and 52 years Palestinian families finally reunite…(21)

"Tears flowed and hands reached out as dozens of Palestinian families saw each other 52 years after being torn apart.
Men, women and children stood on either side of the "Israeli"-Lebanese border in the liberated village of Dhayra with only a fence separating them as they arched their necks trying to recognize each other and called out their names.
“I am Itaf, the daughter of Rihan,” an elderly woman yelled from the "Israeli" side. “I am Itaf, the daughter of Rihan. Who are you? Do you know me?”

Despite the waist-high barbed wire, Itaf and other Palestinians stepped over it and walked to the fence. Her hand reached through it to grasp the hands of relatives on the Lebanese side that she had not seen since 1948.
“How did this happen to us?” she cried out. “Oh, how did this happen?” Itaf was born in the Acre region. Her family fled to Lebanon in 1948 and all contacts with relatives ceased.
All around, sobs filled the air as relatives either recognized each other or met for the first time. Most were children when they fled Palestine and only childhood memories remained of their friends and relatives. They persisted, calling out their names and trying to identify who was standing in front of them.
“We were neighbors, don’t you remember?” one woman shouted. “Tell me about everyone. What has happened to your uncle? And your aunt?”

"Israeli" soldiers had expelled her to Lebanon to join her Lebanese fiancee. She was only 13. She had never  returned nor seen her family since.
“I was alone here,” she explained. “All my family was in Palestine. Look over there, these are my sisters and this man over there is my brother, Ahmad. I’m the eldest. I’ve been praying to see them for so long. I’m so happy now.”

Meanwhile, dozens of voices exclaimed joyfully when identities were established. Youngsters kissed the hands of the elderly extended through the fence.  “You look like me,” said Hamdeh Mahmoud from Lebanon to her cousin standing in "Israel".
Mohammed Zahour, 75, stood silently. He did not see any of his friends. “I guess they died,” he said. But he did find his relatives who were children when he last saw them.
“I don’t recognize them anymore,” he said. Not knowing what to give her relative on the Lebanese side, a Palestinian woman passed over two Pepsi cans with Hebrew lettering. Another man threw over a keffieh, requesting that it be given to his brother. One family exchanged photographs.

"Israeli" soldiers watched the crowd but did not stop the Palestinians approaching the fence. “This is not exactly okay but it’s going on,” said one "Israeli" solider to a foreign journalist in the crowd.

The location was reportedly chosen because many of the elderly Palestinians used to graze their sheep in the area over 50 years ago, and  knew it well.  The message was spread and early on Monday morning, a busload of Palestinians, mainly from the southern refugee camps, climbed the hill to reach the fence. The day came to an end far too soon. Families made their way back after setting a date and time for the next reunion.
But they fear that they will no longer be allowed to return to the meeting point since the Lebanese Army has already forbidden civilians from entering the liberated zone without special permission.
And although "Israeli" soldiers have not yet protested, they have in the past forbidden Palestinians approaching the border. So there was no reason to believe that they would not prohibit them again. “What if we can’t come back?” said Moussa as she reluctantly left the site where the brothers and sisters she had left 52 years ago stood watching her tearfully.
“I’m supposed to come back on Friday. What if the "Israelis" or the Lebanese don’t let me see my family again?”
