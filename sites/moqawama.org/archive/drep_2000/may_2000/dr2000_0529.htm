Daily Report

 

May Monday 29-2000:


Introduction

Hizbullah's Secretary General H.E. Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah said that "if we find Ron Arad alive or dead, the price we will demand will not be the release of Lebanese hostages, but also Palestinian detainees held in "Israeli" prisoners".

Meanwhile, two girls were killed and seven people wounded, including four children, when a land mine, left by the "Israeli" retreated forces, between the villages of Kfar Romman and Arab Salim on the former frontline exploded.

Elsewhere, three Lebanese civilians were injured also yesterday in a clash with the "Israeli" troops at the frontier section of Kfar Kila town where the border Fatima crossing point located.

While the "Israeli" enemy Prime Minister Ehud Barak ruled out further military withdrawals from Shibaa Farms area along the Lebanese-Syrian border on Sunday, Lebanese officials continued to maintain Lebanon's sovereignty over the hamlets.


Nasrallah demands release of detainees held in "Israel"…(1)

Hizbullah’s secretary-general on Saturday demanded the release of Lebanese and Palestinians held in "Israeli" prisons in return for information on missing "Israeli" Air Force navigator Ron Arad.
Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah told Al-Jazeera Television that his group rejected equating the two, considering Arad a prisoner of war and the Lebanese and Palestinians as hostages.
But Nasrallah said "Israeli" Prime Minister Ehud Barak was well aware of the price for Arad’s release, if he is found.
“If we find Ron Arad alive or dead, the price we’ll demand will not be the release of Lebanese prisoners only, but also Palestinian detainees held in "Israeli" prisons,” he said. Nasrallah repeated past promises to continue trying to discover the fate of Arad, who was captured alive by fighters after his jet fighter was downed over the south in 1986.
Arad is a war prisoner, Nasrallah said, “because he was on a bombing mission.”
The secretary-general also said he was willing to start a new page in the relationship with France following Hizbullah’s victory over "Israel". Although he initially said that France, through its Prime Minister Lionel Jospin, destroyed what was considered a good relationship with Hizbullah, there was still an opportunity for improved ties.
Nasrallah, however, warned that the party rejected being insulted, referring to Jospin’s description of Hizbullah fighters as terrorists while he was on a visit to "Israel" in February. Earlier Saturday, French Ambassador to Lebanon Philip LeCourtier visited Nasrallah. It was the first meeting since the tension between Hizbullah and France over Jospin’s comments.
Also on Sunday, President Emile Lahoud, Premier Salim Hoss and other officials telephoned Nasrallah to congratulate him on the victory achieved with the withdrawal of "Israeli" forces.
The officials also praised Nasrallah’s call for national unity during the address he gave during his first visit to the liberated areas on Friday in Bint Jbeil. Mufti Sheikh Mohammed Qabbani also telephoned Nasrallah to congratulate him.


Two children dead in south Lebanon mine blast…(2)

" Two girls aged five and eight were killed Sunday and seven people injured, including four children, when a mine exploded on a road in southern Lebanon, police said.

The blast occurred near the former crossing point of Kfar Tibnite along a road linking the former "Israeli" occupation zone in southern Lebanon with the rest of the country.

Hospital sources said Alaa Madi, five, and eight-year-old Lama Hamadeh were killed on the spot.

Two people were seriously wounded, including Hilal Ammar, 11, and the 39-year-old father of Madi, while the others received light injuries, including three other children under 11, they said.

UN special envoy Terje Roed-Larsen has repeatedly called for the rapid creation of a demining team in southern Lebanon, where "Israeli" forces withdrew last Wednesday after more than two decades of occupation.

He has said he will press the "Israelis" for detailed maps of mine emplacements in the area.

Hundreds of thousands of people have flocked into south Lebanon, inaccessible for the past 22 years, since the end of the occupation early Wednesday.


Three Lebanese wounded in border clash with "Israeli" troops…(3)

" Three Lebanese civilians were wounded Sunday in a clash with "Israeli" troops at this border flashpoint, raising pressure on the Beirut authorities and Hizbullah to cool the mood of confrontation with the Jewish state.

They were the first casualties on the border since "Israel's" pullout from southern Lebanon on Wednesday after a 22-year occupation. The frontier section at Kfar Kila has become an area of growing tension since then, as Lebanese crowds have gathered to taunt "Israeli" border guards daily.

Tension went up a notch Sunday when some 20 Lebanese civilians, cheered on by a large crowd, stepped across the border into "Israeli" territory.

After repeated stonethrowing by youths, "Israeli" troops fired warning shots, then smoke grenades and finally live rounds.

Ali Barjawi, 21, who was taken to nearby Marjeyoun hospital for treatment, told AFP he had been wounded by shrapnel, but denied that he had stepped onto "Israeli" soil.

Smoke grenades thrown by "Israeli" soldiers made it difficult for witnesses to say where Barjawi had been standing.

Another civilian, Hussein Habib Hamid, 19, was also transported to Marjayoun hospital after suffering two fractures in the leg from an "Israeli" bullet, police and hospital sources said.

A third civilian, Jihad Zein, 28, was also slightly wounded in the foot, also from shrapnel.

The Lebanese government promised the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL) on Sunday that it would adopt measures aimed at avoiding confrontation at the border, a UNIFIL source told AFP.

"As of tomorrow, the Lebanese government will adopt new measures to channel the flow of people going to the south," the source said.

He said "contacts at the highest level" between UNIFIL and the Lebanese government were being held in an effort to "calm" the situation.

Meanwhile militants of Hizbullah movement attempted to close the gate at Kfar Kila with a bulldozer, three cement blocks and a burnt-out car.

A Hizbullah spokesman told AFP that the group's militants had received orders to prevent Lebanese youngsters from crossing the border into "Israel".

"Similar measures will be taken at the other gates along the border," he said.

UNIFIL and "Israel" adopted steps on Saturday to block access to the fence in the hope of preventing any escalation, but with little success.

"Israeli" troops at Kfar Kila fired blank warning shots Saturday to deter stone-throwing youths. Later, crowds, including women and children, briefly entered the empty strip between the fence and outer coils of "Israeli" barbed wire.

Incidents on the border are a key to determining whether southern Lebanon will enjoy calm or be plunged back into tit-for-tat violence after only a brief respite.


Barak rules out further pullout…(4)

"Israeli" Prime Minister Ehud Barak ruled out further military withdrawals from the Shibaa Farms area along the Lebanese-Syrian border on Sunday, but Lebanese officials continued to maintain Lebanon’s sovereignty over the hamlets.
“In line with (United Nations) and "Israeli" maps, 90 percent of the surface area around these farms does not belong to Lebanon, and these territories will not be evacuated,” a statement from Barak’s office quoted him as telling an "Israeli" Cabinet meeting on Sunday.
The "Israeli" Army said earlier that "Israeli" troops had destroyed and pulled back from two positions in the area of the Shibaa Farms, land that was seized in the 1967 war but claimed by Lebanon.
In its statement, Barak’s office said the outposts and a section of road in Shibaa had been evacuated and the army had “definitively situated itself on the international border according to the agreement with the United Nations and in line with UN Security Council Resolution 425.”
"Israel's" Army Radio said that the pullback was done to “avoid friction” with Lebanese resistance fighters, who say their campaign to recover Lebanese land will be incomplete as long as the Jewish state holds onto the Shibaa farms.
Lebanese residents say that the "Israeli" Army maintains at least five other positions in the area, including a radar post on Mount Hermon. The farmlands extend over roughly 25 square kilometers on the slopes of the mountain.
Speaker Nabih Berri said that the Shibaa Farms “are indisputably Lebanese.”
“This is proved by the fact that its residents are Lebanese,” the speaker argued, “and the fact that judicial rulings over real-estate disputes in the area were handed down by Lebanese courts.”
Speaking during a dinner held in his honor by a the Baskinta Cultural Club in a restaurant in Dbayyeh on Saturday, Berri brushed aside "Israeli" claims that the area is in fact Syrian. “Syria and even the Palestinians say that the Shibaa Farms are Lebanese,” the speaker maintained.
He stressed “the need for avoiding any dispute between Lebanon and the United Nations over the matter.”
Berri also told the Orbit TV satellite channel that “there will be resistance to liberate the area, but only after diplomatic efforts with the United Nations fail.
The National Bloc Party’s newly elected leader, Carlos Edde, said that the group “knows of a map at the French Archives in Nantes, France, showing that the Shibaa Farms are inside Lebanon. It dates back to the French Mandate, which delineated the border between Lebanon and Syria.”
Speaking during a news conference at the party’s headquarters in Gemaizeh, Edde added: “The map is filed under Syria-Lebanon/Beirut/448/5. It dates back to 1920 and was not amended by the 1923 Paulet-Newcamp Agreement between the French and British mandates, which defined the border between Lebanon and Palestine.”


PM pays second visit to newly freed areas
Hoss promises local leaders government will move swiftly to help redevelopment…(5)

Prime Minister Salim Hoss paid his second visit to the liberated areas on Sunday, touring the eastern towns and holding talks with local leaders about their communities’ needs.
Hoss was accompanied by his daughter Wadad and her son Salim, in addition to Information Minister Anwar Khalil and Post and Telecommunications Minister Issam Naaman.
“"Israel" didn’t leave the south; it was forced out of the south. It didn’t withdraw; it was defeated by the courage of the resistance and the steadfastness of the people,” the premier said in Hasbaya, his first stop.
Hoss, who toured the central sector of the former occupied zone on Thursday, was greeted by cheering crowds in the predominantly-Druze area. Many villagers danced the dabke and threw rice on his convoy. “The government will return to the liberated areas. You’ll feel this return soon. It’ll provide security and carry out badly needed development projects,” he said.
Naaman hailed residents of the liberated areas for “foiling "Israel's" plans to instigate sectarian strife.”
“"Israel" seems to have forgotten,” Naaman said, “that these areas have a long history of sectarian coexistence.”
Khalil urged the areas’ residents “to support the government’s efforts to carry out development plans. The government will never leave the residents of the liberated areas. It will be on their sides all along the way.”
After inspecting in nearby Zaghle what is left of Khalil’s home, which was used as barracks by "Israel's" proxy militia, Hoss and his companions moved to Ain Qinya.
In Bayada, the home of Druze spiritual activities, many Druze clerics called for security forces to deploy as soon as possible. The officials then headed to Shibaa, where they were greeted by Yehia Ali, the principal of the town’s high school that Hoss inaugurated when he was premier in 1977.
Ali, who was expelled from Shibaa by "Israeli" troops several years ago, explained to Hoss and the two ministers what the town needs in terms of services and development projects.
Hoss vowed that the government would continue to press for the liberation of the Shibaa Farms, which lie outside the village and are claimed to be Syrian by "Israel". The premier then inspected the farms from behind a barbed-wire fence. An "Israeli" jeep was driving and stopped a few hundred meters away, curious to find out what was going on across the fence.
The delegation also inspected Kfar Shouba, Kfar Hamam, Rashaya al-Fakhar, Fardis and Habbarieh. In Kawkaba, Hoss’ last stop, residents complained about the departure of many townspeople to "Israel".
“They should return. The judiciary is just and transparent and will do injustice to no one,” the premier pledged.
In an interview with NBN on Saturday, Hoss said that “security forces will not go to the south before UN peacekeepers finish confirming that the "Israelis" have pulled out to the international border.” He said that the process should not take more than “two to three days.”

Liberated region sees clerics and officials flood in…(6)

Dozens of clerics, officials and politicians flocked to liberated villages over the weekend, seeking to reassure Christian residents and promising help in reconstruction and reconciliation with the government.
Education Minister Mohammed Youssef Beydoun said that only the state would handle security in the south. “Security in the liberated areas is the responsibility of the Lebanese government only,” Beydoun said during a visit to Khiam on Sunday.
Beydoun, who toured the village’s notorious detention center, was responding to a question about the government’s ability to maintain security in the region, adding that “Hizbullah and other forces have a complete awareness and national responsibility” to realize this.
In Bint Jbeil, Tripoli MP Omar Karami said the entire population must “be unified at this historic stage.”
“We all have to be aware of our national responsibilities … Only the unity of the government and the people can restore this nation so that it can play a leading role,” Karami said.
Zghorta MP Nayla Mouawad, who accompanied Karami, promised to support the residents of the liberated areas
“I promise to remain by your side and defend your causes,” she said. “I’ll make sure that the government treats you like a forgiving mother.”
Several officials and clerics also visited Qozah on Saturday where they met the local pastor, Father Butros Najm and a delegation of residents.
The visitors included Bint Jbeil MP and Amal official Ayoub Hemayed, Jezzine MP Suleiman Kenaan, and a delegation of archbishops.
They met Najm separately and were briefed on the situation in the village and the demands of the residents.
A delegation of Armenian officials, including Tourism Minister Arthur Nazarian and several Armenian MPs and clerics, visited Naqoura, Alma Shaab, Aita Shaab, Rmeish, Marjayoun and other villages on Saturday.
They met local residents and listened to their demands, which focused on the need for the government to establish its presence in the region.
Other officials stressed the need for coexistence among Christians and Muslims as they visited liberated Christian villages and met with representatives of the local population.
Ali Fadlallah, the son of Shiite cleric Mohammed Hussein Fadlallah, visited Ain Ibl, where he met residents at the village church. He reassured them that relationship with Christians dated to ancient times, and promised to remain by their side. Fadlallah also called on the government to begin providing services to the liberated areas.
The president of the Higher Shiite Council, Mohammed Mehdi Shamseddine, also called on the state to mobilize all its efforts and institutions to help the south. Shamseddine visited Naqoura and Alma Shaab, where he met the local pastor, Father Maroun Ghafari.
He also called on all former “Israeli”-allied militiamen who left to "Israel" to turn themselves in to the government, adding that the judiciary would give them a fair trial.


In the steps of the Hizbullah…(7)
 A rare mood of joy and satisfaction overcame the Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza Strip this week, in light of the political developments. "The glorious victory of the Lebanese resistance," as it was described by the Palestinian media, "compared with the shameful retreat of the "Israeli" army" - brought several days of happiness. For a brief moment, the "prisoners' intifada" (dozens of detainees have been on a hunger strike for the past month) was forgotten, the settlements and daily hardships were forgotten, and virtually everyone sat glued to the television stations from around the world broadcasting the pictures of the joyous liberation of South Lebanon. Over the weekend, the Palestinian Authority organized official, relatively modest celebrations at the El Bireh city hall and the Gaza cultural center. There were also spontaneous outbursts of joy, and at one procession in Ramallah Hizbullah's yellow flags were flown. PA But while the joy on the street was genuine, Arafat and his men were also greatly embarrassed by the pictures from Lebanon. The reason is clear: Everyone made the obvious comparison between the Hizbullah's resistance and its successful results and the meager achievements of Palestinian diplomacy. Almost every official Palestinian spokesman declared in recent days that the lesson learned from Lebanon is that every occupation must end. Tayeb abd al-Rahim, director general of Arafat's bureau, called on Prime Minister Ehud Barak to "make a courageous decision to withdraw from all the Palestinian territories as well," and several commentators in Arab newspapers adopted a sympathetic tone toward the "Israelis", asking: Is it not a shame that a thousand "Israeli" soldiers were killed, and an immense military effort, with planes and all modern technology, was invested? Is it not a shame that millions of dollars were wasted, and in the end you "Israelis" suffered such a disgrace? The conclusion: The time has come for you to withdraw peacefully from the Palestinian and Syrian lands as well.
But the voices heard on the Palestinian street were of a different nature. Almost at every corner someone was quick to explain that Arafat had failed: The Lebanese fought and got their land back with honor, whereas Arafat is conducting shameful negotiations and has failed. A member of the Palestinian parliament said in a private conversation that for the first time he now has doubts about the Oslo path. Late Egyptian president Anwar Sadat also opted for the negotiation path, but he played his cards right and got all his land back; the Lebanese chose the path of armed struggle and got everything; Arafat, on the other hand, chose an unclear path and now it has become apparent that he made a mistake. This is the position expressed by most spokesmen for the Palestinian opposition, and specifically Hamas.
However, the contribution of the events in South Lebanon to the "Israeli"-Palstinian negotiations is clear: There is now heavy public pressure on Arafat not to give in and to fight to the very end to succeed like the Lebanese resistance succeeded

Holdouts keep toehold on Shibaa Farms
Zahra family has refused to yield in face of "Israeli" pressure…(10)

The goatherd shook his head. “None of Shibaa’s people have lived there since 1967,” he said, pointing beyond the barbed-wire fence to the Shibaa Farms across the border.
“Except the Zahra family,” he suddenly remembers. “They refused to leave.”
The Zahra family have waged their own war of resistance against the "Israeli" occupation.
Nouhad Zahra, her husband Qassem, and their nine children, in addition to her uncle Ahmad and his daughter, are the only Lebanese living in Shibaa Farms. They have resisted numerous "Israeli" attempts to drive them off their land.
“Leave our land and go where? We had to stay here,” Nouhad Zahra told The Daily Star while visiting Kfar Shouba to sell goats milk. “Today, they say the land isn’t Lebanese but we’re the ones who can prove the contrary.” When "Israel" captured the Golan Heights from Syria in 1967, it annexed the Shibaa Farms as well, cutting off Shibaa, an agricultural town on the steep western slopes of Mount Hermon, from the farms which fed it.
Agriculture was, and is, the only source of income for 95 percent of the largely Sunni population of Shibaa, which officially lies in Lebanon. With their farmlands off-limits and little alternative economic activity, Shibaa’s youth abandoned their homes in droves. They left behind them an aging population. But Ahmad Zahra and his wife stood their ground.
“My father refused to leave,” Ahmad’s son Mohammed, who was 12 at the time, recalled. “They took his goats, threatened him and tried paying him off but he wouldn’t go.
“My mother died there, on April 25, a month to the day before liberation. I wish she’d lived to see Shibaa freed.”
"Israeli" tourists come to the farms every day, to visit the Prophet Abraham’s grave. “We’re not allowed near it. We just watch from a distance,” Nouhad said.
About 15,000 people lived in Shibaa in 1988. Only 6,000 live there now. Without the farms, few will be able to return to their newly liberated homes.
Half-built homes line the narrow streets, which are carved into the rugged mountains which from a distance appear to defy gravity. Shibaa’s population in exile ­ most of whom live in Beirut or the Gulf, while others reside in South America, Canada and Africa ­ started building new homes in the town about two years ago, when an "Israeli" withdrawal became a realistic possibility. More buildings are necessary if Shibaa is to accommodate even a small proportion of its sons and daughters. But the locals are hopeful.
Abu Tareq, one of the village elders, pointed to the destruction of one of the "Israeli" bases perched on the surrounding hilltops ­ altogether the Middle East’s largest military observation post ­ as an indication that the "Israelis" are preparing to leave the farms.
But on United Nations maps, most of the farms appear to be in Syria, and the UN has ruled that they fall outside the jurisdiction of Resolution 425, which called for the full withdrawal of "Israeli" troops from Lebanese territories.
Asaad Farhat, Shebaa’s mayor, said he challenged the United Nations to prove that the farms do not belong to Lebanon, adding that the UN was being unjust.
“I remember my father, who was a mukhtar and who was present when the map was drawn, telling me that there was a problem because of one olive tree, which had one branch in Lebanon and one in Syria,” he said.
On Thursday, "Israeli" Premier Ehud Barak told French President Jacques Chirac that "Israel" would withdraw from part of the farms ­ areas that some UN maps indicate are in Lebanon.
Locals claim that "Israel" is clinging to the farms, which cover 250 square kilometers, because it is taking advantage of their fertile soil, once planted with olive trees, fruits, vegetables and nuts.
From this side of the border, the farms look like a wilderness covered by brush and rocks. Shibaa itself is framed by barren hillsides.
The old men in the main square claimed they have title deeds to the farms. They showed us one document. The deed was handwritten and signed on a yellowing piece of paper in pencil and ink. But the signatories and any witnesses are long since dead. Even so, the elders reiterated that “the village is part of Lebanon and that the farms have belonged to the village since before the Ottoman era.”
Both Lebanon and Syria seem to agree. Last Friday, UN special envoy Terje Roed-Larsen opened up new avenues of negotiation, claiming that the Shibaa dispute was “technical and not political.”
“It’s not up to the UN to demarcate borders. It’s a matter for states to decide themselves,” he said. “The two countries (Lebanon and Syria) don’t have an international border agreement. If Lebanon and Syria signed an international border agreement in the future, they ­ should they wish to do so ­ may request that the Security Council change the mandates of UNIFIL and UNDOF.”
“We want our land given back to us, to our country, we want to live. I’d like to be able to see my father before he dies,” one man said.
The dramatic landscape of Mount Hermon, almost 2,000 meters above sea level, still has isolated patches of snow.
“There used to be a ski resort in Bayader, near the farms, but it was destroyed by the "Israelis",” said one youngster visiting the area for the first time in 10 years. Returning residents are considering building new ski resorts.
The village locals claimed that it was local defiance that created these new opportunities. Now they are ready to boast about their suffering and resistance.
“There was no armed resistance here,” said Abu Khaldoun, who hopes to run for Parliament, “but we resisted huge pressure to collaborate and tried to get on with our lives.”
The old men described how "Israel" blew up homes to intimidate residents. When families refused to join they forced them from their beds and drove them away, warning them not to return. They took the men to the notorious Khiam detention center. Some were even killed.
“They surrounded the town once, trying to force us to collaborate, but we refused so they tried to tempt us with food supplies, but to no avail,” Abu Khaldoun said.
Several “Israeli”-allied militiamen in Shibaa surrendered last week. Their families remain, Abu Tareq said, because people understand they were unwilling collaborators. “I think 22 surrendered.”
The mukhtar and Abu Khaldoun found the figure reasonable. The goat-herd disagreed.
“There were more than 100 militiamen in Shibaa, and they’re gone now. It was only 100 because 300 others handed themselves in last year, when they were offered light sentences.”


Fish back on the menu in southern Lebanon after "Israeli" pullout…(11)

"A gleaming harvest of sea bream, perch and red mullet tumbled from the nets of fishing boats here Sunday as trawlermen celebrated unfettered access to local waters following "Israel's" pullout from Lebanon.

For 15 long years, Tyre's inshore fishing fleet barely ventured beyond port, so fearful were skippers of suffering harassment at the hands of "Israeli" patrols in Lebanon's territorial waters.

"I went out to do some deep-sea fishing for the first time without having to cross those wretched "Israeli" gunboats in the morning mist," said Hassan Tahhan, 61.

He and his son were getting ready to head out for sea again.

A year ago, he says, he was wounded in the shoulder by a round fired from an "Israeli" navy vessel. Around 20 of his colleagues had suffered the same fate over the past 15 years.

Seeking to thwart any guerrilla attacks on northern "Israel" by sea, the "Israeli" navy used to stop any vessels from operating in a zone 50 kilometers (30 mile) long by two kilometres (one mile) wide from the coast.

The "Israelis" fired warning shots in the air to intimidate the boats, cut their nets or arrested their crew before releasing them after interrogation, hours, days or weeks later, the fishermen said.

Wary that the pullout might be short-lived, they only dared to return to the sea some four days after the "Israelis" left.

Issam Ali, 45, rubs his hands in glee. His catch was spectacular. "The pickings were meagre under the "Israeli" restrictions. A typical catch would not bring in more than about 30 dollars. Today, I got 75 dollars at the market."

Khalil Shahine was jubilant. He caught 20 kilos (44 pounds) of perch and 30 kilos (66 pounds) of mullet.

"I went down to the border at Ras Naqoura and I saw an "Israeli" gunboat, but this time it was in "Israeli" waters. As fishermen, father to son, we know the best catches are to be made off the coast around Naqoura," he said.

Along the quays, men repaired nets and women and children helped to bait hooks. From time to time, they burst into song -- about the wide open sea, the families waiting for fishermen to come home and their safe return.

The head of the fishermen's union, Khalil Taha, said there were 260 vessels and 510 fishermen based in Tyre.

"Most of them are fathers, and the number of dependents is around 4,000," he said. "The fishermen must never again be reduced to eking out their lives."

Palestinian refugees set eyes for the first time on their Palestine…(14)

" Scores of Palestinian refugees rushed to feast their eyes for the first time ever on Palestine, stretching beyond the barbed wire on Lebanon's border with "Israel".

They came from all the refugee camps around Lebanon, where they have been living in miserable conditions since 1948, to this tiny village a few miles away from the Mediterranean coast.

Men, women and children climbed up a hilltop covered with blue thistle and Spanish yellow broom, emanating sweet smells into the air, to reach the barbed wire Sunday.

On the other side, a few miles away, stood their cousins, aunts, uncles and even strangers who seemed so close to their hearts ... Arab "Israelis", or, more exactly, Palestinians who had remained in their lands when the Jewish state was created in 1948.

Between them, "Israeli" soldiers patrolled the area, with assault rifles on their shoulders.

The soldiers easily switch from Arabic to address Palestinians in Lebanon to Hebrew to tell those on the "Israeli" side not to approach the fence as they try to shake hands, clumsily grasp an arm, exchange phone numbers or offer tissues to relatives wanting to wipe sweat from their brows.

"I came to smell the perfume of Palestine. It smells so nice. I am here but my heart is on the other side," said Mahmud Abu Sheba, who was born and still lives in the refugee camp of Rashidieh, near the port city of Tyre.

Abu Shibba's parents were originally from Safad, in northern "Israel".

"Since Wednesday, it is the third time that I come here. I can't get enough of it," he said, although the scenery on both sides is nearly identical.

Actually, nobody knows why they all chose to come to this particular border village, and not any other place along the border.

Ahmad Rabah, 70, proudly wears the traditional Keffiyeh checkered headscarf, but hides his tears behind a tree.

For the first time in 50 years, he saw his brother who had called him Saturday to fix a date and a location for them to finally meet.

His wife, carrying the key of the home they were forced to leave behind decades ago in Palestine, asks about relatives still there before recalling her house near Acca.

Their 26-year-old nephew, who was born in the Ain al-Helweh refugee camp on the outskirts of the southern coastal city of Sidon, wears a medallion representing the map of Palestine.

"I am very happy. I finally saw my uncle whom I had long heard of," he said.

The Palestinian refugees are ecstatic about the "liberation of Lebanon" from the "Israeli" occupation Wednesday because they could not previously approach the borders to see the land they were forced to leave half a century ago.

As long as the "Israelis" or their allied militia controlled the southern region, it was out of question for them to reach the border.

On one side, three women were chatting, laughing and crying. Their physical resemblance was striking.

The one standing on the other side was introducing her husband, a Palestinian from "Israel", to her mother and her sister on the Lebanese side.

"I married him 25 years ago and I never had the occasion to introduce him to them," she said.

If most are happy, Tarek Abd, 28, is rather sad.

"It is terrible to live in a country like a refugee, without real rights, and to be here, separated from one's country by this little piece of iron which we can't cross over," said Abd.

Khawla Maqsud, 30, can't stop crying. She came from the Burj al-Shamali camp near Tyre to discover the country that she had long heard of.

She remains ecstatic, vowing that the Palestinians will do the same as the Lebanese and would surely return to Palestine.

But then, she starts crying again and throws a stone on the other side ... at nobody, just in a painful sign of helplessness.
