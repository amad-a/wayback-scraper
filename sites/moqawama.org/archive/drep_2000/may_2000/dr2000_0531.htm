Arafat under pressure to follow Hizbullah lead…(3)

By Phil Reeves in Jerusalem

31 May 2000

Almost a week after "Israel's" troops beat a hasty retreat from Lebanon, Yasser Arafat has publicly acknowledged he faces growing political pressure to emulate the success of Hizbullah's guerrilla forces.

"Hizbullah are being portrayed as heroes in the Arab world, while the Palestinians look like losers," he told an "Israeli" minister, Dalia Itzik, at a West Bank meeting.

More than three weeks ago, the bloody unrest of the so-called "Days of Rage" began in the West Bank and Gaza as a protest over prisoners in "Israeli" jails and swelled to reflect deep frustration among young Palestinians over the failure of Mr Arafat and his team to secure sufficient results in six years of peace negotiations.

"Israel's" Ma'ariv newspaper said Mr Arafat's comments were unusually forthright. "You have to understand what sort of pressure is being placed on me by the public," he told Ms Itzik, the "Israeli" environment minister. "My public perceives Hizbullah to be heroes who succeeded in getting the "Israeli" Army out of Lebanon and believe that is the route we should take as well.

"My situation is not simple. The pressure on me is coming from every direction. The Palestinian people want to see results in terms of the release of prisoners. In practice, they see nothing is moving."

This may be a ploy to bounce "Israel" into offering more concessions during talks being transferred back to the Middle East from Stockholm.

The message is clear: if Ehud Barak, "Israel's" premier, and his team continue to play hardball, the process could collapse, because the Palestinian public will not tolerate more long delays and feeble results.

The Jerusalem Post said the Palestinian leader has written to President Clinton complaining of the state of the talks – which he said were in an impasse – and asking him to push for progress. Mr Clinton meets Mr Barak in Germany tomorrow.

But, while the Palestinians are unlikely to return to an armed struggle to end the "Israeli" occupation of the lands, Mr Arafat has cause to be worried about hardening public opinion.

The television pictures from Lebanon of Hizbullah celebrating the end of "Israel's" 22 years of occupation, watched joyously by Palestinians in the occupied territories and "Israel's" 1.2 million Arabs, embarrassed him and his team.

His dilemma is deepened by Hizbullah's leader, Sheikh Hassan Nasrallah, who lost no time allying himself more closely to the Palestinian cause. He recently tried to take up a Palestinian cause célébre by telling an Arabic satellite television channel that the price for information on the missing "Israeli" airman Ron Arad would be the release of Palestinian as well as Lebanese prisoners in "Israel".

Negotiations with "Israel" have limped along – another deadline next month is sure to be missed – producing few results that satisfy Palestinian demands.

Mr Barak has proved a master at presenting himself on the international arena as a peacemaker, but Jewish settlement building has continued on the West Bank. He tends to characterise any offers to the Palestinians as "concessions". Mr Arafat has not found him easy to deal with, as another remark to Ms Itzik revealed. "Oh, how I miss Yitzhak Rabin. With him, a fight was a fight, and spat was a spat, a date was a date and a word was a word."

Life is hard for the aging Palestinian leader, who has had to crack down ruthlessly on internal dissent. From now on, it may be even tougher.


PM defends army’s absence in South
Critics ‘seem to insinuate that the government is not doing its job’…(4)

Prime Minister Salim Hoss  insisted on Tuesday that the government could restore its authority in the liberated areas without deploying the army.
“Those who are asking for the army to be sent to southern Lebanon should know that UN Security Council Resolutions 425 and 426 do not specifically mention the need for an army deployment,” Hoss said in a statement. “The resolutions call for the ‘return of the effective authority in the area.’ The government is the only authority to decide the effectiveness of its return to the area and how to do so.
“The Internal Security Forces have deployed personnel in the area since the first day of liberation. More personnel have been sent to the area and are doing their job alongside UN peacekeepers,” he added.
“The government continues to shoulder its responsibilities toward the liberated areas and will take any step that restores its full and uncompromising authority there.”
He said that “the current stage of Resolution 425 is still that of verifying the withdrawal.”
“That is what the UN admits to be doing now,” he added. “When this stage ends, the second stage, that of ‘restoring international peace and security,’ begins as stipulated by Article 2 of Resolution 425.”
Hoss said that in the third and final stage, “the peacekeeping force should help the government restore its effective authority to the area.
“Thus, sending the army now to the liberated areas does not follow Resolution 425 and may be considered an unjustified attempt to skip the first two stages.”
He argued that if the army had been deployed immediately after the withdrawal, there would be no need for UN peacekeepers. “Also,” he added, “it is practically and militarily difficult to station two military forces with separate leaderships in the same area, even if they are friendly, because their prerogatives and tasks would clash.”
Since the withdrawal was completed last Wednesday, many politicians have called for the army’s immediate deployment. “Such calls seem to insinuate that the government is not doing its job,” Hoss complained.
Meanwhile, UN envoy Terje Roed-Larsen visited the South to verify "Israel's" withdrawal, while army checkpoints on the former frontline only allowed residents to visit the liberated area.
Larsen met officers from UNIFIL, whose cartographers have been investigating whether "Israel" has in fact withdrawn to the international border. The envoy said that he had asked the Lebanese government to deploy its army in the South, but Beirut had insisted on waiting for confirmation of "Israel's" withdrawal.
“Lebanon wants to verify the border first,” he said. “It’s up to the government of Lebanon to deploy its army, but a political and military dialogue will go on.”
Larsen has delayed his departure for Syria until Wednesday and spent Tuesday touring the border. In the evening, he traveled to Naharia, "Israel", to meet Danny Yatom, Premier Ehud Barak’s security adviser, to follow-up on his meeting last week with Barak.
A UN spokesman said that Larsen and Yatom discussed progress on all issues related to "Israel's" withdrawal and had “another round of discussions on cartographic matters.”
Larsen also raised again the issue of Lebanese civilians detained in "Israel" and was informed that "Israel" was “actively looking into this matter.”
Larsen will meet Syrian Foreign Minister Farouk Sharaa as part of plans to draw Damascus into a process of ensuring secure post-pullout borders.

Riad’s story: 14 years of horror and pain in Khiam hellhole…(12)

The soft unassuming tones of Riad Kalakish contrast with his chilling tale of how "Israeli" officials at Khiam prison ordered the "Israeli"-allied guards to gas rebelling prisoners in their cells, killing two inmates.
When the detainees were gassed in 1989, Riad was three years into his imprisonment. The polite, neatly dressed 33-year-old was to spend a further 11 years locked up before his release last week along with the remaining detainees when the prison was stormed by hundreds of Khiam people.
During his 14 years in detention, Riad said he was repeatedly beaten, drenched in hot and cold water, tortured with electrical wires attached to his earlobes and genitals, starved and even sodomized with a wooden baton. The "Israeli" proxy guards carried out the brutal torture under orders from "Israeli" security personnel, members of the General Security Service (GSS), who ran the interrogations.
The appalling conditions under which the inmates were forced to live sparked three days of rioting in 1989. Riad said that acting on the instructions of their "Israeli" superiors, the guards flung gas canisters into the dank confines of the cell blocks to subdue the rioters.
“Everyone began screaming as the gas reached them, then there was a hush,” Riad said. He added that the detainees began to shake uncontrollably as the gas took effect.
The sudden silence from the cells apparently panicked the guards outside the blocks. They smashed the windows to allow the gas to escape.
Two detainees choked to death. Their bodies were taken to Marjeyoun hospital along with 12 inmates requiring treatment from the effects of the gas.
Riad said that Ahmad Fakhoury, who was the military head of the jail, told the prisoners that those harmed by the gas were suffering from allergies.
“Fakhoury told us that there was very little gas in the canisters. We told him that they had killed two of us and wanted to kill us all. Fakhoury denied this and said the two men were being treated in hospital.”
The prisoners taken to Marjeyoun hospital actually overheard the militia's guards blaming the doctors for the two deaths.
Riad’s ordeal began in 1986 when he was snatched from the village of Shaqra while visiting his grandfather. The 19-year-old was not a member of Hizbullah although his two brothers were resistance fighters.
He was taken to Khiam in the trunk of a car and was interrogated and tortured for two months. “They began by repeatedly jabbing a screwdriver into my neck, kicking me in the shins and punching me in the chest. Several ribs were broken,” Riad said.
The interrogators questioned him about his association with Hizbullah and whether he knew of any members of the resistance living in the village of Dibbine just north of Marjeyoun in the occupation zone.
Riad was given no food or water and would be shaken awake if he fell asleep.
“Assad Sayed from Qlaya was the worst torturer,” he said. “If he wanted to hit you, he would do it as hard as possible. He left the militia in 1992 and now lives in Sweden.”
The interrogations were conducted by "Israeli" GSS agents. "Israel" has always denied responsibility for the running of Khiam prison, arguing that the militia it trained, armed and paid had sole jurisdiction.
“The "Israelis" did the questioning, the militiamen did the hitting,” Riad said. He could remember the codenames of some of his "Israeli" interrogators: Abu Dib, Jackie, Abu Nabil and Elie. The "Israeli" GSS traditionally adopt Arab nicknames.
Sometimes a cotton bag was pulled over his head during interrogations. But Riad knew "Israelis" were present in the room from the Hebrew being spoken among themselves and the accented Arabic in which they addressed him.
“Elie was the worst "Israeli". A giant over 2 meters tall, he used to grab me, throw me against a wall and hit me,” Riad said.
He said that some prisoners, including himself, were sodomized with wooden batons.
He was tortured for three days with electricity. Wires were attached to his ear lobes and the tip of his penis. Shocks were delivered every 15 minutes using an old field telephone.
“They’d wind the handle and it would send painful jolts through my body,” Riad said. “The pain was so intense I began bleeding from my penis.”
He was confined to a room barely 1 meter square, wrists bound and tied to his ankles. He spent 15 days enduring the stifling heat of the cell.
The conditions the prisoners endured were pitiful. Each cell contained four detainees who were forced to sleep on the filthy cement floor, their shoes served as pillows. The toilet was a bucket that was only emptied every two days.
They were allowed a brief shower once every fortnight and exercise periods lasted around 10 minutes a week in an open-air courtyard.
To break the monotony, the prisoners would make home-made ink by soaking processed cheese wrappers in cups of water. They used the red ink to write verses from the Koran which they would then read to each other, attempting to draw comfort from the words.
In 1995, the International Committee for the Red Cross visited the prison. Conditions improved considerably. Showers, beds and sinks were fitted into all the cells and relatives were allowed to visit.
As the deadline for "Israel's" withdrawal approached, the militiamen began treating the detainees a little better.
“They were scared of what we would do to them after the "Israelis" had gone,” Riad said.
The dramatic storming of the prison last week in which all the detainees were released came as a total surprise.
“Looking through my cell window I could see the guards gathering in the center of the prison,” he recalled. “In the distance we could hear chants of ‘Allahu Ahkbar.’ The chants grew louder and louder.”
When the crowd reached the jail, they told the guards inside to open the gate or it would be broken in.
“The guards fired shots in the air and the crowd began chanting ‘Allahu Ahkbar’ again. Then we answered their cries with chants of our own.”
The collaborators asked to be allowed to leave the prison in peace. “The militia left, holding their guns but hysterical with fear,” Riad said. The crowd surged into the prison blocks and smashed open the cells.
“It was only when we were outside that I understood that we were really free. I felt as though I had been born again.”
For someone who has endured and survived such terrible treatment over such a long period of time, Riad harbors little resentment for his torturers.
If he met the "Israeli" interrogator Elie again, Riad said, “I would forgive him.”
“What happened to me is in the past. If Elie and the others want to become better people and stop doing these wicked things then God will help them. God will judge Elie, not me.”


‘Khiam cost me my baby’…(13)

The hardest moment of Mona Ashour’s life was when she had to tell her husband Adel that she couldn’t present him with his child because the beatings she suffered at the hands of her "Israeli"-allied captors caused her to miscarry.
In 1993 ­ just two days after Adel was imprisoned ­ the militia snatched her from her home in the then-occupied village of Dibbine and took her to the Khiam detention center. Her brother-in-law, Riad Kalakish, had already been inside Khiam for seven years. Both Adel and Riad remained detainees until last week’s storming of the prison.
Mona was two-and-a-half months pregnant with their first child. The first insults were the forced removal of her veil and the fondling of her body.
At first the harassment was psychological. “They threatened to remove my clothes and hang me outside in front of all the men,” Mona recalled. “Then they said they’d rape me while my husband watched.”
Terrified, she answered their questions. “My husband was a Hizbullah fighter and I told them what I knew,” she said. “But he never told me anything so there was nothing much I could tell.”
The "Israeli"-proxy interrogator was obviously not convinced. So for a whole day, Ashour was made to kneel with her hands tied behind her back facing a toilet. There she stayed and was forced to watch as militiamen relieved themselves.
“And on the way in and out, they threw either cold or hot water on me,” she said.
Meanwhile, she was deprived of sleep as interrogations continued. “The interrogator slept but I wasn’t allowed to,” she recalled. “I had to sit there watching him sleep, then wait for him to wake up and sip his coffee ever so slowly.”
Taking a break from questions, the interrogator would then masturbate in front of her.
“I kept thinking to myself,” she said, “that I was a married woman. What about the young girls who came through here?”
Then the physical harassment began. Slaps and beatings became the norm.
She lost her baby around the third day after a militia woman ordered her to clean some rooms and carry heavy items. Already dizzy from the beatings, Mona miscarried and fell unconscious. She was taken to Marjeyoun Hospital.
Back in prison, she was placed in a small cell with four others. The women quickly bonded. Once a week, they received a bucket of water to wash themselves and their clothes. “We had a system going among us that whoever was menstruating would get all the water,” she said.
They cut up their blankets to use as sanitary napkins ­ though these were later provided. “We always looked out for each other,” she said. “We found ways to cling together.”
When one of the women from nearby cells was taken away for interrogation ­ frequently during the night ­ the woman would find some way to signal to the others that she was being taken away.
“For example, she’d cough as she’d go by our door and we knew who it was,” Mona said.
And then they listened intently. If they heard her screaming they would scream back.
“One time a woman was taken to another room,” she said. “And there, a militiaman stripped her clothes off and his as well. Then he tried to rape her. She was strong and pushed him off. And she screamed loudly. We heard her and screamed back. This somehow scared the militiaman and he let her go.”
With Adel’ return, these memories are now behind her. It’s time to settle down to a new life ­ one in which children will hopefully play a part.

The former speaker of the Kuwaiti parliament, Ahmad Al-Saadoun stated, "the Zionists fled like rats. This is a clear message to all organizations that took billions not to liberate their land but to sign peace deals."

Moreover, at the end of a Kuwaiti parliamentary session, the following statement was made, "The logic of armed struggle and painful strikes will help retrieve Arab's rights and realize a just and comprehensive peace for the people in the region; for what has been taken by force shall be restored by force."

Kuwaiti Foreign Minister Al-Sheikh Subah Al-Ahmed Al-Subah announced that his government decided to offer Lebanon the amount of 20 million dollars in order to rebuild the areas that were previously under "Israeli" occupation.

The Saudi government expressed its gratification as to the "Israeli" withdrawal from South Lebanon and hailed the role of the Lebanese resistance.

The Saudi news agency quoted, "King Fahd's government congratulated the Lebanese government and people for the achievement that would not have been realized without the steadfastness of the brave Lebanese Resistance all through the abominable years of occupation."

King Fahd had previously sent a message to the Lebanese President Emile Lahoud congratulating him on the "Israeli" withdrawal.

Moreover, the Islamic Development Bank has agreed to lend Lebanon US$100 million to help finance reconstruction of south Lebanon after the withdrawal of "Israeli" occupation forces.

A statement from the bank said the money includes a US$10 million loan to help in the rebuilding of schools and hospitals.
