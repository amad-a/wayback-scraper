Hoss pleads case of detainees to ICRC (2)
Prime Minister Salim Hoss officially asked the International Committee of the Red Cross to delegate a team of inspectors to the Khiam detention center inside the Israeli-occupied zone on Thursday.
In a message to Henri Fournier, the ICRCs chief delegate here, Hoss asked for an inspection of the notorious conditions at the prison, a statement from his office said.
He also asked the ICRC to lobby international governments to pressure Israel to release the detainees from Khiam, which is run by the Israeli-allied militia, the statement added.
Hoss highlighted the case of Mohammed Sayyed Ali, who was arrested two years ago on suspicion of cooperating with the resistance, and who was reportedly tortured in prison.
At least 150 Lebanese, including several women and teenagers, are held in Khiam. The ICRC has not been able to visit the prison or organize visits by the families of detainees for more than two years.
In another message to Fournier, Hoss denounced the militia shelling of an ICRC position near Nabatieh on Sunday.
The attack destroyed a car belonging to Medecins Sans Frontiers and damaged two ICRC ambulances.

The only border dispute here is among Israelis (3)
Authority on Lebanons southern frontier refutes claims of mandate modifications.
Israels apparent confusion over the delineation of the border with Lebanon has resulted in a contradictory scenario for its planned withdrawal, whereby the troops would move back to the line they crossed in 1978, but retain key outposts just inside Lebanese territory.
On Thursday, Israels Haaretz newspaper quoted a senior diplomatic source as saying that the Israeli Army would pull back to the armistice line that existed between Israel and Lebanon until the Litani campaign in 1978 and all Israeli outposts on Lebanese soil would be dismantled.
The source then added that the border fence will remain in its present location even in places where it does not correspond with the international border and border outposts which jut out into Lebanese territory would be retained.
The decision to stage a full withdrawal to the 1978 border is believed to be based on advice received last week by the Israeli government, warning of continued resistance attacks if troops remained on Lebanese soil following a unilateral pullback.
The border crossed in 1978 by Israeli troops corresponded to the 1949 armistice line which in turn followed exactly the original frontier mapped out by the British and French mandatory authorities in 1922 and ratified a year later.
In the early 1980s, the Israelis pushed the border fence northward and westward in a few places a few hundred meters into Lebanon to annex the high ground along the frontier. It is these areas that the Israelis apparently intend to retain in the event of a unilateral withdrawal.
Israels ambiguous approach to the delineation of the border is exemplified by a four-volume file prepared by the Israeli Armys mapping unit which concludes that 60 percent of the frontier remains open to dispute. This is because ­ according to the file ­ the border was adjusted in places during the mandate period between 1923 and 1940 to resolve local issues such as links between fields and villages. The alterations were backed up in written documentation and approved by the League of Nations, Haaretz said.
Haaretz said that part of the strategic Ramim ridge between Adaisseh and Kfar Kila was not even discussed by the British and French. Furthermore, no agreement was reached on other stretches of the border.
However, Frederic Hof, an acknowledged expert on Israels borders with Lebanon and Syria, said that he was unaware of any boundary adjustments during the mandate period. The only alteration was the administrative transfer of the so-called Seven Villages from the French mandate of Lebanon to the British mandate of Palestine in 1924.
Even that adjustment was in full accordance with the 1922 final survey report by the British and French, Hof told The Daily Star.
He noted that the two mandatory authorities agreed in 1926 to some common-sense flexibility with respect to frontier matters ­ such as free passage for local inhabitants, cross-border water use, grazing, taxation and pilgrimages ­ without changing the boundary itself.
Hof added that the League of Nations in 1934 accepted the entire Palestine-Lebanon-Syria boundary as originally demarcated by the British and French mandates 12 years earlier.
The only section of the border that remained disputed between the Lebanese and Israelis was the final 5 kilometers in the east where the two parties disagreed on the interpretation of the original 1922 mandate document delineating the frontier.
That document recorded the exact delineation of the border in painstaking detail and was accompanied by a hand-drawn scale map rendering the path of the frontier.
Lebanons position on the border is that the 1949 armistice line ­ which follows the mandate border ­ is the legitimate international frontier. The UN ­ which helped broker the armistice between Lebanon and Israel 51 years ago ­ also believes the border follows the armistice line.
Hof suggested that the reason the Israeli file extended to four volumes was to contain the many unilateral Israeli actions toward the border ­ such as pushing the fence deeper into Lebanon.
I honestly believe that the assertion that 60 percent of the boundary is disputed would come as a genuine surprise to the Lebanese government, Hof said. I believe that much of what is disputed is disputed, at this point, among Israelis.
Certainly, by referring to the mandate document and the accompanying map, it is evident that the border along the Ramim ridge between Adaisseh and Kfar Kila was included in detail, contrary to the findings of the Israeli Armys file as reported by Haaretz.
Thence it follows the path from the col of Odeissah (Adaisseh), and passing 1,400 meters to the northwest and to north of Jebel Ariak, turns to the southeast at the junction of the paths leading to Jedeida of Merj Ayoun (Marjayoun), Khiam and Kaleia (Qlaya), the document states.
The report adds that the border runs along the southern side of the path from Adaisseh.
Today, the path is the main road between Adaisseh and Kfar Kila and the border fence is still on the southern side, conforming to the wishes of British and French mappers 78 years ago.
