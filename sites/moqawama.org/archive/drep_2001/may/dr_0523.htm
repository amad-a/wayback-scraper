Nasrallah urges more intifada…(1)

Hizbullah’s secretary-general urged the Palestinians Tuesday not to pin their hopes on Arab leaders or the United States in their quest for statehood, insisting that the intifada was the only option for ending the “Israeli” occupation.
Addressing a Loyalty to the Palestinians rally in Haret Hreik, Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah warned of attempts by Arab leaders to “delude the Palestinians” with promises of a better future in return for an end to the intifada ­ an indirect reference to the Jordanian-Egyptian bid to broker a cease-fire.
“Just like we in Lebanon did not rely on Arab support, our brethren in Palestine should not either,” he told thousands of supporters, who interrupted him repeatedly with chants of “Allahu Akbar.”
Nasrallah said that contrary to “Israeli” allegations, Hizbullah neither has ambitions in Palestine nor cells for attacking “Israel”, but that its role was restricted to supporting the Palestinians from a distance.
As for Hizbullah’s aspirations, he said: “I will be a moderate this evening. We do not want to throw the Jews into the sea; we simply want them to go back to the countries they came from.”

“Israel” corrects new violation of "blue line" with Lebanon…(3)

“ “Israel” on Tuesday removed a camera from the UN "blue line" between Lebanon and the Jewish state, correcting a new violation of the acting border between the countries.

In the presence of officers from the United Nations Interim Forces in Lebanon (UNIFIL), “Israeli” technicians, accompanied by armed troops, dismantled a camera attached to a pole, AFP reporters on both sides of the border said.

The action was taken "following a request by the United Nations," an “Israeli” army spokesman said.

The camera had been put up May 3 on the Lebanese side of the village of Ghajar, opposite the village of Wazzani and the river of the same name.

The pole and the cement blocks around it were removed with the help of a crane, and moved to the “Israeli” side, while map drawers from the Lebanese army watched the operation from a distance.

The United Nations had rejected a first complaint made by Lebanon as soon as this observation unit was put up. Beirut repeated its complaint and the UN then agreed it was a violation of some 10 metres (yards), according to the Lebanese cartographers.

On March 1, Ukrainian troops with UNIFIL eliminated another “Israeli” violation in the same sector, at the request of Lebanon.

They dismantled a cement wall erected some 300 metres (330 yards) inside Lebanese territory, which covered around 12 ditches dug by “Israel” in April beyond the "blue line."

The Alawite village of Ghajar is divided in two by the "blue line" and is on the border between Lebanon and the Syrian Golan Heights, occupied by “Israel” since 1967.

“Israel” has accepted that Lebanon assumes its sovereignty over the northern part of the village.

The residents of the village, most of whom obtained “Israeli” citizenship after the conquest of the Golan by “Israel”, are calling for the village to be returned to Syria.

They protested against the UN line dividing their village and succeeded in having no border fencing in the village itself.

The blue line was drawn to mark the line of “Israel’s” withdrawal from south Lebanon last May 24, which ended a 22-year occupation.

 

“Israeli” forces maintain surveillance over Shibaa Farms area…(4)

“ The “Israeli” army, on alert for a possible attack by Hizbullah, is maintaining a close watch on the disputed Shibaa Farms border region that it controls.

Tuesday morning, an “Israeli” surveillance plane overflew the area at medium altitude along a flank of Mount Hermon, which overlooks the region, witnesses said.

Lebanese police later reported that “Israeli” planes had violated Lebanese airspace for the ninth consecutive day Tuesday.

The planes went as far as the Iqlim at-Tuffah hills, an area controlled by Hizbullah 70 kilometres (45 miles) south-east of Beirut, they said, dropping flares along the way.

An observation balloon was also in place overnight, but later withdrawn.

On Monday, six fighter-bombers flew at low altitude over southern Lebanese border areas, shaking windows of homes in the city of Marjayoun, and dropping flares over other nearby regions, AFP correspondents said.

Lahoud affirms commitment to free remaining detainees…(5)

President Emile Lahoud affirmed Tuesday Lebanon’s commitment to resolving the issue of detainees held in “Israeli” jails, which he said was a priority in any discussions to achieve a just, comprehensive and permanent peace in the region.
Lahoud was addressing a delegation representing relatives of detainees in “Israeli” prisons, who visited him at Baabda on the first anniversary of the South’s liberation from “Israeli” occupation.
A presidential statement quoted Lahoud as saying that the release of the detainees was “equal in importance” to liberating the land from “Israeli” occupation, and unless they were set free, “we will continue to consider incomplete the achievement of liberation.”
The issue of releasing the detainees is a permanent item on the agenda of talks held with world leaders, Lahoud said. “We will continue to chase this matter with those concerned until the detainees and the abducted are set free.”
The president explained that contacts were continuing with the International Committee of the Red Cross and with other concerned parties to inquire about the detainees’ well-being, “despite “Israel’s” attempt to sever any communication between them and their families.”
Lahoud said military, economic, political and media pressure exerted on Lebanon would not change its position calling for liberating the Shibaa Farms, releasing the detainees, liberating the Golan Heights, and repatriating the Palestinians: “These four conditions are equal in importance and constitute an inseparable part of the principles of Lebanese policy.”
A speaker for the delegation earlier thanked Lahoud for the attention he was giving to the detainees issue, wishing he would maintain his efforts to ensure their release.

 

Hammoud scoffs at “Israeli” threats…(6)

Foreign Minister Mahmoud Hammoud dismissed on Tuesday the possibility of large-scale “Israeli” military strikes, saying the Jewish state knew it would not “remain shielded” from resistance and Syrian retaliation in the event of such an adventure.
Speaking to reporters at the Foreign Ministry, Hammoud said “Israel’s” threats against Lebanon and Syria “reflect its fear” of the two states, particularly on the eve of the first anniversary of liberation.
“It hasn’t forgotten its army’s defeat at the hands of the resistance movement, not in a war against another state,” he added. He said the Palestinians were in fact following the “Lebanese pattern of resistance” in their confrontation with the “Israelis”.
Hammoud endorsed proposals to dispatch a disengagement force to separate the Palestinians and “Israelis” with the consent of all parties concerned.
Referring to President Emile Lahoud’s forthcoming two-day visit to Paris, Hammoud said the trip affirmed the traditional ties between the two countries, France having “supported Lebanon politically and economically on various occasions.”
He added that talks during the May 28-29 visit would cover reconstruction projects and preparations for a second economic meeting to address the country’s ailing economy. Security issues will also be on the agenda since a significant portion of weapons in use are French-made.
Hammoud said the French expect successful outcomes on bilateral ties and dealing with developments of the Middle East crisis. The planned reduction of UNIFIL’s forces and plans for the Francophone summit scheduled for October in Beirut will also be discussed during the visit, he said.

Meanwhile, on the eve of the first anniversary of the liberation of the South and Western Bekaa, a government source assessed the “achievements” of the past year. The source said despite full withdrawal from territory occupied since 1978, the “Israeli” military establishment remains “alarmed,” fearing for their troops’ lives from resistance fighters.
“This dominant alarm has mobilized “Israeli” forces on the border with Lebanon to prevent prospective Hizbullah operations marking the first anniversary of liberation,” the source added.
UN and European efforts have so far failed to produce an agreement on exchanging 13 Lebanese detained by “Israel” ­ in violation of the Geneva Convention ­ for three “Israeli” soldiers and an intelligence officer captured by Hizbullah.
Hizbullah has continued its operations against “Israeli” military targets, although the UN concluded the Shibaa Farms were subject to the provisions of Security Council resolutions 242 and 338, not resolution 425, as Lebanon contends.
Other points outlined by the same source include differences between Lebanon and the UN on UNIFIL’s strength following plans to reduce troop numbers, and the deployment of the army on the border with “Israel”, a move rejected by Lebanon.

 

Expert techniques demonstrated at de-mining workshop…(7)

International experts demonstrated various de-mining techniques in the South Tuesday, on the last day of a workshop held to gather support for Lebanon’s campaign to clear some 130,000 land mines.
The field demonstration was part of the two-day International High-Level Workshop for De-mining Lebanon, which was jointly organized by the Lebanese government and the United Nations, and funded by Italy.
The demonstrations illustrated techniques by experts of the Lebanese and Syrian armies, and Ukrainian experts from UNIFIL, as well as Italian, British and US non-governmental organizations.
The techniques used by sappers varied from the use of trained dogs, manual detectors, and remote-control detonations of land mines.
The event included one display of equipment used by the teams in the de-mining process, and another showing unexploded “Israeli” devices found by the Lebanese Army after “Israel’s” withdrawal a year ago.
Defense Minister Khalil Hrawi, who was present during the demonstration, announced Monday a five-year plan to clear the country’s land mines, with the help of the United Nations and other international bodies and governments.
Local officials and representatives of various foreign embassies whose countries offered assistance in the de-mining process also attended the event.
Staffan de Mistura, UN secretary-general Kofi Annan’s envoy to southern Lebanon, said during Tuesday’s demonstration that an accelerated de-mining operation could rid Lebanon of all estimated mines in four years’ time.
“Today was a demonstration of the types of professional capacities and the type of equipment available around the world,” he said.
De Mistura also said that once the required funds were made available, the process could be speeded up.
“Once the first contracts are signed by the United Arab Emirates, it will be sufficient to bring in equipment and people within 45 days to start de-mining at the necessary pace,” he said, in reference to a $50 million donation by the UAE.
“I’m confident that with the UAE’s $50 million used properly and operationally, we can do a lot,” he added, “even some economic development.”
De Mistura said the de-mining operation would only require four years because the area involved was relatively small.
“The (border) line is 80 kilometers long,” he said. “We’re not talking about thousands of kilometers. The territory to be cleared is not as large as what we’ve been facing in other countries,” he said.
“And the number of mines is 130,000 at the maximum,” he continued, “which is a large number, but not as large as the five million we have seen in other countries.”
De Mistura added that Lebanon needed at least 1,000 personnel to complete the program, whereas it currently had only 200 Lebanese Army de-mining experts.
He added however that the most important factor in speeding up the de-mining process was “the political will,” saying that “there’s a real genuine concrete commitment by the Lebanese authorities.”

 

Northern “Israelis” still live in fear a year after Lebanon pullout…(8)

“ Like many of her neighbours living on the Lebanese border, Michal Nir fears a resumption of rocket fire from southern Lebanon, despite the relative calm that has followed the “Israeli” pullout from that area on May 24 last year.

"I can remember the Katyushas flying over my head from when I was only little, and I am afraid of them starting up again," she says in the Kfar Giladi kibbutz, located in what is known as "the Galilee panhandle" right next door to Lebanon, as she watches over her twins Tom and Lihi, who are nearly three.

In the distance are the slopes of Mount Hermon, which could become the "casus belli" of a new war between the Jewish state and its northern neighbours, Lebanon and Syria.

The head of the Lebanese Hizbullah, Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah said Sunday that "the resistance will continue ... until the liberation of the Shibaa Farms," an area of some 20 square kilometres (eight square miles) between Lebanon, Syria and “Israel”.

The same day, “Israeli” Defence (War) Minister warned Syria its troops based in Lebanon would be targets again if Hizbullah persisted in its attacks.

“Israel” hit a Syrian radar station in Lebanon on April 16, killing a Syrian soldier, two days after a Hizbullah attack on an “Israeli” position in the Shibaa Farms, killing one “Israeli” soldier and wounding two others.

In previous operations in the area Hizbullah has killed four “Israeli” soldiers and captured three.

"Har Dov (the Hebrew name for the Shibaa farms) is too close," says Michal Nir.

Her friend Miri Bergman, who works at a kindergarten and has two small children, shares her concerns. Having come from Rishon le Sion, near Tel Aviv, five years ago, she is particularly fearful of "the security situation."

"Since the withdrawal and up till yesterday, calm prevailed and I felt safer in the north than those dear to me that I had left behind in the centre (of the country), despite the intifada," she says, referring to the Palestinian uprising.

But "what happened Friday has put everything into question," she adds, alluding to a suicide martyrdom bomb in the “Israeli” coastal resort of Netanya and a retaliatory “Israeli” F-16 warplane strike on the West Bank, which between them left 18 dead.

The same feeling of worry can be felt all along the “Israeli”-Lebanese border, from the resort city of Nahariya up to Metulla on the border.

Nahariya ice cream seller Nili Fintzi bemoans the lack of “Israeli” tourists from within the country.

Toy salesman Roni Kitai says he is "paranoid after years of unrest."

"The state of relative calm, free of Katyusha rockets, can only be temporary as all that Nasrallah wants is to eat us raw."

Metulla, a compulsory crossing point up to a year ago for thousands of workers and pro-”Israeli” Lebanese militiamen, is now deserted.

Shalhevet Glick, a 52-year-old widow, has stayed in the cafe opened by her father 25 years ago. "The border is hermetically sealed and I have no other customers now apart from soldiers and rare visitors like you."

But the mayor of Kyriat Shmona, Haim Barbivay, is determined to remain upbeat.

"This year has been the best in the town's history. Silence and calm have prevailed and final year pupils have for once been able to succeed in their leaving exams," he says with pride, recalling earlier years where the failure rate was high as the pupils spent much of their school time in shelters.

Hizbullah will continue lighting until last inch is liberated…(12)

Addressing reporters during a press outing to meet resistance fighters in south Lebanon, Hajj Mustafa, a senior Hizbullah commander defined Hizbullah’s current theater of operations as running in a northeasterly direction from Moghr Shibaa, at the foot of the disputed territory, to the “Israeli” listening post on a mountain peak 2 kilometers southeast of Shibaa village.
“The government says it’s our land and we will resist and liberate it from Moghr Shibaa to the ‘radar’ (the “Israeli” listening post) at Shibaa,” Hajj Mustapha said, tapping a map of the Shibaa Farms area.
All 14 of the original Shibaa farmsteads are included within the area Hajj Mustapha marked as territory to be liberated. However, his boundaries excluded the village of Nkhaile, 1.5 kilometers west of Moghr Shibaa, which was occupied by “Israeli” troops during the June war of 1967 along with the Shibaa Farms and the Golan Heights.
Like the Shibaa Farms, Nkhaile was inhabited by Lebanese citizens but is considered by the United Nations to fall within “Israeli”-occupied Syria.
Because Nkhaile lies only 1 kilometer north of the “Israeli” border, Lebanon in 1947 transferred the village to the jurisdiction of the Syrian military for a 10-year period. The agreement elapsed 44 years ago and the village is, thus, considered to fall under Lebanese sovereignty.
The exclusion of Nkhaile from Hizbullah’s theater of operations offers a possible loophole for continued military activity against “Israel” in the event that the “Israeli” government decides to pull its troops out of the Shibaa Farms.
Asked to clarify whether Hizbullah intends to liberate Nkhaile, Hajj Mustafa said: “This is for (consideration) afterward.”
“Right now the conflict is within the set perimeters (Moghr Shibaa to Shibaa),” he said.
Hajj Mustafa refused to comment on Hizbullah’s future strategy if the Shibaa Farms are returned to Lebanon, but declared: “As long as there is an inch of land under occupation by the enemy and our detainees have not been released, we will continue our liberation efforts.”
The veteran Hizbullah commander, dressed in US military fatigues, beret and sporting sunglasses, was one of several resistance fighters who had emerged from the shadows to meet with a horde of journalists on a hill-top near the Shibaa Farms.
Conforming to Hizbullah’s policy of strict secrecy, the windows of the buses ferrying all 137 reporters to the front line were blacked out until arrival. But the Shibaa Farms front line is a comparatively small area and it was not difficult to ascertain that Hizbullah had transported the reporters to a brush-covered hill between Hibbarieh and Rashaya Fawqa.
The fighters, their faces smothered in green and black camouflage paint, were scattered in several small sandbagged positions along a path winding up the hill.
With an “Israeli” pilotless reconnaissance plane buzzing unseen overhead in the blue sky, the fighters displayed their weapons: roadside bombs consisting of explosive charges hidden inside fiberglass “rocks,” anti-tank mines, Sagger anti-tank missiles, rocket-propelled grenades and Katyusha rockets ready for firing on the back of a small truck.
“My biggest wishes are, first, to achieve victory and, secondly, to achieve martyrdom,” said Abu Haidar, 34, like the others, giving only his nom de guerre.
Al-Kayyed, 28, a veteran fighter of 16 years who lost his arm and two fingers in an operation against the Sojod outpost in 1990, said that he felt “extraordinary happiness” to be marking the first anniversary of “Israel’s” troop withdrawal from the south.
“But even after a year, there is still a black spot within me when I come to the South and smell the land and remember all those people who are not here today,” he said. “No matter what the difficulties, though, we will keep on struggling.”

 

Peace elusive on “Israel”-Lebanon border…(13)

“ “Israel’s” war with Lebanon ended a year ago, but peace has not yet begun.
      When “Israel” abandoned its military occupation of south Lebanon last May 24, then-Prime Minister Ehud Barak spoke of the Jewish state and its northern neighbor extending hands of peace. That now seems a vain hope.

      "When we decided to pull out from Lebanon, we expected to be finished . . . there," said “Israeli” army spokesman Olivier Rafowicz, speaking inside a fortified army post at the Fatma Gate, a former border crossing. "But we have to keep a good eye on our northern border."
      “Israeli” soldiers next to him train their machine guns and binoculars across the frontier, to where tattered Hizbullah flags flap in the wind.
      The Hizbullah guerrilla group, which led the Lebanese resistance to the 22-year occupation, controls the border area in southern Lebanon and shootings are common.
      “Israel” is still counting its dead and missing — three “Israeli” soldiers have been killed along the northern border since the withdrawal, and three have been kidnapped.
      Thousands of refugees, former soldiers in “Israel’s” proxy south Lebanese militia and families, are still in “Israel”.
      “Israel’s” northern residents keep bomb shelters stocked up. Hizbullah has fired mortars and anti-tank missiles from Lebanon into “Israel”, while “Israeli” forces have fired at Hizbullah from helicopters and tanks.
      Despite the U.N. stamp of approval, Hizbullah does not recognize “Israel’s” withdrawal as complete, saying “Israeli” troops are still occupying a border area it says is Lebanese and calls Shibaa Farms. “Israel” calls it the Har Dov area.

      Yossi Peled, a former “Israeli” army commander in Lebanon, mulls the options as he sits with a Lebanese refugee family in northern “Israel” one year after the withdrawal.
      "We need to pray for peace and believe that one day it will happen," he says. "But this is an unpredictable area."

“Israel’s” Lebanon lesson…(14)

The recent rocket attacks coming out of Lebanon and directed against “Israeli” troops, followed by a tough “Israeli” response, serve as a poignant reminder that “Israel’s” withdrawal from Lebanon a year ago tomorrow did not exactly live up to its expectations.

It may be useful to recall just how high those expectations were. By a nearly four-to-one margin, “Israelis” endorsed the retreat from Lebanon as an excellent strategic move.

On the left, Internal Security minister Shlomo Ben-Ami thought that Syria's president was "very stressed by “Israel’s” decision to withdraw from Lebanon." On the right, foreign minister David Levy declared that the pullout would weaken Syria's position.

Others speculated further. Dan Margalit of Ha'aretz forecast that it would "spur Syria to come back to the negotiating table." Novelist Amos Oz boldly predicted about Lebanon's most aggressively anti-”Israel” organization: "The minute we leave south Lebanon we will have to erase the word Hizbullah from our vocabulary." A year later, how do things look?

The idea that an “Israeli” retreat would scare Damascus into restarting negotiations turns out to be as silly as it sounds. President Hafez Assad went to his grave without returning to the bargaining table and his son Bashar has so far shown no willingness to talk.

The expectation that “Israel” would enjoy a peaceable northern border proved similarly misguided. Hizbullah concocted a new claim to a piece of “Israeli”-held land (the Shaba Farms) to justify continued hostilities. No longer restrained by “Israel’s” security zone in Lebanon, it threatens to use Katyusha rockets against “Israel” proper, prompting an alert as far away as “Israel’s” third-largest city, Haifa. Hizbullah has already attacked “Israel” seven times, attempted many infiltrations, abducted three “Israel” soldiers and killed two others. In response, “Israel’s” government has deployed helicopter gunships and attacked a Syrian radar site, killing three Syrian soldiers.

In brief, "Hizbullah" has hardly been erased from the “Israeli” vocabulary.

BUT THE greatest consequence of the “Israeli” retreat was felt among the Palestinians. That impact is partly practical, with Hizbullah providing instruction and arms to the Palestinian Authority. For example, Hizbullah reached an agreement with the PA "to train fighters and provide weapons against tanks and aircraft," reports the Middle East Newsline.

Palestinians took up Hizbullah's distinctive tactics and tools – suicide martyrdom bombings on the one hand, roadside bombs detonated by mobile phones on the other. They even adopted the Hizbullah technique of filming themselves carrying out attacks on “Israelis”, then making the film available to the Arab and Muslim media.

The impact is also psychological. Palestinians watched Hizbullah imposes every last one of its demands on “Israel”, without having to sit around a table with “Israeli” diplomats; this served as an object lesson.

Palestinians concluded that if they used enough violence, they too could get all they wanted from “Israel” without having to compromise. This "Lebanonization" of the Palestinians has had major consequences.

Prime Minister Ariel Sharon draws a connection between the “Israeli” retreat from Lebanon and "what happened later on" with the Palestinians. The head of “Israel’s” former Lebanese allied force puts it more strongly; “Israel’s” every concession to Hizbullah, he says, has been "very costly" for it in dealing with the Palestinians.

Specifically, Hizbullah's success first inspired the Palestinians to turn down even the amazingly generous terms that prime minister Ehud Barak subsequently offered them, confident that they could do better on the battlefield. It prompted the Palestinians to abandon the bargaining table and revert to violence against “Israel”.

It helps account for the escalation in that violence, which started with rocks and now includes long-distance mortar shellings.

The great majority of “Israelis” a year ago lived in the sweet delusion that unilateral concessions to neighbors would eventually win acceptance and quiet. After eight months of Palestinian violence - partly attributable to “Israel’s” withdrawal under fire from Lebanon - the hollowness of this hope is becoming increasingly apparent.

As they shudder back to reality, “Israelis” can console themselves with the knowledge that, by abandoning their Lebanon delusion, however painful that process is, they are taking the necessary first step toward dealing with today's crisis. The second step will be to understand that acceptance by neighbors will result not from “Israel’s” making unilateral concessions, but from its being respected and feared.

Debate goes on over Lebanon withdrawal…(15)

Effi Eitam yesterday recalled a conversation last year with Ehud Barak. "I told him 'it's not that you're taking the IDF out of Lebanon, you're bringing Lebanon into “Israel’s” territory'." Fein was then a brigadier general in active service, the commander of the Lebanon battalion. Barak was prime minister and defense (war) minister and despite warnings by Fein and other commanders, he ordered the IDF's withdrawal from Lebanon. The move won widespread public support spanning the political spectrum from left to right.
A year after the withdrawal from Lebanon, it appears to some that the consensus surrounding it has fallen victim to the Intifada that broke out four months later. In the difficult situation of doubt and suspicion fostered by the Intifada, there is stiff competition between those who claim there is a direct connection between the withdrawal and the Palestinian violence, and those who reject the argument. Each side derives a different lesson from the withdrawal, depending on their overall political outlook. Indeed, it seems that more than they are arguing over public opinion, each side is trying to shape the collective memory of the historic withdrawal according to its ideology.
Fein, known for his national-religious outlook, had no doubt that his harsh predictions would materialize exactly as he said and that is what happened: the Lebanon model - "Lebanonization" - spread across the land. "The consortium of terror" that was very successful in Lebanon is now emptying roads and malls and sparking a feeling of fear, he says. For him, the root of the problem is not in the actual withdrawal, but in a series of wars that led to the complete disintegration of “Israel’s” deterrence capability - from the Yom Kippur War, the Gulf War and the Lebanon War to the withdrawal.
"The army had an objective in Lebanon and it fought to achieve it, but it had to leave under pressure from the enemy which forced it to evacuate the area without an agreement, without a decision. This left a huge hole in our deterrent ability. The immediate result is what we see today in Judea, Samaria and Gaza," says Eitam. He believes “Israel” is very close to an all-out war. "Arafat plans to spin the area into a regional conflict. Arafat today is today the poor man's Saddam Hussein," he says. The U.S. sought to undermine Saddam's regime and eliminate him personally and so, too, “Israel” has to dismantle the Palestinian Authority (PA).
"People ask me if we should get rid of Arafat personally. It's unimportant to me what happens to the man. But if “Israel” doesn't dismantle the consortium of terror, the alternative is to dismantle the state of “Israel”. Only by dismantling the PA will “Israel” be able to restore its deterrent capability." Without doing that, says Eitam, “Israel” cannot fulfill the peace agreements it already has with some Arab countries and obtain new ones. "If there is an IDF, it should please show its face immediately," he concludes.
This is the context in which the Lebanon withdrawal should be seen, Eitam says, "and not by counting how many days have passed without them shooting on the north. True, we spared the lives of 10 soldiers in Lebanon, but we allowed the killing of many dozens of “Israeli” civilians and children and the undermining of law and order in “Israel”."

Bruria Sharon is one of those very people who have been counting the quiet days in the north since the withdrawal, for which she campaigned as a member of the Four Mothers group.
Every quiet day and every soldier saved she considers net profit. Comments like those of Eitam seem idiotic to her. "Even when we were deep inside Lebanon, there was an Intifada," she recalls. Many months before the second Intifada, the IDF was already warning about it, as did Barak and the Likud when Arik Sharon visited the Temple Mount. She does admit "undoubtedly" that the withdrawal encouraged the Palestinians to believe that it is possible to defeat “Israel” and expel it from occupied territories.
She believes that those, like Eitam, who draw a connection between the Lebanon withdrawal and the Intifada are incapable of dealing with the fact that their predictions regarding the outbreak of war along the northern border did not materialize.
"There is a part of the “Israeli” public for whom any withdrawal is a red flag and they are the foremost advocates of the idea that the Lebanon withdrawal caused the Intifada," she says, adding that the group has a name: "Gush Emunim."
Yossi Peled, a former OC Northern Command, opposed a unilateral withdrawal, but says he understands there was no alternative, because staying in Lebanon "tore apart the nation.
"I thought Barak had to try it so that he could look the soldiers' mothers in the eyes and say he tried," says the ex-general, who was Barak's point man for negotiations with the Syrians.
Peled thinks "every politician who is perplexed and confused because they have no explanation or solution for what's happening now with the Palestinians, clings to the connection between it and the withdrawal. It's a cynical use of the withdrawal and it is also stupid.
"One is a fight between two nations over the same strip of land and the other isn't," he concludes.
MK Michael Eitan was among the first to propose a unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon. During the Netanyahu government, he established the Kochav Ya'ir circle together with the dovish Yossi Beilin and the hawkish Gideon Ezra. Like Bruria Sharon, he also thought that those who linked the withdrawal and the Intifada were the ones who opposed a withdrawal from Lebanon all along, "instead of thinking what might have happened if we were now facing two fronts and instead of admitting that we spared the lives of many dozens of soldiers and hundreds of enemies who we would have had to kill there."
He also has an answer to Gush Emunim's arguments. "Our grip on Judea and Samaria weakened because we opened a second front in Lebanon, when we had no idea how to deal with the problems in Judea and Samaria," says Eitan, relating that he was among the few Likud members who as early as the 1980s argued that “Israel’s” presence in Lebanon had this effect.
Eitan does accept the arguments that the Palestinians were encouraged by the Lebanon withdrawal and that “Israel’s” deterrent capability was damaged it. But he believes the price was worth it, not only because the number of human lives that were saved, but also because “Israel” is now located along on an international border from which it is easier to defend its citizens, and it improved its international standing as a result of this move to this border.
But he also learns an interesting lesson from the unilateral withdrawal: "Some people say if a unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon proved itself, why not a unilateral withdrawal from Judea, Samaria and Gaza. I don't rule out considering such a move - unilaterally establishing a border between “Israel” and Judea, Samaria and Gaza."
But Eitan also cautions that no comparisons can be drawn between such a possibility and the Lebanon withdrawal because the situation in the territories is far more complicated and there is no international border to move to and on top of that, the problem is not only a territorial one, but also a demographic one.
Yossi Sarid is the complete political opposite of the ostensibly rightist Eitan - a leftist who opposed the withdrawal. He does believe the unilateral withdrawal "added motivation in the territories and what happens there may increase motivation in the north."
But he also belongs to the same small camp now willing to acknowledge, with very limited responsibility, that their forecasts may have missed the mark. He predicted very gloomy scenarios - the IDF will have to reenter Lebanon, this time in far worse circumstances than those it had before its departure.
"I'm very glad that for now the predictions didn't materialize and relatively speaking, it's quiet," he says, "but the test of Lebanon still lies ahead. We're in a transition period where we can't determine if the withdrawal paid off or not."
The feeling that this may not be a closed book also colors the positive memory of the withdrawal as taking part within the context of a national consensus. In addition to the uncertainty, there is confusion - after Barak decided to withdraw from Lebanon and received widespread support for it, the person most responsible for the controversial invasion of Lebanon 19 years ago, Ariel Sharon, thrashed him in the elections.
“Israel” has actually not begun the soul searching necessitated by its stay in Lebanon - a stay which in hindsight, and not only thus, seems incomprehensible to many - and it is already being led by the man who brought it in or as some may say, "entangled" it there.
Nevertheless, Yossi Beilin is convinced that history will judge the exit from Lebanon in the correct light, as a move that long overdue. "Only by studying history will future generations learn that staying there was superfluous and stemmed from only one thing, fear of leaving," he says.

 

Villagers have been kind to local hero…(16)

Residents of a small southern village have welcomed back their hero in their own way.
A year after his release from the Khiam detention center, Riad Kalakish, 34, awaits the construction of his new house on a recently acquired piece of land ­ all donated by Shakra villagers.
A few months after his release, a village school teacher approached Kalakish and took him to a vast piece of empty land. “This,” he told Kalakish, “is yours. Choose as much land as you want and consider it your very own.”
Kalakish was dumbstruck. Villagers were well aware the teacher was having trouble making ends meet and was depending on selling his land for income. But the teacher wouldn’t take no for an answer and gave 1,160 square meters to Kalakish ­ enough to build a house for himself and his brother, also a former Khiam detainee. “This is the least I could do for you after all the suffering you’ve been through,” the teacher told Kalakish.
Kalakish immediately consulted an architect but was told that his $10,000 would barely cover the expense of foundations for a house. “But don’t worry,” said the architect. “You’re our hero, and I will build you a house.”
Construction began within the next few weeks. Upon hearing of the project, an electrician provided the wiring for free. The architect who had waived his fee also managed to collect some donations from various villagers.
“I do not know who has been donating the money,” said Kalakish. “When I ask, they tell me do not worry about a thing. I am just so grateful.”
An even bigger gift awaited him when he left Khiam: his fiancee had waited for him for 14 years: “I got many marriage proposals while Riad was in prison,” said Nada, who is now his wife. “But I refused them all. The only man I ever wanted was Riad.”
The couple met when they were only 14 and immediately fell in love. “I told her then I loved her and asked her to wait for me until I grew up,” said Kalakish. “She said that she would and promised to remain loyal and faithful to me.” Five years later, the couple got engaged and began to plan their wedding.
It was during that time that Kalakish, then 19, visited his grandparents in Shakra. While both his brothers were resistant fighters, Kalakish was not. Nevertheless, he was snatched from his grandparents’ house and taken to Khiam. He remained there the next 14 years.
For the first nine years, Nada received no news of her fiance. It wasn’t until 1995 that she was occasionally allowed to see him across the fence. Again, she renewed her promise to wait.
Six months ago, the couple finally were married, and are now living in a rented apartment until their new house is completed. Their dreams are similar to all newlyweds: to settle down and have a family. “And put the past behind us,” said Kalakish.
But the past has a way of creeping up unnoticed. The severe torture endured for 14 years has taken its toll. Once a house painter, Kalakish can no longer do any physical work: “I have several slipped discs, and they cause me so much pain,” he said. “I tried working this year on several things. But I couldn’t continue. The pain was so severe.”
Kalakish is hoping the government will provide some promised employment, “a job that doesn’t require physical work from me.” Until then, he receives monthly assistance of $400 from Hizbullah while Nada continues to work as a pre-school teacher.
Another struggle is trying to adjust to a new world filled with technology. “A lot happened during those 14 years,” he said. “Everyone has a television with a remote control as well as computers. There’s so much to learn. I’m like a reborn man.”
He also finds himself grateful for even the smallest luxuries: “Everything seems too wonderful now,” he said. “An ugly sofa to somebody else, for example, seems beautiful to me.”
But always, the Khiam experience lurks in the back of his mind: “I try to look at it positively,” he said. “If I could endure that, then I could survive anything in life. I’m proud that I was able to survive. When I feel low, I draw from that strength.”
During his years in detention, Kalakish said he was repeatedly beaten, drenched in hot and cold water, tortured with electrical wires attached to his earlobes and genitals, starved, and sodomized with a wooden baton. The SLA** guards carried out the torture under the orders of the “Israeli” Army, he said.
The food provided was often rotten and meager. Several inmates in one cell were forced to use one bucket to relieve themselves that was emptied every two days. They were allowed a brief shower once every two weeks. The detainees slept on the hard cement floor and used their shoes as pillows.
The pitiful conditions remained until 1995 when the International Committee for Red Cross was finally allowed to visit the facility. After that, conditions improved considerably. Showers, beds and sinks were fitted into the cells, and relatives were allowed to see prisoners from outside the fence.
“In a way it became a familiar world,” said Kalakish. “I went back to Khiam prison after it was liberated and saw my bed and my stuff. I wanted to take some things but somehow they belonged there.”
The only belongings he did take with him when he was released was some needlework he had created with material provided by the Red Cross.
The work now hangs on the wall of the couple’s apartment.
“It doesn’t look like much here, does it?” he said. “But it seemed like the most beautiful thing in that cell.”
As for his torturers, Kalakish has one wish: to meet them one day and remind them of the pain they had inflicted on him.
“And then to forgive them,” he added.

 

Remembering men who sacrificed for liberation  
Zeina Mobassaleh reports on the fate of those still languishing in “Israeli” prisons after being captured while fighting to liberate the South…(17)

A year ago Wednesday, residents of the South broke into the notorious Khiam detention center, severing locks and kicking in doors to free their loved ones. It was by far one of the most emotional moments of the liberation, but the anniversary cannot be marked without remembering the prisoners who are still held in “Israel”.
Eleven men who fought against the occupation remain in “Israeli” prisons, despite condemnation from international human-rights groups.
Some are being held as bargaining chips for the return of “Israeli” pilot Ron Arad, who was shot down over south Lebanon during the “Israeli” occupation. Many have reported being severely tortured and beaten, even raped, by their guards.
Fadi Jazzar, of Palestinian origin, was born in Beirut and is the second-youngest child in a family of eight boys. He was captured in 1991 at the age of 20 while carrying out a guerrilla operation against the town of Zaarit in northern “Israel” opposite Marwahine in south Lebanon.
The operation conducted an attack against an “Israeli” patrol. While two of the fighters were killed immediately, Jazzar hid in a deserted house in nearby woods until he was wounded two days later when “Israeli” troops strafed the area with machine-gun fire.
“He conducted the operation as an objection to the Madrid Peace Conference because it sold out Palestine and was a surrender and an acceptance of the Zionist entity’s occupation,” his brother Khaled said.
Jazzar was denied medical treatment in Nafah Prison by the “Israeli” authorities and was threatened with solitary confinement if he so much as said “ouch,” said Khaled.
It was only because his fellow prisoners smuggled antibiotics to him that his injuries healed.
“For a while we thought that he was paralyzed on one side because he always sent us pictures of him sitting down. But when some fellow detainees came back, we found out that he was fine,” said Khaled.
Jazzar is serving a 25-year sentence in Nafha Prison.
Sheikh Abdel-Karim Obeid has been detained in “Israel” for the last 12 years after being kidnapped from his home in Jibsheet during an “Israeli” commando operation.
His youngest of five children, Majahida was only three months old when it happened, but she is able to recount the story clearly.
“He was captured because he used to train people in the resistance and was getting in the way of the “Israelis”,” she said. “They just got bothered by him and took him away.”
According to Majahida, the “Israelis” approached the home from the nearby valley and blasted the door using a concussion grenade. It was the middle of the night and she was asleep next to him when they came in and locked everyone in their rooms.
Obeid, a leader in Hizbullah, tried to escape from the balcony but discovered that the whole house was surrounded. He was tied up, drugged and whisked away in a getaway car.
The sheikh originally obtained a degree in architecture from the Lebanese University in 1977, but was teaching religion in the Mustafa school at the time of his arrest.
The children have all grown to love their father through stories they hear about him, even though the first time most of them remember seeing him was on television six months ago during his trial in “Israel”.
One of the few letters that has reached his family through the International Committee of the Red Cross asked his sons to kiss the hands of those who captured the “Israeli” soldiers last October because they will bring him his freedom.
“The years used to pass by quickly with him gone,” said Majahida. “But we feel these few months more than all the others before because now we’re just waiting.”
Obeid is being held at Sarafand Prison in Tel Aviv.

Mustafa Dirani was kidnapped on May 21, 1994 from his home in Qsarnaba in the Bekaa in a huge “Israeli” commando operation.
It was the middle of the night during Eid al-Adha when the doors to the house were suddenly broken down and more than 70 “Israeli” commandos surrounded the area.
His wife and one of his five children were interrogated in one room while he was tied up in another. The 43-year-old resistance fighter was then hustled into a car and taken away.
Dirani had finished two years of his law degree before he joined the Lebanese Army in 1970. When the war began, he joined Amal and became head of security. Although he broke away from the group, he continued to support the resistance.
He tried several times to finish his university degree, but was deterred for “security reasons. But he wanted to become a lawyer and defend people’s rights,” said his wife Zeinab. “He ended up defending the land and people in a different way.”
With education high on his list of priorities in life, Dirani encouraged his wife to pursue a university degree in English literature.
“He’s very aware and wise and respects women and the role they could play,” said Zeinab. “He had a love of education and always told the children that he wanted them to go to college.”
Dirani, who has filed a lawsuit in “Israel” accusing his guards of severe torture and rape, has spent the last seven years in solitary confinement in Sarafand Prison. The only news his family receives about him comes from the media. “Israeli” authorities have prohibited the exchange of any letters, even through the route of the International Committee for the Red Cross.
“When the three “Israeli” soldiers were taken, not a country was left that didn’t lobby for their release. We’ve been shouting for seven years and no one’s listened,” Zeinab complained. “Don’t we have the right to defend our land when it’s being attacked?”

Ali Balhass, a resident of the front-line village of Siddiqine, lived most of his life enduring regular “Israeli” shelling.
He was 24 years old when he was detained in 1992 while carrying out an attack near Yater in south Lebanon.
Balhass graduated from high school before becoming a fighter with Hizbullah.
“But we didn’t know about his operations, because as usual, all of it is secretive,” said his younger brother Hussein.
According to Hussein, Balhass was a strong student who was always at the top of his class.
“He was exemplary in his way of life and always treated people with kindness and helped those in need,” he said.
Ali is serving a life sentence in Nafha Prison. 
Youssef Wazni from Tibnin in Bint Jbeil was born in 1960.
He was abducted in 1988 and is serving a 25-year sentence in Nafha Prison. He was never married. Family members could not be reached to provide more information for this story.
Jawad Kasfi was born in 1962 in the village of Sultaniyeh. He was abducted in 1988. Kasfi was married and had one child. He is serving a 20-year sentence in Nafha Prison. Family members did not wish to be interviewed for this story.
Thirty-year-old Hassan Ankoni was defending his village of Maidoun near Meshghara against “Israeli” soldiers in the former occupied south when he was detained in 1988.
While 18 other Hizbullah fighters were killed, he sustained a foot injury during the fighting, and was captured and taken to “Israel”.
Because Ankoni never received proper medical treatment for his foot injury, he has difficulty walking, according to his wife Leila.
Leila remarked on his dedication to his children.
“He used to take his (then-3-year-old) son everywhere with him and show him “Israeli” positions and teach him about Jihad (holy war) and patience,” said Leila. “He dreamed of simply raising his children in a peaceful environment, but “Israel” came and destroyed those dreams. His daughter doesn’t even know him.”
Although Leila has been in a regular correspondence with her husband with International Committee for the Red Cross, she complains that it has been 14 years since she has seen him. “I live on this hope that I will see him again. It’s what keeps me going.”
Ankoni has four years left of the 18-year sentence that he is serving in Nafha Prison.
Mustafa Hammoud, born in 1970, was detained in 1988.
He was taken from his home in Marakaba, which was under “Israeli” occupation at the time, when he was accused of leading a resistance operation earlier that day.
Hammoud is serving a 20-year sentence in Nafha Prison.
Mohammed Birzawi was born in 1961 and is from Baalbek. He was detained in 1987 while carrying out an operation in the western Bekaa in the occupation zone. Birzawi is serving a 17-year sentence in Ashkelon Prison. He was never married. Family members declined to be interviewed.
Samir Qantar, from from Abay in Aley, is the longest-detained Lebanese held in “Israel”. Imprisoned at 17, the 41-year-old has spent more than half his life in prison.
He was captured in 1979 when he landed in a dinghy in northern “Israel” while trying to kidnap settlers for future prisoner exchanges in what became known as the Nahariya Operation. Two of the fighters were killed, and two others were wounded and captured.
Qantar was sentenced to 542 years ­ 99 years for each of the five “Israelis” killed during his operation and another 47 for hitting an “Israeli” officer during interrogation.
He has since been moved to Nafha Prison, which has become known as the “prison of death.”
Qantar joined the resistance independent of any political party but rather as a Lebanese fighting for the Palestinians. He wanted to send a message to Lebanon, which had just begun its civil war, that sectarianism should not split them and that everyone should sympathize with the Palestinian cause, according to his younger brother, Bassam.
“He wrote us once something that embodies his personality,” said Bassam. “He wrote: ‘Human will is more than we think or imagine, and my own experience proves it.’”
Anwar Yassin from Dalafa in the former occupied zone was only 18 when he was detained by “Israeli” soldiers in 1987. He was participating at the time in a 15-person resistance operation in Mount Hermon that left him with an arm injury.
It was years of living under occupation that compelled him to join the resistance.
“He was not the type who liked violence but participated in operations from a humanist point of view,” explained his older brother Haider. “He felt like a doctor who performs surgery to get rid of an illness. This was just a different kind of operation.”
Yassin was starting his second year of computer programming at an institute in Beirut when he was detained. His brother described him as the quiet and calm member of the family.
“Everyone was surprised when they heard he was taken. No one knew he was in the resistance.”
Yassin is serving a 30-year sentence in “Israel’s” Eyalon Prison.
