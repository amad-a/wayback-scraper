 Lawyers renew call to sue “Israel”…(1)

Over 1,500 Arab lawyers and political activists visited the South on Friday and renewed a pledge to sue “Israel” at the International Court of Justice for its aggressions in Lebanon.
“Arab countries that suffered “Israeli” aggression must file lawsuits, seek financial compensation and make it stop its aggressions,” Beirut Bar Association president   Michel Lian said during a visit to the former prison camp in Khiam.
The lawyers are attending a four-day conference in Beirut organized by the Federation of Arab Lawyers to support the Lebanese people and the Palestinian intifada.
Former detainee Ali Ksheish briefed the group on the methods of torture “Israel” used against the prisoners.
The group also visited the Lebanese-”Israeli” border, and stopped at the Fatima Gate where they looked at “Israel’s” border posts and reinforcements.

 ‘Shibaa close to exploding’ as collaborators men add to volatile mix…(2)

Shibaa, the sprawling mountain village on the slopes of the Mount Hermon foothills, epitomizes the seething tensions found throughout the Muslim border villages.
The village is a potent mix of political parties, rival families and hostility toward former militiamen ­ all underpinned by unemployment and economic disadvantage.
A resident of Shibaa, who requested anonymity, said the mood in Shibaa was “very bad” and the village was set to “explode.”
“Added to the political mix are the returning militiamen. The people here are very angry at the (short prison) sentences. A year ago, the militiamen were walking the streets like kings, treating everyone badly. It will take a long time for the wounds to heal. You can’t just drink a cup of coffee together and then act as if nothing ever happened.”
A returning militiaman from the Dali family was badly beaten up two weeks ago in Shibaa. The spark for the assault was the perception that the former militiamen was “driving his car arrogantly fast.”
“Collaborators should be more humble,” the resident said. “He would have been killed if some of us hadn’t intervened.”
“There is no work here,” he added. “Young people have nothing to do but cause trouble. An argument between two people over the right of way can easily erupt into a family feud, drawing in half the village.”

Wounds of occupation are still fresh for many who find it hard to bury the hatchet…(3)

Tensions are building in the villages along the border with “Israel” between former members of the South Lebanon Army* freshly released from prison and residents who believe that the militiamen were treated too leniently by the courts.
Isolated incidents of violence against those accused of collaborating with “Israel” are being aggravated by unemployment in the area, traditional rivalries between leading families, and tensions between political parties.
The problem has not yet reached dangerous levels, mainly due to a determined effort on behalf of political parties, Hizbullah in particular, to ensure that the fruits of liberation are not soured by internecine fighting and mob justice.
But there is a clear sentiment running throughout the villages in the former occupation zone that the militiamen have been treated too leniently by the Military Tribunal in Beirut.
Many southern residents are in a vengeful mood.
“It’s impossible for me to share a country, let alone a village, with a heretic, collaborator and traitor who was the reason for so much suffering,” said Adnan Srour, a resident of the village of Aitta Shaab in the western sector of the former occupation zone.
Srour spent the final six months of occupation as a detainee at the Khiam detention center, where he was tortured every day for the first two months. He was one of 150 residents of the village detained at one time or another in Khiam.
“I think there are many like me who would like to fight these SLA returnees,” he said.
The Daily Star found similar views in every village visited in the former occupation zone, including Rshaf, Beit Lif, Khiam, Hebbarieh and Shibaa.
The occupation forces imposed a clampdown on Aitta Shaab in July 1999 following two bomb attacks in the vicinity: one against a bus full of “Israeli” soldiers crossing the border into Lebanon and the other against Ahmad Shibli Saleh, the head of the SLA’s intelligence wing in the village. Saleh survived the blast.
In the wake of the attacks, the SLA conducted house-to-house searches, detained several residents and banned inhabitants from leaving the occupation zone.
Srour had been working for Hizbullah inside the zone since 1987. His job was to set up munitions dumps with the weapons, explosives and ammunition he received from resistance fighters whom he would meet at night in remote wadis.
On Nov. 16, the SLA came for Srour. Two senior SLA security members from Aitta Shaab, Hassan Mansour and Hussein Shibli Saleh ­ a cousin of Ahmad ­ smashed down the front door and took Srour to Khiam.
Srour was severely tortured in the prison. He was stripped naked and repeatedly submerged in a tank of water ­ his head held down by the boot of an interrogator. Electrodes were placed in the water and kept there until Srour passed out.
“Over a period of seven hours, they would repeat this about 10 times,” Srour recalled.
Other forms of torture included having electrodes attached to his tongue, earlobes and penis; having buckets of hot and cold water thrown over him; being suspended by his wrists from a pole with his legs pulled apart by chains; and being repeatedly punched and kicked about the body and face.
The interrogators made sure that his clothes and mattress were always soaking wet, despite the cold winter weather. He spent a total of 52 days in solitary confinement.
Srour said he refused to talk to his interrogators. After 15 days, his mother and wife were detained to pressure Srour. He still refused to submit and the torture continued for another 59 days.
While Srour languished in Khiam, Ahmad Shibli Saleh harassed his relatives, beating up two of his brothers, as well as stealing $4,000 and burning two cars belonging to the family.
Two months before the “Israeli” withdrawal, Hassan Mansour left Lebanon for Germany. When the occupation zone collapsed in May, both Ahmad and Hussein Shibli Saleh fled to “Israel”.
Despite his role in the SLA’s security branch, Hussein Shibli Saleh later returned to Lebanon ­ but not before receiving a $40,000 payoff from the “Israeli” government. He was sentenced to five years in prison. His family still lives in Aitta Shaab.
“Unfortunately, the government has offered the collaborators a badge of honor for having betrayed their country,” Srour said. “Five years is no sentence for this man.”
Srour’s soft voice, shy smile and meticulous politeness are at odds with his unforgiving views toward the SLA. Still a loyal member of Hizbullah ­ the walls of his home are festooned with party posters and pictures of Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah ­ Srour says he has no intention of exacting revenge.
“As a person in the school of Hizbullah, I take orders from them,” he said. “If it’s in the interests of Islam as represented by Hizbullah that I eliminate a collaborator, then I will, obediently. If not, then I will do nothing.”
Would he enjoy fulfilling such an order?
“Of course, with all happiness,” Srour said. “I don’t just mean Hussein Shibli Saleh but any collaborator who caused suffering.”
Like most villages in the former occupation zone, some families in Aitta Shaab were more closely connected to the SLA than others.
“The majority of the Saleh, Zein, Jawad and Tahine families pine for the days of the occupation,” Srour said. “No matter how often you tell them about the evils of occupation and the Zionists, their only concern is to look after their own interests: to own fancy cars and big houses and have lots of money.”
The frustration and resentment of Adnan Srour and others has led to a growing number of revenge attacks in the past two months against known or suspected collaborators and their families.
Last month, the widow of Fawzi Saghir, a former head of the SLA in Beit Yahoun, was driven out of her home in Bint Jbeil. Saghir was killed in a roadside bomb ambush in 1998.
In January, the graves of 17 SLA militiamen were vandalized in the village of Aittaroun near Bint Jbeil.
At the beginning of this month, Abdel-Azir Dawi, a former SLA militiamen from Khiam who had completed his six-month prison sentence, was beaten up by a group of young men in the town. He subsequently left Khiam for Beirut.
On Sunday, a house belonging to Rabia Diab in Shemaa was partially destroyed in an explosion. Diab’s father Ghazi was a former head of the SLA in Shemaa. He was killed by the resistance in the early 1990s.
Security forces discovered four other bombs around the house which had failed to detonate. The bomb attack was “amateurish,” according to an engineer who lives in the village. Rabia Diab, who was not a member of the SLA, lives in Zambia and was having the house repaired prior to his return to Lebanon.
Last Tuesday, a house belonging to Ali Shaar, who spied on detainees at the Khiam detention center, was burned down in the village of Hebbarieh. Shaar is serving a three-year prison sentence and is banned from returning to the qada of Marjayoun/Hasbaya for another five years.
Death threats have also been made in Hebbarieh against Deeb Issa, the former head of the militia’s civil administration. Issa is serving a one-year sentence.
Sheikh Mohammed Kawtharani, a member of Hizbullah’s politburo, has slammed the SLA trials as “ceremonial” and the sentences as “scandalous.”
Retribution against former SLA militiamen has even reportedly extended to the United States. In January, news reached Bint Jbeil that the former security chief of the town, Kamel Fayyad, had been severely assaulted in the States and was in a critical condition.
Veteran UNIFIL spokesman Timur Goksel has watched the growing number of violent incidents with unease.
“There’s a feeling (among residents) that (the SLA) are getting away too easily. It’s worrying but I don’t know if it’s a trend yet,” he said.
Hamiyeh Mustafa, a resident of the Shiite village of Beit Lif in the western sector of the former occupation zone, said that the poor economic climate and “old feuds and power struggles between leading families” has helped fuel tensions.
“What is feeding the tension was the “Israeli” occupation, which pitted one family against another,” he said.
Around 30 residents were conscripted into the SLA, Hamiyeh said. Another four joined willingly. Two of them were Ali Khalil Humayed and Abbad Sadeq Hammoud.
Humayed was responsible for the 1981 killing of the brother of Bint Jbeil MP and leading Amal official Ayoub Humayed.
“One way the “Israelis” were able to secure the loyalty of the SLA was to order them into killing anyone with nationalist sympathies,” Mustafa explained. “Once they had killed, there was no turning back.”
Mustafa has adopted a magnanimous approach to the former members of the SLA in Beit Lif.
“We know that many were pressured into joining the militia and we’re happy that the government was easy on them,” he said. “But there are also people who we think should have received much more than one year in prison. Some of them are in this village.”
Majed Malek is one of six former militiamen to have returned to Beit Lif.
He spent 10 years with the SLA yet was sentenced to only six months in prison.
The villagers said that Malek had covertly worked for Hizbullah and military intelligence, which explained the lenient sentence. But Malek said his release was simply “an act of God.”

A Palestinian Martyr, 28 Wounded in A Day of Rage…(4)

In the latest “Israeli” aggressions against  Palestinians, a Palestinian security  officer, Osama Hassan Selim, was  shot dead while posted at a  Palestinian security checkpoint near the Jewish settlement of Kfardarom in the Gaza Strip.

“Israeli” troops also shot and wounded  at least 28 Palestinians  including a 12-year-old boy.

Clashes erupted after weekly Friday  prayers across the Palestinian  territories.

17 were injured when shots  were fired at around 100 stone-throwers  in clashes north of El Bireh in the West  Bank.

Palestinian journalist Ahmed  al-Zaki, covering the incident for  Oman television, was among those hurt.

The clash broke out at the  end of a march from a mosque in  Ramallah joined by around 1,000  Palestinians, some of them waving their  national flag and shouting "continue the uprising."

Another  ten Palestinian youths were injured  by live gunfire from “Israeli” tanks in the  Mintar region of the northern Gaza  Strip.

Secretary General of Amnesty International Pierre Sane visited the Gaza Strip during the day, passing by the Mintar region where clashes erupted.

In central al-Khalil(Hebron) in the West Bank, one Palestinian was injured by a rubber bullet fired by “Israeli” occupation troops in another clash that broke out after the weekly Friday prayers.

In a separate development, Palestinian security officials said they had found the body of a Palestinian man in a valley by the West Bank village of Baytillo northwest of Ramallah on Friday, but said they did not know the circumstances of his death.

Medical sources told AFP the man’s head had been badly beaten and that he had died around three days ago, identifying him as Jaber Hamdan al-Hanadsheh, from al-Khalil, in his 40s.

Hamas Claims Responsibility For Mortar Attacks on Jewish Settlements…(5)

The armed wing of the Islamic movement Hamas claimed Friday  three separate mortar attacks on  Jewish settlements in the Gaza Strip in  the past week, in the first admission it possesses such weapons.

One of the attacks, claimed by Hamas's  Ezzedin al-Qassam brigade in a fax  sent to AFP, was the shelling Sunday  night of the Nahal Oz settlement  located inside occupied Palestine or what is now called "“Israel”".

The  first such attack left one “Israeli” soldier slightly injured.

Ezzedin al-Qassam  also claimed responsibility for the  mortar shelling of Gaza's Netzarim and Morag settlements  Wednesday night.

The brigade said it  was a swift and direct reaction to  the shelling of a Force-17 position  and the killing of a Palestinian  officer  by “Israeli” occupation troops.
