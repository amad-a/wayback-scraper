Foreign Ministry called on delegations abroad to celebrate Detainees Day(1)
"The Lebanese foreign Ministry today (Thursday) informed all the official and diplomatic delegations abroad, the necessity of celebrating the "National Day of Lebanese Detainees in "Israeli" Prisons" on July 14, 1999".

"The Ministry called on the Lebanese missions all over the world to organize seminars and different activities to let the world know about the issue of the 120 Lebanese detainees abducted by the "Israeli" occupation forces and their collaborators at the Khiam prison and in occupied Palestine.

Diplomatic delegations were asked to coordinate with the Lebanese communities abroad to make the necessary contacts with the concerned authorities and committees in each country to make them see the dangers of violating the international law by ""Israel""."

Parents visit their detained sons at Khiam prison(2)
"The convoy of the International Red Cross this afternoon (Thursday) returned from the occupied zone via the Kfar Tibneet crossing, after taking 23 detainees' parents to see 8 of their sons imprisoned at the Khiam prison".

"The convoy set off this morning via the crossing to make the weekly visit arranged by the Red Cross to the Khiam prison that confines 120 Lebanese detainees within its cells, some of them are women and children".

"The visited detainees are: Azza Qassem Malkani, Haidar Issa, Nehmeh Daqdouq, Hassan Daqdouq, Youness Qaawar, Youness Abbass, Ali Marmar and Samir Qassemi".

"As they returned, the parents said the news of "Israeli" withdrawals had reached the prison.
Their morale is high and many of them are hoping that they might spend the rest of the summer freet
their homes, the parents noted.

"This might be possible, especially after the initiative of Hezbollah that called for the release of Lebanese detainees in return for releasing four collaborators that were captured in the occupied zone and are now held by the party".

Uneasy ride in the south(4)
"Nicholas Blanford drives through the occupied zone, a part of the country most people never see
You cannot avoid a slight sense of foreboding when crossing from the comfort of everyday Lebanon into the uncertain dangers of the occupation zone".

"The ambiance ­ a combination of fear, claustrophobia, and isolation ­ is palpable the moment the last Lebanese Army checkpoint recedes in the rear-view mirror and the first SLA* militiamen come into view.

"The Kfar Tibnit crossing, near Nabatieh, is one of five access points into the zone. You must negotiate no-mans land, a kilometer of rutted track, gouged out by dozens of heavy trucks that pass in and out every day laden with sand or rock.

"Several trucks and Mercedes taxis are parked beside the SLA checkpoint. Plainclothes militiamen with AK-47 assault rifles wave the vehicles through a swing gate one-by-one. Depending on the level of violence in the area, the SLA open the crossing at 10am and close it at 2pm.

"The apparatus of a military occupation is immediately evident. Concrete-filled oil drums, sandbags and car tires provide the fortifications for the Ali Taher outpost on a small hill overlooking the checkpoint. Camouflage netting has been thrown carelessly over one side of an administration office and empty wooden ammunition crates ­ with Hebrew markings ­ lie piled to one side of the road.

"Waiting to greet me at the SLA checkpoint is Boutros Wanna, a school teacher and The Associated Press correspondent based in Marjayoun.

"Boutros must accompany me as a minder. The "Israelis" forbid anyone to drive alone in the occupation zone as a precaution against suicide car bombers.

"The best place to begin our 73-kilometer journey is Naqoura, a ramshackle and dusty village scattered untidily along the shores of the Mediterranean.

Since 1978, the village has been home to Unifils headquarters and its multi-national mix of military and civilian staff.

"The peacekeepers fill the village with rows of accommodation blocks, offices and mess halls stretching alongside the sea. Taking a break from compiling his daily news summary, Unifils spokesman, Timur Goksel, informs us that the zone has been quiet apart from some routine shelling.
The road east out of Naqoura climbs in a series of hairpin bends through the brush-covered hills. The Tyre Peninsula, with its cluster of apartment blocks, is just visible through the heat haze to the north, jutting out into a sparkling sea.

"Alma Shaab, a Christian village four kilometers from the coast, appears almost deserted apart from a few chickens scratching in the dust. Like most villages inside the zone, the curb of the sidewalk, the street lights and iron railings alongside the road are painted in sun-faded stripes of red, white and green. The decor is provided courtesy of the SLA in a somewhat clumsy bid to cheer up the otherwise mundane surroundings.

"Apart from the odd tractor, there is little traffic. The road is narrow, uneven and the frequent potholes and ramps treat the suspension of our car with appalling disregard. Our route takes us close to the border and from time to time on cresting a hill we gain a brief glimpse of neatly laid out settlements and wheat fields inside "Israel".

"Most residents of the western sector are busy harvesting tobacco. Headscarfed women with weather-beaten faces squat amid rows of bright green plants and pluck the leafs into sacks.

"Boutros urges caution as we approach the "Israeli" hilltop outpost at Jabal Blat. His caution is justified: Hizbullah has just finished pounding the position with mortar rounds. The shelling has set light to the undergrowth beside the eastern ramparts and a cloud of gray smoke billows into the blue sky. There is no movement in the post that we can see. The "Israeli" soldiers, unwilling to expose themselves to further shelling, appear content to let the fire burn itself out.

"The tiny village of Marouahine lies at the foot of Jabal Blat and is reached by a rutted, dusty track. A group of women, seemingly unconcerned at the recent shelling, sit on the stone floor of their house and thread tobacco leaves together.

Sometimes shells land near the house and we get showered with shrapnel. But weve grown used to it, says Mona Adayikh, 22. She points to a viciously jagged piece of shrapnel the size of a cigarette lighter lying on the floor beside the front door. That fell there from the shelling earlier on, she says.

The Blat outpost is one of the most frequently targeted "Israeli" positions in the zone, so Mona only has half my attention. The other half is listening intently for the sounds of renewed shelling. She explains that the family plants wheat to eat and tobacco to sell".

"The tobacco crop generates an annual income of $2,400. This, says Mona, is sufficient to feed and clothe the eight members of her family, and there is still enough money left over each year to add one more room to her sprawling house".

"The occupation becomes more apparent from Blat. The SLAs western sector headquarters is located at the beginning of Bint Jbeil. An "Israeli" soldier stands beneath an observation tower beside the entrance to the camp as a dark blue Mercedes with tinted windows and a forest of aerials on the roof speeds through the gate".

""Israeli" officers tend to travel throughout the zone in armor-plated Mercedes sedans which are supposed to render them inconspicuous to avoid being targeted by Hizbullahs roadside bombs. No one is fooled: "Israeli" Mercedes are easily spotted because they lie low in the road, weighed down by armor plating. The tinted windows are a further giveaway".

"The street at the entrance of the town widens as we drive into the center of Bint Jbeil. In the morning, the broad high street had been packed with shoppers taking advantage of the towns weekly market. It was once famous in the area, with its abundance of fresh local fruits and vegetables attracting customers from as far away as Tyre. Now, the reduced size of the market reflects the loss of Bint Jbeils population from a pre-occupation total of around 30,000 to little more than 1,000.

Everything is getting worse here, both on a military level and an economic level, says the owner of a shop selling electrical goods. He prefers not to give his name and glances nervously through the window as an SLA armored personnel carrier (APC) roars up the street. He explains that his goods ­ pocket calculators, cheap computers, CDs ­ all come from Beirut via the crossings near Naqoura or Jezzine.

The SLA charge a tax on the goods we bring into the area. Usually we pay around 1 to 3 percent of the retail value of all these goods. But the SLA decides on the exact amount on the spot, he says.

He says he spent nine years living in New York but returned to Lebanon two years ago. It was a mistake. Living here is more expensive than New York, he says, more concerned with costs than safety.

"The APC is back, thundering down the street again. The clatter of steel tracks against the asphalt road invokes a sense of menacing urgency. Whats going on? People walking along the pavement turn their faces away from the APC, unwilling to be seen paying too much attention. The vehicle lurches to a halt. A militiaman, leaving his M-16 rifle clamped to the side, jumps down and runs into a shop. He emerges a moment later clutching nothing more lethal than a packet of cigarettes, which he tosses to a colleague.

"While other areas of Lebanon have succumbed to massive development, much of the zone, outside the towns and villages, has probably changed little in the past 100 years.

Undulating expanses of honey-colored grass surround the villages of Aaitaroun and Blida. Patches of wheat and green tobacco nestle at the bottom of wadis. But the evidence of occupation is never far away. The hillsides are scarred by tracks of churned earth carved by "Israeli" tanks.

"As we drive through Meiss al-Jabal, my eye is caught by a tiny flash of light from the brow of a small hill about 500 meters away on the border. Then we hear the loud report of an artillery gun, audible over the sound of the car engine. Moments later, another flash, another thunderclap. Two "Israeli" 155mm guns hidden behind a wall of sandbags are firing shells northwards.

"The road snakes into the zones central sector from Meiss al-Jabal. Some of the most important "Israeli" outposts lie ahead of us. We are also entering an area subject to roadside bombs.

"Plumes of smoke rising from a hillside just below the "Israeli" Abbad position opposite the village of Houla warn us that a resistance attack is either in progress or has just finished. Boutros and I momentarily consider whether it is prudent to continue. We pull into Houla and await developments.

"In the center of the village, a group of old men line a stone bench shaded by a tree adorned with clusters of scarlet flowers. "Israeli" warplanes can be heard circling high above, invisible against the brilliance of the afternoon sky. The guns at Meiss al-Jabal continue to hurl their lethal loads across the central sector. On the edge of the zone to the west, around the villages of Majdel Silm, Khirbet Silm, Jmaijme and Safad al-Battikh, columns of dirty white smoke mark each exploding shell.

The area is taking a savage pounding but the old men finger their beads and chat on, ignoring the violence unfolding around them. Giggling children flock around us and act up for the benefit of my camera.

We just carry on as usual, says one old man. If something hits the village then we hide in our houses.

"The "Israeli" warplanes head west and moments later comes a faint thump, barely audible above the breeze. A cloud of black smoke towers into the sky, 20 kilometers away near Jabal Botom.

Whenever the "Israeli" warplanes find themselves short of a clear target, they tend to drop a token bomb or two into a wadi near Jabal Botom. Good practice for the aircrews and little chance of harming nearby civilians.

"The shelling has stopped ­ it appears to be safe to continue.

"The Abbad outpost with its landmark radio antenna was the scene of two roadside bomb ambushes last November in which five "Israeli" soldiers were killed.

"Slowing down beside the entrance to the position, I recognize the spot where the first bomb exploded from the video footage of the attack shot by Hizbullah. The bomb was planted just meters away from the entrance, not only in full view of the "Israelis" but also the SLA position at the village of Markaba, which we reach by following a narrow lane, beginning opposite the entrance to Abbad ­ a spot which the locals refer to ominously as bomb alley.

"Boutros tells me that until a year ago, roadside charges were regularly planted in the stone walls running along either side of the lane. Markaba is still prone to the deadly devices. Dumped to one side of the village square are the blackened remains of a small van destroyed in a roadside bomb attack between Markaba and Talloussa just three days earlier. The blast crumpled the front right-hand side of the vehicle as if it had been punched by a giant fist.

"It is quiet today. Mahmoud Krayani, 64, is overseeing the task of threading tobacco leaves and hanging them in bunches to dry in the sun. All 13 members of his family are sitting amid heaped piles of leaves sewing them together.

"Krayani admits that the future is worrying but says a withdrawal is in everyones best interests. We have no problems with Hizbullah because once the "Israelis" leave, there will be no need for Hizbullah to continue their operations.

"We have our first uninterrupted view of "Israel" after passing through Adaisseh. The plain of northern Galilee unfolds in a series of fields, lakes and neat settlements surrounded by pine-tree windbreaks. The barren slopes of the Golan Heights rise gently in the distance. Mount Hermon soars majestically to the north.

"At this point, the road runs alongside the border. "Israel" is just five meters away, the other side of twin barbed-wire fences and a track edged by swept sand.

"In contrast to the barren, boulder-strewn hillside on the Lebanese side, "Israel" is dense with fruit orchards. The soil the "Israelis" stole from Lebanon last October has been transformed into a series of terraces near the border fence. The soil now supports young fruit trees. Two "Israeli" farmers are tending the saplings: The "Israeli" government might have admitted to the theft but they have no intention of returning the soil.

"The road descends to Kfar Kila, a village which lies in uncomfortable proximity to the "Israeli" settlement of Metulla. Only the border fence separates the scruffy buildings of Kfar Kila from the neat, white, box-shaped houses of Metulla. "Israel's" obsession with security is taken to the extreme in Metulla. The sides of the houses facing Lebanon have narrow slits for windows, ­ just in case any Lebanese decides to take a pot shot at an "Israeli" in his bathroom.

Some of the estimated 3,000 Lebanese who have jobs in "Israel" come from Kfar Kila. The "Israelis" initiated the good fence policy in the 1970s to foster ties with the Christians of south Lebanon. Much of this workforce enters "Israel" via Metulla to seek work as waiters in restaurants or shelf stackers in supermarkets.

"Marjayoun, the capital of the occupation zone and headquarters of the SLA, lies on the slopes of a hillside eight kilometers beyond Kfar Kila. Traditional square stone houses, with terracotta roofs and blue- or green-painted shutters, flank the towns steep, cobbled streets".

"The SLA headquarters, located in a former Lebanese Army barracks, is on the southern edge of the town. Video cameras on the side of the road monitor all passing traffic. A statue of the SLAs founder, Saad Haddad, hands purposefully placed on hips, guards the approach to the town center.

""Israeli" soldiers are rarely seen in the zone, mainly confining themselves to military affairs. It is the SLA that impinge on the daily lives of the residents through house-to-house searches, arrests and possible detention in the militias prison at Khiam".

"Here, in this bastion of the SLA, we bump into a familiar face. The last time I saw Johnny, he was summoning courage from a bottle of whisky just prior to pulling out of Jezzine. He was the driver of the very last APC to depart the enclave during the militias retreat last month. He and his three colleagues had faced the very real prospect of being killed by Hizbullah fighters on the road to the new front line at Kfar Houne.

We were the last to leave Jezzine but I led the column into Marjayoun the next day, Johnny says proudly. He says that the militiamen uncovered 12 roadside bombs along the route south of Jezzine.

What about the talk of an "Israeli" withdrawal? Johnny shrugs. I hope there will be a proper war. At least that way we either win or were all killed, he says. In the meantime, I do as Im told. I carry on driving my tank. Thats all I know and all I can do.

Monsignor Antoine Hayek, head of the Greek Orthodox community in Marjayoun, admits that there is uncertainty in the ranks of the SLA. They long ago entered a tunnel that is now very hard to come out of, he says, sitting in the cool splendor of his residence beside the Church of St. Peter. Pictures of Hayek with assorted dignitaries, including Pope John Paul II, share space with oil paintings of the Virgin Mary.

Hayek is reasonably confident that a post-withdrawal south will remain calm. This area is known for its coexistence. I dont foresee problems, he says. Unless external elements become involved. Were swayed by foreign influences ­ this has always been Lebanons weak point and this is what "Israel" fears.

Dusk is falling across south Lebanon as we enter the northern sector which begins at the Antoine Lahd Bridge straddling the Litani River west of Marjayoun. The lowland hills of the western sector seem a distant memory when confronted with the Wagnerian grandeur of the jagged mountains, precipitous valleys and pine forests that comprise the northern tip of the zone.

The roadsides have been scraped clear of vegetation and boulders for at least 20 meters to prevent the planting of bombs. There are no other vehicles on the road, no houses and no people to be seen. There are no civilians to get in the way of the fighting. Here, Hizbullah guerrillas and "Israeli" commando units fight close-quarter battles undisturbed. Here the occupation zone becomes a war zone.

At the Jarmaq outpost, suspicious SLA militiamen train their machine guns on our car as we pass by. A selection of T-55 tanks and APCs jam the entrance. The sleek lines of an "Israeli" Merkava tank, hidden behind sandbags, comes into view. An "Israeli" Star of David flag flutters disdainfully from the Aishiye compound.

"TV cameras are positioned along the road; some can be spotted down the slopes of seemingly inaccessible wadis. Pine trees, which could have provided cover for a guerrilla team, lie smashed and torn beside a mangled Syrian T-55 tank, apparently destroyed during the 1982 invasion.

Boutros tells me to drive slowly: Each corner has to be approached cautiously. Dusk is when the "Israelis" drive their tanks to their night-time positions, and in the northern sector, the "Israelis" are liable to shoot first and ask questions later.

"Journeys end is the village of Rihan, a miserable collection of abandoned and derelict stone houses dominated by two outposts, one occupied by "Israeli" troops, the other by the SLA.

A handful of old men sit in silence outside a store. An out-of-uniform militiaman leaning on the balcony of his house speaks into his walkie-talkie as we climb out of the car. The fighting has whittled the prewar population from 5,000 to less than 100, mostly elderly, people.

"The silence of the early evening is broken by the roar of a tank engine being warmed up. "Israeli" soldiers clamber over the vehicle to prepare it for its nightly duties.

In contrast to the exodus that has taken place from Rihan, the residents recently welcomed two new arrivals. A woman and her elderly mother were given homes in the village by the SLA after they accompanied the militia during its retreat from Jezzine.

My mother cooked for the SLA in Jezzine and they said she had better head south with them or the government would throw her in prison, the daughter said.

Her mother now prepares meals for the militiamen in the Rihan outpost after they were unable to find accommodation in Marjayoun. We were refugees from the Chouf and arrived in Jezzine in 1975. I spent all my life there. Im not happy to be in Rihan, she says.

"A dust-coated Mercedes pulls up. Inside are two very fat and unsmiling men with AK-47 assault rifles cradled in their laps. Both wear grubby jeans and both have slicked-back greasy hair.

"In a tone full of menace, the driver asks who we are and why we are there. His obese comrade in the passenger seat stares at himself in the rear-view mirror and smoothes back his hair with fingers the size of salamis, choked by several chunky gold rings. They are like sinister and grotesque twin cartoon characters ­ Tweedledum and Tweedledee with guns. Apparently satisfied with our credentials, the pair drive away".

Its almost dark. Its time to go. Neither Boutros nor I feel much regret at leaving Rihan with its "Israeli" tanks, silent old men and suspicious militiamen.

Nearly everyone who visits the zone remarks on its sense of isolation, of its dissociation from the rest of Lebanon. While the rest of the country moves forward, the zone lags behind, caught in a trap of outdated fears and mixed loyalties.

Theres no real joy of life in the area, said Monsignor Hayek. We celebrate holidays and feasts but theres always firing going on. The people here never stop worrying. Theyve always been worried and all this speculation of a withdrawal has simply created new worries.
