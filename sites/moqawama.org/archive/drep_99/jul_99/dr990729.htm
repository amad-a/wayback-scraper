Aita al-Jabal still seiged (2)
The blockade of Aita al-Shaeb in occupied south Lebanon is still imposed by the Israeli occupation forces but failed to stop the spread of the news to the outside.

After confiscating all the town's cellular phones, one of the residents succeeded to slip out a full report about the situation inside the town and news of the last week of the seige.

The "Follow-up Committee of Lebanese Detainees in Israeli Prison" said that 75 of Aita's residents were subjected to questioning by the Israeli occupation forces.

Among those questioned were women and children. The interrogations focused on the movements of the house masters and the "strangers" who visited them.

Five of the town's residents were taken to the Khiam prison and they are: Khalil Srour, 25, a university student - Samih Srour, 23, a first adjutant in the Lebanese Internal Security Forces (the police) - Hassan Moussa Rida, 15, a student - Mohamad Moussa Rida, 25, - Mohamad Nehmeh Rida.

Sources in the occupied zone said the above arrests came as a terrorism act to scare the steadfast people of Aita al-Jabal and the surrounding villages.

The storming of Aita's houses is continuing as some houses have been stormed several times and their furniture was destroyed during the Israeli occupation's violations

HUMAN RIGHTS WATCH CALLED ON ISRAEL TO STOP EXPELLING LEBANESE FROM OCCUPATION ZONE  (3)
The New York-based Human Rights Watch has pledged to move the issue of Lebanese expelled from occupied southern Lebanon up the international diplomatic agenda. The group unveiled the first ever report on expulsions by Israel and its allied militia from the south. It also urged Israeli Prime Minister Ehud Barak to put an immediate halt to the summary expulsions of Lebanese civilians from their homes.

This elderly couple was forced out of their village, Shibaa, in occupied southern Lebanon because their son refused to serve the Israeli allied militia. He fled the occupied zone, and only hours later, occupation forces shoved the old couple in a car.

The destination: the Zemraya border crossing which links the area Israel occupies with the government-held territory. There are no reliable figures on the number of Lebanese forced out of their land since Israel rolled its tanks into Lebanon in 1978.

Hundreds if not more have been thrown out of the occupied zone. Their plight received the first international media attention today, when Human Rights Watch today(yesterday) exposed the issue to at widely-covered news conferences in Beirut and occupied Jerusalem. The group's Executive Director for the Middle East and North Africa read out a report on expulsions, based on testimonies and interviews from expelled southerners, and on evidence compiled from diplomats and high ranking officials. The report says the number of people expelled from the occupied zone has been increasing in correlation with the rising number of defections from the Israeli allied militia. Those expelled have been reduced to poverty, leaving behind their homes and land. The US State Department's human rights report last year claimed only twelve people were expelled from the zone.

But in the occupied Arkoub region alone, more than 250 residents were expelled between 1987 and 1999. Mostapha Hashem is from the occupied village of Shibaa in Arkoub.

His family along with his uncle's family were expelled from the zone in 1989, after his father, a Canadian-Lebanese, led a demonstration against Israeli attempts to institute a so-called civilian administration in the village. It aims to "normalize" ties between the occupation zone and Israel. The report says it is hard for people opposed to the occupation to remain in the zone.

It warns that forced conscription in the Israeli allied militia has turned the life of families in the zone, which constitutes ten percent of Lebanon, into a nightmare. Human Rights Watch called on Israeli Prime Minister Ehud Barak to immediately instruct Israeli troops and Israeli allied militiamen in the south to stop expulsions and allow back Lebanese to their homes and villages. It also urges Israel to revise the restrictions on freedom of movement imposed on residents of the zone. Human Rights Watch says their report is a step in the right direction. (NS: Hanny Megally, executive director, Human Rights Watch, Middle East and North Africa, told Future TV: "it is the first report on the subject, even when we talked to organizations here in Lebanon, it became clear to us that there are not substantive reports on the subject, we are trying to raise it and put it on the international agenda").

The report calls on the US and the European Union to publicly condemn deportations and forcible transfers, describing expulsions as collective punishment for suspected activities of some residents against Israel. It stressed, expulsions are categorically prohibited by the Geneva Conventions. The group asked US Secretary of State Madeleine Albright to raise the issue during her visit to Israel next month. (NS: Megally, saying: "We asked her (Albright) to raise this subject when she goes to Jerusalem, we sent her a letter, and copy of the report, we raised it during Prime Minister Ehud Barak's visit to New York and Washington"). Human Rights Watch is also demanding that those who have committed or ordered expulsions, regardless of nationality, be brought to justice. Mohammed Safa, a local activist on behalf of Lebanese detainees in Israeli prisons, said the report amounted to evidence of Israeli war crimes and is a clear condemnation by the international community against Israeli atrocities in occupied southern Lebanon. The fifty-six-page report provides evidence that Israel is directly responsible for the expulsions in the south, contrary to claims that such atrocities are the sole responsibility of its allied militia. Israel has yet to comment on the report.

Woman recounts through tears suffering and destruction from expulsion  (5)
Souraya Hammoud, her husband Sharif and two children were expelled from the village of Kfar Hamman in Hasbaya in 1989 after her eldest son Afif was detained by South Lebanon Army militiamen.

But the 57-year-old spent six weeks in detention before being forced to leave the village for the first time in her life.

I will never forget how much I suffered the first two years after I came to Beirut. We would move from one relatives house to another because we didnt want to feel we were a burden, Souraya said as she wiped tears from her cheeks.

The Hammouds finally rented an apartment in the southern suburbs, waiting for their eldest sons release and their return to their village.

Sourayas heartache lessened when her son Afif was released in 1998.

I kept trying to visit my house and finally, three months ago, the SLA gave me a pass to go there for only two hours, she said.
The house, she said, was rundown, with their furniture eaten by rats and termites and their trees dried out.

Even with all this destruction, I will go back now if they let me. It is enough for me to sit at the porch and look out at my garden, she said.

Military trials of former SLA members begin in earnest  (6)
A group of 21 former members of the Israeli-allied South Lebanon Army were tried at the Military Tribunal on Wednesday on various charges, including collaboration with an enemy state.

George Youssef Youssef was the first to appear before the court, which questioned him about allegations of smuggling cigarettes from the occupation zone to the government-controlled territories.

Shadi Mansour admitted entering Israel in 1995 with his brother Wisam, who is still at large, while Paul Charbel Youssef said that he had been a technician at Middle East Television, which is jointly run by the SLA and American Christian missionaries.

George Moukheiber admitted to having worked as a guard in the SLAs main post in Jezzine since 1989, while Tannous Elias Tannous admitted leading the SLAs 20th regiment, which gained notoriety as being the SLAs intelligence apparatus.

Sixteen others were questioned about their role as fighters with the militia.

All the defendants said that they had to join the SLA because they were either pressured to do so by the militia or had to work to support their families and could not find other jobs.

The court, presided over by Brigadier Maher Safieddine, was meeting for the second time since last Wednesday, the day the trial of 220 former SLA militiamen began.

The trials of the former militiamen ­ who turned themselves in to the authorities after their militia pulled out from Jezzine last month ­ will take place every Wednesday until all the cases are studied.

Assistant Military Prosecutor, Myassar Shokor, demanded the sentencing of all defendants to various terms in prison with hard labor, in line with the list of charges.

But defense lawyers called on the court to take into consideration the conditions that had reportedly forced the defendants to join the militia.

The panel had not handed down verdicts by the time The Daily Star went to press.

- Justice Minister Joseph Shaoul issued a memorandum Wednesday warning employees at judicial departments not to provide any information about security cases to the media.

Violators will face punishment, the memorandum said.
