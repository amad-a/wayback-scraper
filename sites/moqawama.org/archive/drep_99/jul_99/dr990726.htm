July Monday 26-1999:

Nassrallah urges more cooperation to highlight plight of Khiam detainees (1)
Hizbullah Secretary-General Sayyed Hassan Nassrallah on Sunday urged the two rival groups seeking the release of Lebanese prisoners to unite to better serve the interests of the detainees. He warned against being taken in by false promises of peace made by Israels new Prime Minister and said attacks would not stop for the sake of peace negotiations.

Nasrallah was speaking at a rally held at the Unesco Palace to commemorate the 10th anniversary of the kidnapping of Sheikh Abdel-Karim Obeid and his two colleagues Hashem Fahs and Ahmad Obeid by Israeli commandos from his house in the southern village of Jibsheet. Imprisonment succeeded in uniting the detainees affiliated to different parties; should not those groups calling for their release do the same? Nassrallah asked. It is possible to unify both groups, but only if they overcome their personal and political differences.

The two organizations, the Follow-Up Committee for the Support of Lebanese Detainees in Israeli Prisons (FCSD) and the National Committee for Lebanese Detainees in Israeli Prisons (NCD) have long been in conflict because of differing political allegiances.

The FCSD was formerly known as the Gathering for Detainees in Ansar, the site of the infamous Israeli detention center in the 1980s. The group, which brings together primarily leftist-oriented individuals, began campaigning for the release of detainees in Khiam and other prisons in Israel in 1992. In 1997, the NCD was established and offered an office at the headquarters of the Council of the South, a government-run institution affiliated with Speaker Nabih Berri.

Officials of both groups attending the ceremony voiced their willingness to fulfil Nasrallahs calls. We have been calling for cooperation and unification since the day the NCD was established and we are ready to make such a step now, said Mohammed Safa, FCSDs secretary-general.

Ibrahim Kalash, NCDs deputy-president, met Nasrallah earlier this week and declared his groups readiness to consolidate efforts with other organizations. We suggested it to the secretary ­ general and we dont have a problem in going ahead with it, Kalash said.

Prime Minister Salim Hoss listed the governments efforts in publicizing the plight of the detainees.We supported local and international efforts for the release of detainees, pronounced the Day of the Lebanese Detainee as a national event, and asked Lebanons envoy at the UN and our representative with the Human Rights Committee in Geneva to highlight the cause, said Hoss.

But Nasrallah said: I dont want to undermine other peoples efforts, but in a world ruled by force not law, only the resistance fighters who are confronting one of the toughest armies in the Middle East, can bring the detainees back home, said Nasrallah.He played down claims that resistance attacks had decreased after Ehud Barak, the Israeli prime minister, had assumed office at the beginning of the month.

Nobody should be deceived into believing that we would ever reconsider halting our attacks, which occur on continuous daily basis, merely because there are talks of an Israeli withdrawal within one year said Nasrallah.

Hoss said a separate peace agreement between Lebanon and Israel without Syria was out of the question.

Our past experiences with Israel have been marked by lying, deceit and lots of maneuvering. We are treating Baraks presumed openness (toward peace) with extreme caution, said Hoss.

The commemoration ceremony, Nasrallah said, would help expose the Israeli governments breach of international treaties on human rights.

Nassrallah condemned Arab satellite television stations for what he called the minimal exposure of detainees and the plight of their families.

Were not asking them to turn into a resistance satellite station but the minimum they could do is broadcast programs to highlight the cause, he said.

Ibrahim Abdullah, president of the Lebanese Human Rights Committee suggested that Obeids kidnapping anniversary should be used as a benchmark to launch a worldwide campaign to shed light on Israels brutality and illegal detentions.

Ethnic cleansing, domestic style(2)
 While the new Interior Minister, Natan Sharansky, is examining his ministry's policies on East Jerusalem, a ministry official told historian Dr. Mousa Budeiri of Jerusalem that his residency visa would not be extended. The announcement means that Dr. Budeiri will be unable to leave the country - or, more accurately, that if he leaves he will be unable to return except as a tourist, and then he would lose the right to live permanently in Jerusalem. Budeiri's case is an example of the methods used by the Interior Ministry to mistreat the city's Arab residents.Dr. Budeiri was born 54 years ago in West Jerusalem's Katamon neighborhood, and soon after moved with his family to the eastern part of the city. His family is one of the most respected and wealthiest among Jerusalem's Arabs. His father was a doctor and leftist activist, who also left behind a well-known book of memoirs. Budeiri graduated from high school in East Jerusalem before going to Oxford. In 1967, while he was in England, the Israeli Interior Ministry approved a "family reunification" for him, and he received an Israeli identity card as a resident of East Jerusalem. His wife, who is English, also received an Israeli identity card.

Budeiri travels abroad frequently to pursue his academic interests. He has taught at both Bethlehem and Bir Zeit Universities, and for the past several years has been teaching at Jerusalem's Al-Quds University. Previously, he studied and taught at Cambridge and Princeton as well. Until 1979 he traveled abroad under an Israeli laissez-passer - a sort of passport issued by the Interior Ministry to Arab residents of the city who are not Israeli citizens.

In 1980, during a sabbatical in London, Dr. Budeiri noticed that his laissez-passer was about to expire. He turned to the Israeli embassy, where he was told to wait for an extension of the document to arrive from Jerusalem. He waited, and in the meantime the travel document expired. The Israeli consul told him that the only way he could return to Israel was by obtaining a foreign passport. He could then obtain a "returning resident" certificate and a visa that would allow him to return to Jerusalem and continue to hold an Israeli identity card.

Budeiri followed these instructions: Since his wife had a British passport, he obtained one as well and used it to return to Israel. Since then, Budeiri has traveled abroad frequently on his British passport, which had an Israeli visa stamped in it to enable him to enter and leave as a resident of Jerusalem rather than as a tourist.

The Israeli visa is very significant, since if he were here on a tourist visa, he would be prohibited from working. He would also be unable to receive health insurance and other benefits from the government.

The last time, in 1996, the Interior Ministry gave him a three-year visa (in his British passport). This was very convenient, because it meant that he did not have to extend it each time. However, a few weeks ago the visa expired. Three weeks ago he went, as usual, to the Interior Ministry to renew it. His wife and children divide their time between Jerusalem and London, and they wanted to spend Dr. Budeiri's summer vacation from Al-Quds University together in England.

This time, he was told at the Interior Ministry that there was a problem, since he travels abroad frequently, and they want to check whether he really lives in Israel. They checked and they checked and finally, last week, told him that they will not issue a visa for him. This means that he can leave the country on his British passport, but without a visa that would enable him to return. In other words, if he goes abroad, he would lose his right to live permanently in Jerusalem. In the meantime, about half of his vacation has gone by and he has asked his wife and children to come here, since he cannot go to them.

According to human rights organizations in Jerusalem, there are thousands more like Budeiri. The fact that in 1967 Jerusalem Arabs did not receive full Israeli citizenship, like all other Israeli Arabs, allows the Interior Ministry to torment them. The ministry seeks to take away their Israeli identity cards and force them to leave the city. That is the essence of the process that Jerusalem Arabs call the "quiet transfer." Those who prefer more direct names call it the Israeli bureaucracy of ethnic cleansing in Jerusalem
