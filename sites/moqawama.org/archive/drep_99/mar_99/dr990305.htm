March Friday 5-1999:

Introduction
In Jerusalem  , the news said  that the "Israeli" army's so-called  northern commander  Gabi Ashkinazi has confirmed reports that a computer hard disc has disappeared from the Northern command HQ. Senior military sources insisted the disc contains sensitive data which could have a serious impact on military operations if  it falls  into the wrong hands. It is to be pointed out that this information had spread shortly after an "Israeli" brigadier general and three soldiers were killed by the Islamic Resistance (IR) militants deep inside the "Israeli" occupied area of south Lebanon on last Sunday.

Enemy command still investigating hard disc theft
"Israeli" Yedioth Ahronot newspaper said "Israeli" army's so-called northern commander Ashkenazi has confirmed reports that a computer hard disc has "disappeared" from the Northern Command Headquarters.

However, Ashkenazi was quick to claim that the disc did not contain data pertaining to military or security activities in southern Lebanon.

It was discovered on Tuesday that the disc was taken from the northern commander's computer, and although not containing information pertaining to operations in Lebanon, the disc did contain classified data pertaining to "military secrets", "Israeli" media said.

The northern commander also insisted that the data contained on the stolen disc did not have any connection to the recent operations against "Israeli" occupation forces in south Lebanon which claimed 7 "Israeli" lives over the past week.

Senior anonymous military sources insist the disc contains "sensitive" date which could have a serious impact on military operations if it falls into the wrong hands, Yedioth Ahronot added.

The investigation into the missing disc continues and among those questioned by "Israeli" military police were several soldiers assigned to the northern command.

Enemy northern command officials report the theft took place about three weeks ago, but it was only released for publication earlier this week.

It was explained that the missing disc was a type enabling it to be removed from the machine, permitting access to it to several persons operating on different computers. It also enabled the disc to be placed in a safe at the end of the day's work, enemy media said.

Resistance operations are paving the way back (2)
Hezbollah Deputy Secretary General his Eminence Sheikh Naem Qassem yesterday received a delegation from the town of Mleikh at the party's Secretariat HQ.

The delegation stressed the residents' determination to return to their hometown. They said they will tour officials and influential figures to seek assistance.

Sheikh Qassem commended the residents' action which reflects "national responsibility and clinging to the land, especially as the town is a place where different sects lived".

We came to discuss with his Eminence the town's status, besides congratulating him on the recent victories achieved by the resistance, Ezzet Abu Melhem, who spoke on behalf of the delegation, said.

We wish the resistance more victories, and more unity with the people, especially the Mleikh residents, whom we hope will be able to return to their town soon, he added.

Khalil Abu Zeid, another delegation member, said the "Resistance's operations have paved the way for us to return to our town from which we were displaced 14 years ago."

We noted a confusion in the ranks of the "Israelis", and we are trying to seize the opportunity with the blessing of the resistance, he added.

The delegation announced that a popular march will set out on Sunday from Jarjou towards Mleikh to achieve the return.

Time to go
A senior officer speaks frankly about the need for the army to get out of Lebanon...(5)
                                                                                                                    By Amos Harel
"The time has come to stop mincing words: We have no business staying in Lebanon. The army always has a ready excuse for why it's not the right time to discuss it. Either that it's too early - we haven't even buried the dead yet - or that we should wait until the investigation is complete, because it's still not clear what really happened up there. Then too much time passes by, and aside from the soldiers and their families, everyone loses interest in the situation. But in Lebanon, we're always between one incident and the next, between a terrorist raid and some other tragedy. So there's no choice but to talk about it now, while the soldiers are being killed."There is something symbolic about the death of Erez [Brigadier General Erez Gerstein, commander of the IDF's* liaison unit in Lebanon]. Reality has thrown us a tragic slap in the face. Of all people, Erez was a guy who was wholeheartedly in favor of staying in Lebanon and taking a tough stance, a fellow who always belittled the Hezbollah. He considered them third-rate terrorists. Erez would always say there was a military solution in Lebanon, that "we've gotta pull the finger out of our - and start ripping them to shreds." He was a very brave guy who always seemed as if nothing could happen to him. His death is a slap in the face to the military doctrine in Lebanon. It once again proves how the IDF has fallen victim to those same patterns of action that repeat themselves, in a sort of recurring ritual.
"There is also a symbolism about the way Erez was killed, after having paid a condolence call at Kafr Shab'a to the family of a fallen South Lebanese Army security officer. This in itself was the embodiment of the idea that we can rule over the security zone as if it were ours to rule over, and not as a military force that has seized the high ground in order to protect the valley below. This is what's happening to us: We act like colonial rulers, and then we're brought down to earth. This is the place to mention the advice that Charles de Gaulle gave the Americans about getting involved in Vietnam. Don't fall into the trap of being colonial occupiers, he told them, but they didn't know what he was
talking about. They had never considered themselves as such.

"Hezbollah isn't a terrorist organization. It's a guerrilla organization. It's an national liberation organization. Show me a single case in history in which an army managed to occupy territory over time and defeat a guerrilla organization that benefited from massive support and political backing from the local population. Even if a unilateral withdrawal is interpreted as weakness, that doesn't mean it ought to be ruled out. There are plenty of nations that have already done it: the Americans folded in Vietnam, and the Russians ran as fast as they could from Afghanistan. We can also get out. There are means of doing this gradually, without having to play games of honor and dignity. When he decided to pull out of Algeria, de Gaulle didn't try to sell his people any stories. Basically, he told the French: 'They deserve independence, and it's costing us too much.' And there it was a matter of tearing away at the flesh of France. Nobody in "Israel" considers South Lebanon to be an inseparable part of the Galilee. I don't think there's one sane Frenchman who is now sorry about withdrawing from Algeria.

"Again, allow me to refer you to the history books. General William Westmoreland (the commander of the American forces in Vietnam) was no idiot. He was a prisoner of the concept: Give me another 10,000 soldiers, and I'll beat the Vietcong. Afterward, when they gave him 8,000 soldiers, he argued - If you'd only sent me another 2,000, everything would have come out differently. Study his biography and you notice how he slowly begins to confuse the end (victory) with the means (dispatching more troops). In certain respects, this is also happening to the IDF, which has its own requests: Give us money for more means - weaponry and protection - so that we can defend our soldiers there.

"Have you been to South Lebanon recently? Have you seen what kind of outposts we've built there in the last year? We are sitting in these huge armored fortresses, which of course invite enemy shelling, and we make the convoys leading to them into easy targets. Little by little we're becoming Crusaders who primarily guard only ourselves. We are so stubborn about putting ourselves out there as bait, that it's frightening. What sort of successes can we be proud of? Discovering the explosive charges planted at the roadsides? But finding those charges is a defensive action; it doesn't cause any real damage to the other side.

"We don't have any way of winning in Lebanon, since such a goal does not exist. Our goal there is survival: protecting the residents of northern "Israel", and suffering as few IDF losses as possible. In Lebanon, the IDF has a sum total of two types of action. One is completely defensive - fortifications, convoy safety procedures, unearthing explosive roadside charges. The entire purpose of the other type of activity - taking the offensive - is to prevent terrorist raids in the security zone. Something along the lines of 'let's be waiting for them when they come.' At its foundation, this is also a form of defensive activity. It doesn't have any genuine, attainable objective.

"Killing Erez was a moral victory for Hezbollah, because this time it was close to home, very close to the highest echelons of the IDF and the Northern Command. In the army, we got the feeling that the Northern Command had stopped functioning for a moment, that things had gone out of control.

Erez's death was an immense emotional blow to them, partly because he really did radiate this sense of invulnerability. But there was another shocking event that took place in Lebanon this week - the demonstration by the residents of Beirut, who broke through the fence [constructed by the IDF] in the village of Arnoun. Either we still haven't figured out what happened there, or the Lebanese themselves haven't figured out what they achieved. Can you imagine what would happen if 10,000 residents came and simply marched up to the Beaufort? What would we do? Shoot them? For the Arabs, the Intifada was a victory over the "Israelis" brought about by sending out the masses to march and demonstrate. As soon as the Intifada is employed in the Lebanese context, we're going to have an even more difficult problem.

"The truth is that there isn't a hell of a lot more the IDF can do against the Hezbollah. We can drop tons of bombs on them, as we did in Operation Accountability and Operation Grapes of Wrath. The truth is there simply isn't any action we could take that would vanquish them, because there are no real targets to attack. I don't believe the government will do very much, beyond issuing proclamations and shows of force. You have to remember that the elections are at hand, and more dead soldiers will not bring Netanyahu any extra votes. I wasn't surprised that the Hezbollah did not react to the air force attack. They're not interested in any real escalation. The Grapes of Wrath understandings are comfortable for them, because on the one hand, they safeguard their civilian population, and on the other, allow them to continue striking at IDF soldiers.

"If there was parity between the populations, the IDF would be able to do more. But while the residents of the Galilee are not indifferent to katyushas, the Hezbollah has a population base that for the most part identifies with it, supports it and is ready to bear the brunt of the shelling and attacks.

Compared to the conflict with the Palestinian organizations in Lebanon, we have a huge problem here. Fatah had no roots in the population, so it had to build itself military strongholds, which were targets that the IDF could attack. But the Hezbollah feels at home in South Lebanon. Aside from a few security patrols of its own, it doesn't have any real targets that can be attacked.

"In my opinion, Hezbollah will not chase us into the Galilee if we leave the security zone and settle down along the international border. They are a liberation organization that has built itself on the goal of forcing the IDF out of South Lebanon. I doubt whether Jerusalem really interests them. It's rhetoric. Nor do I believe that Hezbollah would shoot at the bus at Avivim [a northern settlement; the speaker is referring to a PLO attack from Lebanese soil that occurred in the 1970s.] It doesn't serve their interests. Palestinian terror in the '70s had an objective - weakening the "Israeli" opposition - but what object would the Hezbollah have after the withdrawal? I have only one reservation: the mere fact of our prolonged stay in Lebanon sets into motion a process in which Hezbollah becomes so committed to hating "Israel" that in the end, the indoctrination of its fighters is liable to induce them to commit terrorist raids even after the withdrawal. Right now, this trend that can be forestalled by withdrawing from Lebanon.

"Regarding Syria, I'm in the minority. I have a hard time believing that Assad will still be able to control Hezbollah following an "Israeli" withdrawal. Hezbollah has its own agenda, its own very distinct social and religious background. I doubt whether Assad would be able to force it to continue its attacks following a withdrawal.

"There is only one real reason for staying, and that is our obligation to the SLA and the local populace. This is a troublesome problem, but I can't see how it can justify the continued bloodletting of our soldiers in Lebanon. We have an incomprehensible situation here: What would happen if every country took an extra 20 kilometers for itself to defend its border? Deep down, "Israel's" refusal to withdraw is a very strong defeatist statement - as if we are incapable of protecting our own settlements from the border.

"There is a real political problem here. The people of "Israel" are losing their patience and their ability to absorb more losses. Someone once calculated that the "Israelis" would be willing to accept 25 deaths a year in Lebanon. Now it turns out that the losses have to come in a certain scatter, and not include senior officers, in order for the public to be able to come to terms with "Israel's" continued stay in Lebanon.

"The political-diplomatic ranks are avoiding taking any decisions affecting the army, preferring that the army come up with the solutions first. The politicians are looking for excuses not to do anything. If the army says that we can't withdraw, then the politicians can seize on that as an excuse. Golda Meir used the same argument: 'My generals told me...'

"Lots of officers know that it's folly, but few of them say so out loud. Here and there, you hear it in internal discussions, but it's not easy to express your opinions when the leaders of the army feel differently. Few army officers would express any doubts about staying in Lebanon in front of a guy like Erez, who was so sure of himself. I'm always amazed by the immense gap between what is said at meetings and what some of the officers say afterward, when they head outside with the disposable coffee cups. It's only then that opinions are expressed a lot more openly. Lots of people who live and breathe Lebanon understand what's going on up there. Of course, there are others, who think otherwise. This is another cognitive dissonance. If you're already there, and doing the job with all that madness around you, you have to believe there is justification for the risks you are taking on.
"The saddest thing about the military mantras that officers are always offering up in interviews with the media is that many of them no longer believe them to be true. Army officers are expected to demonstrate determination and a winning facade, so that's what they do. So why don't you generally hear the other side? Because nearly every officer will be on his guard when talking with a journalist, even if it's off the record.

"Amnon [Lipkin-Shahak] was clearly revolted by these kinds of debates. He considered them a waste of time and energy. [Shaul] Mofaz is a very decent person. His recent statements - about how there are differences of opinion on the issue within the army - were made with the intention of sending up a message. I think that, in a very delicate way, he's hinting to the political echelons that they have to rethink everything that's happening in Lebanon. He's not going to come out and say 'we have to get out'; what he's doing is throwing the issue out there for the public to discuss it.
