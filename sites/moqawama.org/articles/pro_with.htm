The Pro-withdrawal Movement in "Israel": Is Peace in Sight?

The following report was written by Gary Gambill, the director of American for a Free Lebanon (AFL).

Introduction
As the "Israeli" occupation of South Lebanon enters its twentieth year, a striking transformation is taking place in "Israeli" society. Once relegated to the radical left, support for a unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon is rapidly proliferating throughout the "Israeli" political mainstream. Ten percent of "Israeli" parliament members now advocate an immediate and unconditional withdrawal from the "security zone." Ironically, increasing support for withdrawal is not rooted in the growth of any fundamental consensus in "Israel" regarding the peace process, but stems primarily from the polorization of attitudes in "Israeli" society.

The pro-withdrawal movement in "Israeli" society is extremely multifaceted, encompassing the political spectrum from the extreme right to the radical left. It has gained currency among both "Israeli" hawks and doves, and has both secular and orthodox religious manifestations. This study will examine each of the various socio-political strands comprising the pro-withdrawal movement in "Israel" and its potential influence on "Israeli" policy in Lebanon.

Historical Background
Opposition to "Israeli" intervention in Lebanon first surfaced in reaction to the 1982 "Israeli" invasion of Lebanon. The most prominent group opposing the war in Lebanon was Peace Now, which at its height organized demonstrations of over 400,000 people (making it by far the largest grassroots movement in "Israel's" history). Several other protest groups that emerged during the war are worthy of mention. Parents Against Silence, which organized a petition for a ceasefire that was signed by 100,000 parents.1 A group called Yesh Gvul (There's A Limit) gathered signatures of 1,500 soldiers asking that they not be called to serve in Lebanon. According to the "Israeli" Army, 140 soldiers chose to go to jail rather than serve in Lebanon.2

However, most of these groups did not oppose the war in Lebanon until "Israeli" casualties began mounting and it became clear that the objective of the invasion exceeded the government's declared goal of siezing a 40-km strip of territory along the border. As Emmanuel Farjoun points out, Peace Now.

"did not come out against the 'need' to secure a 40-km strip in Lebanon. They called for an immediate ceasefire. They did not call for a withdrawal of "Israeli" troops from Lebanon."3
In fact, prior to the 1984 "Israeli" elections, Peace Now released an eight point platform which explicitly called for a "security belt" in southern Lebanon. After "Israel" withdrew from all but a 15-km border strip in 1985, few "Israelis" challenged the prevailing view that the "security zone" endorsed by Peace Now was an essential safeguard against "terrorist" infiltrations from the north. Between 1985-1996, no major opposition group in "Israel" advocated a unilateral withdrawal from all of Lebanon.

Opposition to the occupation that has emerged within "Israel" over the last few years falls into two broad categories. The first, and by the far the most prevalent, is rooted in the perception that the costs to "Israelis" entailed in maintaining the occupation exceed the benefits derived from it. The main pro-withdrawal movements in "Israel" fall into the first category, and in this respect, closely resemble opposition which emerged in the United States to its occupation of South Vietnam. The second category of opposition is grounded in the belief that the devastation inflicted by "Israel" upon the population of South Lebanon is morally abhorrent, and therefore unjustified irrespective of the benefits to "Israel" accruing from it. Both categories of "Israeli" opposition to the occupation are discussed seperately below.

"Freedom from Lebanon"
At a recent gathering of "Israeli" activists near the border with Lebanon, protesters distributed stickers with the caption "Freedom from Lebanon-97" to passing motorists at traffic intersections (the slogan "Freedom for Lebanon" has been decidedly absent at such protests). Underlying the mainstream pro-withdrawal movement in "Israel" is the conviction that the occupation of Lebanon should be ended because it is far too costly to "Israelis". The level of pro-withdrawal sentiment in "Israel" correlates roughly with the level of casualties incurred by "Israeli" forces in Lebanon. Four Mothers
In February 1997, four mothers of "Israeli" soldiers stationed in Lebanon established a grassroots anti-war movement known as "Four Mothers" after 73 "Israeli" soldiers died in collision of two helicopters en route to south Lebanon. "We couldn't sleep that night," explained Miri Sela, one of the mothers. "That evening transformed us from people resigned to the idea that there is no other choice, to those determined to find a solution." 5

The Four Mothers organization, which now consists of 10 full-time members and about 300 volunteer activists, has rapidly mobilized public support throughout "Israel". According to Linda Ben Zvi, the head of English-language public relations for Four Mothers, about 20,000 "Israelis" have signed its petition for the unilateral withdrawal of the IDF* from Lebanon.6 The list of signatures now includes that of Rachel Levin, the wife of Amiram Levin-- the head of the IDF Northern Command.

While the Four Mothers organization remains the most prominent pro-withdrawal movement, it is not the only manifestation of opposition to the occupation in "Israeli" society. In September 1997, a former "Israeli" soldier who had served in Lebanon, Yoram Kastiel, began a highly publicized hunger strike to protest the occupation. A second grassroots pro-withdrawal movement, calling itself the "Good Fence," was established later the same month.

The Four Mothers movement has gained many advocates within the "Israeli" political establishment in recent months. In January 1998, a member of the "Israeli" parliament, Dalia Itzik MK (Labor), announced that she was joining the movement.

The Movement for a Peaceful Departure from Lebanon
The momentum sparked by the Four Mothers movement has rapidly crossed over into the mainstream political establishment of "Israel". In the fall of 1997, shortly after 12 "Israeli" soldiers were killed during a botched commando raid in September 1997, Labor MK Yossi Beilin established the Movement for a Peaceful Departure from Lebanon (MPDL). Beilin, a former cabinet minister of the Perez government and architect of the Oslo Accords, drew sharp criticism from Labor party chairman Ehud Barak. Nevertheless, seven Knesset members joined Beilin MPDL: Haim Ramon (Labor), Nissim Zvili (Labor), Yona Yahav (Labor), Yael Dayan (Labor), Dedi Zucker (Meretz), Naomi Chazen (Meretz), and Zvi Weinberg (Yisrael Ba'aliya).

The MPDL plan calls for "Israeli" forces to unilaterally withdraw from the "security zone" over a six month period and redeploy in strength along the northern border, which would be lined with mine fields and electronic fences. "Israel" would reserve the right to strike back at guerrilla forces that attempt to infiltration the border. The plan also states that unspecified measures will be taken to ensure the safe future of militiamen in the "Israeli"-allied South Lebanese Army (SLA), who will be given the opportunity to immigrate to "Israel". Beilin's plan drew sharp criticism from Ehud Barak, the head of the "Israeli" Labor party, who said the proposed withdrawal "means irresponsible abandonment of the northern part of this country, a gamble where the lives of civilians are at stake." 7

The main reason "Israel" remains in Lebanon," explains Beilin, is that "there is no public pressure on the government to leave Lebanon. Public pressure is likely to have an impact because not only is there no ideological reason to stay, but there is no internal political reason either."8 In the absence of such pressure, Beilin says, the occupation will continue because the "Israeli" government is "afraid that if it withdraws from Lebanon and something happens, it will be blamed for retreating."9

"Israeli" Interior Minister Avigdor Kahalani, who heads the Third Way party, was among the first voices in the political mainstream calling for a unilateral withdrawal.

Not surprisingly, several parliament members in the right-wing Likud party have advocated unilateral withdrawal for similar reasons, most notably Michael Eitan, the Likud party whip and Gideon Ezra, former deputy head of the Shin Bet. So ubiquitous is preservation of "Israeli" control over the Golan Heights in such thinking that Ezra has even suggested that "Israeli"-allied militiamen in south Lebanon be invited to establish a new settlement in the disputed territory. Even renowned "Israeli" hawk Ariel Sharon--the mastermind behind the 1982 invasion-- has announced his support for withdrawal.

Moral Opposition to the Occupation
Opposition to the occupation on moral grounds does exist in "Israel", though not in the political mainstream. Although the far left has sharply criticized "Israeli" intervention in Lebanon, it has little mass appeal. When "Israel" launched a devastating week-long campaign of airstrikes against the inhabitants of south Lebanon in July 1993, a leftist group called "Gush Shalom" (Peace Bloc) organized a demonstration in front of the Press Club in Tel Aviv. According to one journalist present, "the forty or so participants could hardly fill the cafeteria."11

Until recently, moral opposition to the occupation was primarily restricted to secular circles. However, in 1996 an organization called "Women for the Sanctity of Life" was formed by Orthodox Jewish women. Suny Bing, who founded the organization, explained the moral basis of her movement's criticism of the occupation:

"Our pressure to find a solution that will put an end to the killing in Lebanon does not stem from fatigue. On the contrary, our point of departure is a religious-moral one. We are not prepared to accept the terrible cheapening of the value of life, and the brutal and ugly corruption caused us by the continuing occupation."12

Movements rejecting the occupation on moral grounds are likely to proliferate as the "Israeli" government resorts to increasingly oppressive means of counteracting the growing effectiveness of Hizbullah's resistance activities.
