Israeli's Dilemma in The South

The Israeli government should know by now it has no chance of accomplishing any of its political or security objectives in South Lebanon or the West Beqaa. Nevertheless, it continues with an almost suicidal policy. Not only does it refuse to abide by UN Resolution 425 but it is still trying to create a logistical quagmire which it hopes to turn into political advantage. Meanwhile, the bleeding continues in the area, caused exclusively by its occupation, which makes Israeli forces and its allied South Lebanon Army a natural and legitimate target for resistance operations.

The Israelis justify the occupation by claiming a security vacuum would follow a withdrawal. This, they say, warrants political and security guarantees which should precede and succeed any proposed withdrawal.

However, after almost two exhausting decades, this claim has been proven time and again to be false. Large segments of the Israeli society have become convinced of the need to pull out of Lebanon, even if it were the result of a unilateral decision by Israel without negotiations or agreements. Such a unilateral withdrawal would be prompted by a single objective, to stop the bloodshed among Israeli soldiers and thus end an open war which has turned into a lengthy provocation, whose events, as well as its results, are difficult to control, much less to win.

This conviction among a significant sector of Israeli society is no longer limited to marginal political or academic groups, nor to isolated peace activists or leftists. It has emerged as a serious objective, supported by a wide segment of the political, and even the military, establishments. Furthermore , even viewing solely the strictly internal, underlying objectives for such a stance, it would be imprudent to ignore it or to view it as a mere "trick" or another Israeli pressure tactic of maneuver.

The biggest danger, however, is the official policy on South Lebanon of the rightist-religious coalition under the leadership of Benjamin Netenyahu, and the various options which it believes it can still play out. Unlike the policies of the preceding Labour governments of Yitzhak Rabin of his successor, Shimon Peres, which had effectively resigned themselves to the fact they would be unable to separate the Lebanese and the Syrian negotiating tracks )even if they avoided admitting it publicly for understandable internal and regional considerations), it is clear that Netenyahu and his partners in the government still hope to achieve such a separation.

Whether such a policy should take the form of declared objectives such as "Lebanon First", or "Jezzine First", or remain more subtle and conniving , its underlying danger is that it can be carried out only through the internal destabilization of Lebanon, the undermining of its national accord and the frustration of government efforts at local, regional and international levels.

The Israelis know well, having learned from Operation Grapes of Wrath and similar operations, they would be unable to succeed in the current confrontation in the south through traditional, direct, military means. They also know, as a result of many attempts at direct and indirect negotiations in the past six years, the Taif Republic, given the internal and regional forces that drive it, is not about to dismantle its "integral and complementary" ties with Syria. Furthermore, this republic, and what it represents, is fully convinced these ties constitute the only guarantee for its continuity and the only exit for Lebanon from a decade and a half of civil strife and the regional tug-of-war in which it had been caught.
 

 "Large segments of the Israeli society have become convinced of the need to pull out of Lebanon". 
 
The Lebanese government has been successful in avoiding political traps which Israel has often attempted to set. The roots of this success, in its opinion, have been its determination to coordinate with Syria on strategy, security, and policy, and its adherence to the principle of concomitant Syrian and Lebanese negotiating tracks. Israel's failed goal was to get Lebanon to stop resistance activities, to confront the resistance movement, and to disarm it under the banner of proving the government's ability to maintain peace in the south, in the hope that would convince Israel to withdraw.

Why, then, does Israel insist on remaining in the south without legal or logical justification? Why does it pursue a policy which appears to lack a minimum level of reasoning in dealing with the situation there, and with the Lebanese situation in general?

The Israeli government has simply not yet lost hope of achieving its objectives in the south and in Lebanon. The difference, however, is that this government is trying to avoid the mistakes of its predecessor by approaching these objectives through "non-conventional" means which would avoid the need to get involved in wide-scale and costly military and political confrontations.
 

"Israel must be made to realize that the only solution to its dilemma in the south is a complete and unconditional withdrawal from Lebanon".
 
Israel is clearly determined to carry out this new (old) policy and has already embarked on it. This is, perhaps, the best explanation for the recent events in Jezzine and Kfour, the bombing of Sidon, and the resurgence of talk about displacing certain inhabitants and replacing them with "others", as well as new fears of rising sectarian sensitivities and other manifestations which took the Lebanese, at least mentally, back to the era of civil strife when that era was thought to have been long gone.

Simply stated, Israel is now trying to accomplish what it has failed to achieve over many years. It is trying to destroy Lebanon from within, to turn it into a "strategic loop-hole" through which it can penetrate the heart of Syrian and Arab political and strategic security and to further upset the regional balance of powers.

Most importantly, Israel must be made to realize that the only solution to its dilemma in the south is a complete and unconditional withdrawal from Lebanon. Security will come after the withdrawal.

Adib F Farha is a Lebanese businessman
 

by Adib  Farha
