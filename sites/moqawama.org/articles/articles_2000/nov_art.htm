Hizbullah broadens its  airwaves battlefront
Al-Manars satellite television channel moves into Palestinian homes for an extra 14 hours a day(1)

As Arab media mobilizes to support the Palestinian intifada, Hizbullahs al-Manar has joined the struggle through an intensive campaign to make inroads in the Palestinian territories and the Arab world.
The risky decision to increase al-Manars satellite broadcasting time from four to 18 hours a day, supported by the same number of staff, has been a tremendous and burdening task, but one that has paid off.
Al-Manar first began its four-hour satellite broadcast on the eve of the May 24 liberation of the South, because we wanted to transmit the message of this liberation to the Arab world, said Nayef Krayyem, the stations General Manager.
Our message was clear: to encourage the Arabs and show them a model of resisting the enemy. Our main target was specifically the Palestinians living in occupied Palestinian territories, Krayyem said.
After the intifada broke out again Sept. 28, Krayyem decided to increase satellite broadcasting hours to encourage and show support and solidarity with the Palestinians.
The feedback from Arabs, particularly Palestinians, was beyond expectation.
We moved to become one of the first satellite stations watched by Arabs during this time and the main reason behind this success is Hizbullahs credibility, he said.
The 18-hour satellite broadcast is solely dedicated to airing anything related to the intifada. Besides broadcasting Arab nationalist songs, breaking news updates and on-the-hour news bulletins, throughout the day television hosts take telephone calls from Arab supporters expressing their views.
The station has also been airing provocative clips in both Arabic and Hebrew. One that has been on for two weeks now shows photos of the three Israeli soldiers and the colonel Hizbullah captured last month. The numbers 1, 2 and 3 are written on top of the pictures of the three soldiers and the sign +1 is written on top of the picture of the Israeli colonel.
An empty frame appears with a question mark, hinting that more such captures might be on the way. Below the frames, in both Arabic and Hebrew, Hizbullah says Barak, count your soldiers every Sabbath.
Krayyem said that the station will soon begin broadcasting bulletins in Hebrew. The idea is not new, but Krayyem was confident it would to happen within a month. A more long-term plan also includes airing a news bulletin in English.
Al-Manar did not only give the Arabs the chance to make contact with Hizbullah but also gave them an opportunity to express their views on regional developments, he said.
The station has also become an effective tool for showing Palestinians how much Arab support they enjoy. Weve got thousands of phone calls and e-mails from Palestinians telling us how much al-Manar is giving them determination to continue to fight, he said.
Viewers enthusiasm was not only limited to expressing support over the phone. Hizbullahs policy against Israel and the Palestinian Authority prevents it basing a correspondent in Palestine. But this has not hurt the coverage.
Every single one of our viewers has become a correspondent. Wed receive calls from someone to tell us that a Palestinian was just killed, or an Israeli attack was being staged, and wed immediately broadcast the news, becoming the quickest news provider to the Arabs about the attacks on Palestinians, said Krayyem.
The secret of al-Manars sudden popularity is not only news. Were addressing the heart of the Arab nation, reflecting the Arabs determination to not accept the current situation anymore.
Krayyem explained that Arab viewers could see that al-Manar was transmitting a completely different message from that of other stations. Were carrying the message of facing the Israeli enemy. We consider the plight of the Palestinian people as our own and we consider ourselves a basic part of this struggle.
Another small but meaningful decision on al-Manars part is its listing the times of its shows according to Al-Quds and Greenwich Mean Time.
Although Hizbullah is proud of the stations accomplishments, which Krayyem maintained were beyond expectations, the party official solemnly acknowledged the huge responsibility shouldered by the television station.
Our main goal is to give all Arabs the opportunity to speak against the Israeli occupation from a credible platform. Its not our goal to criticize anyone else because we have a deeper objective to achieve, he said.
As a result, al-Manars policy is to prevent viewers shouting obscenities about Arab leaders. Were not a station for expletives. We take objective criticism anytime. This is not a problem, but not more, he said.
He explained that al-Manars policy is not to promote a battle between Arab people and their rulers, regardless of Hizbullahs view of Arab leaders.
We dont want to lose sight of our main target, which is fighting the enemy. If were able to show Arab leaders that their people think strongly against Israel, we may eventually achieve something. However, attacking the leaders will do more harm than good.
Al-Manars satellite logo, which mirrors the stations logo with the words The Arab and Muslim Channel below it, is no coincidence.
We make it a point to have Arab guests from all political affiliations, so youll see an Arab nationalist expert, then an Arab communist one. We believe its our strength to link all these Arab potentials and direct them to one target: the Israeli enemy, he said.
Krayyem proudly recounted how, last week, when Israels Channel One television was interviewing a Palestinian official at his home, the television behind him showed he was watching Al-Manar.
The satellite channel is received in all Arab and European countries and Krayyem is certain Israel has people monitoring the station 24 hours a day.
It seems obvious that, whatever information Hizbullah intends to broadcast about the captured Israeli soldiers, it will first be aired on al-Manar. So weve forced the Israelis to rely on us to know whats happening, he said, confirming that the party has the capture of the three Israeli soldiers on video.

 

For many liberals, the Palestinians are getting what they deserve(2)

Half-way to Al-Quds, Simon began telling me about his Israeli war service. At 73, his army life was over but he had fought in 1967 and 1973 and ended up in Beirut in 1982, landing on the beaches north of Sidon, leaving before the Sabra and Chatila massacres. Mercifully, there was no talk of "terrorists", only of peace, and when his wife asked why the Palestinians should not have Arab east Al-Quds as the capital of their new state  and this, remember, just four weeks after the death of the Oslo agreement  I wondered if there was not an undiscovered Israel.

Our minibus was climbing the hill into Al-Quds from Tel Aviv when Simon's wife she suddenly asked me about Mohammed al-Durah, the 12-year-old shot by Israeli soldiers as he cowered in his father's arms in Gaza: "What was he doing at the time?" she asked sharply. Why was he on the street?"

In fact, he had accompanied his father to buy a car  because the father had to walk to the Gaza border at two each morning for permission to work in Israel  and had been returning home when they were trapped by gunfire. But I understood the implication of these questions at once: if Mohammed did not have good reason to be on the streets of Gaza at the time, if he had been participating in a demonstration, then maybe the little boy had got what he deserved.

This odd disconnection comes in many forms. When I arrived at Ben Gurion airport last week, the young immigration officer cheerfully asked me to remember that Israel was a small country threatened by people outside who wanted to take it". I suggested that the Palestinians had been living in "Palestine"  or modern-day Israel  for generations, that they were not "outside" save those who had been expelled from their lands by Israel, and that United Nations Security Council Resolution 242 might, in the end, bring a real peace. "What is 242?" she wanted to know.

How strange that 242  which is shorthand for any Palestinian who wants to refer to the UN resolution demanding an Israeli withdrawal from occupied lands  would mean nothing to a young, educated Israeli immigration officer. Oslo, of course, had a meaning for her, the very word used with such contempt by the Palestinians of the occupied territories. Deir Yassin, the scene of the worst massacre of Arabs in the 1948 war, would not. The same disconnection creeps into the Israeli press and, of course, into the foreign media.

Israelis are invariably "murdered" or "lynched" by Palestinians  usually a perfectly accurate description  but Palestinians are inevitably "killed in clashes": the implication being that they may have been killed by their own people.

Reuters news agency has followed the Israeli line. Its Monday report on killings by Israelis in the occupied territories refers to Palestinians "wounded in stone-throwing clashes" and "killed in earlier clashes", adding that the "clashes" began on 28 September, that the "clashes have halted peace talks" and that Israeli Arabs have complained about "the killing of their brethren in clashes". But when on the same day an Israeli security guard was shot dead, his killer was accurately described as a "suspected Palestinian gunman".

On the same day, the Associated Press reported: "Palestinian shooting attacks on Jewish settlements" but spoke of a Palestinian who was, of course, merely shot "in clashes".

This double standard of Israeli and foreign reporting finds its way into the most unexpected places. Staying at the King David Hotel in Jewish West Al-Quds, I found myself watching the hotel's home-video history. As British military headquarters at the end of the Mandate, the hotel was blown up by Menachem Begin's bombers. So what did the hotel video tell me about an act which, if perpetrated by Palestinians , would be described by Israelis as an act of bestial terrorism? The video proudly boasts that the King David is "the only hotel in the world that was bombed by a future prime minister" and refers to the perpetrators  whose victims included 41 Arabs, 28 British and 17 Jews  as "activists" who were dedicated to their cause.

Only now, five weeks into the "Al-aqsa" intifada, has the Al-Quds Post given an Arab  George Abu al-Zulof  the opportunity of countering the vicious claims that Palestinians deliberately use their children as human-shield "martyrs" during stone-throwing demonstrations.

But at least the Israeli press is more self-critical than Palestinian newspapers. It was an Israeli paper which first pointed out that the country was in no position to claim that Yasser Arafat was no longer a fit "peace partner" when Israel itself has a lame duck Prime Minister who is asking Ariel Sharon, leader of the right-wing Likud party, to join his government. And the brave reports of Amira Hass in Haaretz provide Israelis with a vivid and accurate description of the suffering of Palestinians under occupation.

Similarly, the occupied territories. Since Mr Arafat's major towns are now in his own hands, Israelis repeatedly tell me, I have no right to call the West Bank "occupied" in my reports to The Independent. So what, I ask myself, is that checkpoint on the edge of Nablus that prevents its peoples incinerating their garbage outside the town? And who are the soldiers who repeatedly tell me in the West Bank that I cannot go down any of half a dozen roads to Jenin or Ramallah? Are these Swiss soldiers? Is it the Burmese army that is shooting stone-throwers in Nablus and Gaza?

When my minibus reached Al-Quds, Simon and his wife said goodbye and they and their friends prepared to set off for home. They were all liberals, all seeking peace, all prepared to allow the Palestinians a real state.

Hizbullah, Israel fight cyberwar(1)

 Now that daily bloodshed has seemingly ceased on the Lebanese-Israeli borders, a new kind of fierce war is raging on another battlefront: the Internet.

It began almost a month ago with an all-out cyber offensive by Israelis and their supporters around the world against sites of Hizbullah, triggering a counter-attack by Arabs worldwide.

A Web blitz of millions of hits crashed the two Internet sites of Hizbullah, which had spearheaded the guerrilla war against Israel until the Jewish state's May 24 Israeli pullout from southern Lebanon,

The cyberwar heated up also because of Hizbullah's fervent encouragement to the Palestinian intifada, or uprising, against Israel and the Palestinians' open admiration for Hizbullah guerrillas.

"We were used to such cyber attacks whenever Hizbullah staged successful or large guerrilla operations, but never has it been so harsh," Hizbullah webmaster Ali Ayoub told AFP.

"The hits came mainly from Israel, but they were also closely coordinated with attacks emanating from the United States and Europe," said the computer wizz, mixing his Arabic fluently with cyber-jargon in English.

Ayoub said "tens of thousands of hits came via the e-mail provider America Online. Any provider notices that tens of thousands of e-mail bombings are being launched through it, but for some reason AOL did not do anything."

He explained that the attacks were either "e-mail bombings" in which sites are flooded with large-size mails, some with viruses attached to them, or through a "virtual overwhelming traffic" of the servers.

Inconet, the Lebanese server that Hizbullah was using for its two sites, also published a statement to denounce the Israeli attacks which overloaded the server for several days.

Ayoub said Hizbullah then decided to keep its two sites, which the group had first launched in 1997, closed "temporarily" while opening seven other back-up sites.

"The Israelis want to prevent us from being on the Internet through under-the-belt dealings, but we did not retaliate as an organization," he said.

"It was our supporters, whether Arabs, Muslims or foreigners, who had the idea of a major counter-attack. They rallied from around the world to attack a number of Israeli sites," he said.

The Beirut media dubbed such hits as "cyber-Katyushas," in reference to the rockets that Hizbullah used to maintain a power balance by launching them against northern Israel every time Israel harmed Lebanese civilians.

The counter-attack crashed or crippled for a short period the sites of the Israeli Knesset (parliament), the foreign ministry, the government, the premiership, the Labour party, the central bank and the army, which was forced to employ a US firm that specialises in protection of web sites to upgrade its security.

Israeli foreign ministry spokesman Noam Katz confirmed the attacks to AFP on October 26, saying that parties involved in the attacks were "controlled by the Shiites in the United States."

The next day, a US government agency headed by the FBI, the National Infrastructure Protection Center, warned that the Israeli-Palestinian violence could spark a rising level of cyber attacks that might even hit US Web sites.

The Hizbullah webmaster explained that the group "considers that the Internet is a public domain and a means of expression for everyone."

"We also considered that our presence on the Internet was the most important thing for us, our sole objective is to remain up and running,"he said.

Ayoub said their other aim was to show that Israels mighty military power in the region did not scare the Lebanese, even less Hizbullah which had always maintained that "the enemy withdrew from Lebanon after a harsh defeat by the heroic resistance."

"There is a power balance in this field. All it takes is a computer and brains. Like the Israelis, we proved to everyone that we have the technology and the brains to do it and win this war," he said.

Arab hackers are attempting to divert Israeli funds from buying weapons and ammunition to fight the Palestinians by forcing the Jewish state to invest in repairing and safeguarding internet service providers in Tel Aviv.
In an e-mail sent to The Daily Star, the staff of UNITY website, created to encourage Arab supporters to target Israeli sites in an attempt to down them, announced that phase three of the cyberwar has begun.
Remember, the more money they lose in fixing and strengthening their systems means less money to buy bullets and rockets for use against our children, stated the e-mail, addressed to Arab internet users who have been active in attacking Israeli sites.
Maybe you cant hold a gun and fight, but you can contribute in the struggle by visiting and forwarding the counter attack links, it said, listing the links: defend.unity-news.com and hizbollah.unity-news.com.
Every dollar they lose means one less bullet for the Zionist soldiers ammunition, it means a life of a Palestinian child, the e-mail said.
One of the UNITY staffers, who spoke to The Daily Star on condition of anonymity, said the attacks were simply an act of self-defense.
He also threatened that Israeli e-commerce sites would be attacked if Israelis and their supporters attempt to touch any anti-Zionist site.
Attacking e-commerce sites is listed as phase four in the cyberwar attack. UNITY said the aim would make the Israelis lose millions of dollars in losses in transactions.
The reason phase three of the cyberwar was initiated, the
staff member explained, was because Israelis and their supporters hacked into Hizbullahs Al-Manar Television website last week, combined with a Zionist action against UNITY.

However, Arab hackers have become so effective that even the FBI has warned that the cyber attacks on Israeli sites could spill over to the US.

The FBI said the method of attacks against Israeli websites included automated e-mail floods and high volumes of coordinated requests for web services by pro-Palestinians.
The FBI has recommended certain security measures for government agencies and private businesses. Security officials should be prepared to take appropriate steps to prevent e-mail flood attacks, block source e-mail addresses in the event of a flooding, and ensure that appropriate patches are installed in operating systems to limit vulnerability to other denial-of-service attack methods.

Cyberwar expands to claim first US victim(1)

Arab hackers have targeted the website of a US company that conducts business with Israel ­  the first such attack since the Arab-Israeli cyberwar began three weeks ago.
The US company, Lucent Technologies, was attacked on Thursday by thousands of hackers at the same time. Although the company confirmed it was targeted, it said company security workers were able to fend off the attack before it brought the site down.
The attack came only a few days after an FBI warning that the Arab-Israeli cyberwar could spill over to the United States.
The Lucent website was listed among 13 sites posted on the UNITY website, a site encouraging Arabs to target Israeli sites.
Over the weekend at least six Israeli sites were hacked, among them a commercial firm for strategic consulting, project planning, and website design; a commercial medical company; an Israeli student association; a college; and a religious high school.
The hackers attacking the Israeli sites identified themselves as Gforce Pakistan, which is most probably the same group that hacked an American pro-Israeli lobby group, Aipac, last week.
The attack on the American-Israeli Public Affairs Committee was the first cyberwar case to be comprehensively reported by major US media outlets.
Meanwhile, the Israeli site, www.wizel.com, that initiated the cyberwar three weeks ago by listing Hizbullah and Palestinian sites, has been removed. However, a group calling itself Israel Hackers launched a website on Sunday that has similar functions as www.wizel.com.
Entitled Hackers of Israel Unite, the site encourages the army of Israeli soldiers on the net to search and destroy all of the Arab sites on the net.
The site, which had received 214 visitors by early Sunday evening, tells Israeli supporters that all they need is a computer, a shell, and revenge.
The sites listed to be targeted include the Palestinian Authority site; Hizbullahs Al-Manar Television site; a Palestinian information site, www.sis.gov.ps; Irans Islamic Republic News Agency site; and a Jordanian portal site, www.albawaba.com.
The Israeli sites front page posts a picture of Palestinian leader Yasser Arafat holding sticks of dynamite. Below is a picture of Palestinian children holding guns with Unicefs logo on top. Another animated picture shows Arafat repeatedly ducking target attacks aimed at him.
The site, www.israelhackers. cjb.net, is trying to coordinate attacks based on ICQ channels.
The Israeli Army website was again downed for at least five days, despite seeking AT&T to host it after it was downed by Arab hackers.
Israeli site administrators are kept busy protecting sites from Arab hackers.
The Israeli Army site, for example, keeps going on and off, indicating that it is being attacked and then reloaded by site administrators.
The Israeli sites that were down on Sunday afternoon included the Ministry of Defense, the Ministry of Immigrant Absorption, the Ministry of Industry and Trade, the Ministry of Religious Affairs, and the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange.
Were expecting the battle to heat up from our side in the next few days, a UNITY staffer told The Daily Star.
Our cyberwar wont stop until all the anti-Zionist sites attacked are restored, he said.

The mothers should cut the cord (1)

By Avirama Golan
Nobody was surprised when, reacting to the bloody clashes in the territories, a group of "Israeli" Jewish mothers issued a statement that they were not prepared to sacrifice their children as soldiers in the "Israel" Defense Forces*, which they said was fighting needless battles in Nablus and on Mount Eval. A month later, the first group of "Israeli" Jewish mothers had organized itself, using the name of a now-disbanded group that had called for a withdrawal from Lebanon: Four Mothers. The new Four Mothers organization is demanding that "Israel" pull out of the territories - for the sake of the children.This is an interesting phenomenon. There are those who are saying that this is a welcome development. After all, they reason, in the heart of a militaristic society, whose members invariably see things through the telescopic sights of an army-issue rifle (especially at a time of confrontation or war), a group of courageous mothers has now arisen, and these women are not afraid of clashing with the top brass of the IDF, or with the defense minister, or even with the prime minister while they set new "civic boundaries" and say categorically, "This is as far as we are prepared to go."

A careful study of how "Israeli" politicians from the various political camps behaved during the different phases of the war in Lebanon - their decisions and their policy changes - will prove conclusively that the original Four Mothers movement, which constantly gained in strength and even recruited the fathers of "Israeli" combat soldiers, achieved several impressive media successes, contributed to a more focused debate on "Israel"'s continued presence in Lebanon and, in the final analysis, no doubt accelerated the withdrawal of IDF troops from there.

Actually, it would be no exaggeration to say that the determination of mothers to confront the IDF commanders responsible for sending their children into battle is a self-evident - perhaps even natural - result of the involvement of "Israeli" parents, especially mothers, in the military service of their children. This involvement expresses itself every weekend at the gates of military bases in the form of parcels filled with all sorts of cooked delicacies or cakes, cookies and other sweet goodies. This involvement expresses itself in the traditional "beret treks" (at the end of which novice soldiers are awarded the particular beret - red, green, black, gray, etc. - proudly worn by the members of their unit) when Mom and Dad join their beloved child for the last kilometer of the grueling ordeal, offering words of encouragement, praise and love. This involvement expresses itself in the telephone calls that angry parents make to battalion or brigade commanders, lambasting them for "needlessly bullying" their children.

In fact, it appears that there are no soldiers in "Israel", only "children" or "our children." In "Israel", when children leave home and are inducted into army, their departure, as pointed out by psychologist Professor Amia Lieblich in a brilliant study, does not mean that they are "leaving the nest," which is usually the case in other countries when teenagers move out of their homes. Quite the contrary, Prof. Lieblich defines the military units where "Israeli" inductees serve as distant branches of the traditional warm Jewish family that, nonetheless, effectively controls what goes in that branch.

In any event, the "Israeli" right ecstatically welcomed the creation of the new Four Mothers organization because the rightists regard that movement as additional proof of the hedonism of the left, whose members, interested solely in their own comfort and security and in that of their children, do not consider the price-tag in national terms. This viewpoint is not completely distorted, although it does confuse, and deliberately so, "mothers" with "the left." The left is currently maintaining a stony silence, which is only broken from time to time by a few mumbled, incoherent statements, while the vast majority of leftists have already made their peace with the eternal existence of "clusters of settlements" in the territories. As a result, a major change might take place in the public debate on the future of the territories and on the price that "Israeli" society is paying for continuing to hold on to them and continuing to allow the 200,000 Jewish settlers in those territories to dictate "Israel"'s national agenda. Given the current circumstances, that public debate could wind up being conducted between the following two parties: On the one hand, the military and the security forces, whose poker-faced commanders speak in an abstruse jargon, and, on the other hand, "Israeli" Jewish women, who speak from the heart.

This phenomenon has nothing to do with feminism or social issues. In fact, the opposite is true: A society that listens to what is being said by its female members, speaking from the traditional perspective of a spouse or mother, is actually backward. Women effectively trap themselves when they go along with this assignment of such an exclusive, and traditional, role, and when they make no effort to actively participate in accepted democratic channels in order to reach positions of influence and to use those positions to express their views - irrespective of their motherly or conjugal relationships. Which party will be supported at the ballot box by a woman whose oldest son wants to serve in the army canteen during his inducted period of service and whose youngest son dreams of becoming a member in the elite Duvdevan undercover unit?

The new Four Mothers organization could therefore end up leading the above public debate into even more of a dead end than it is already in right now and reducing even further the rationality and relevance of that debate. If the left adopts these charming mothers and turns them into the new symbol of the struggle for peace, it will lose yet one more opportunity to speak clearly about "Israel"'s social and political priorities and will allow private norms to blur political positions. Moreover, those who are enthusiastic about mothers getting involved in the issue of the territories should remember that the right can always present its own duo of mothers: Daniela Weiss and Miriam Lapidot

 

Battling for the right to speak up (2)

By Tamar Rotem

The Arab students at Ben-Gurion University have not been allowed to organize on campus.
Last week the sound of drumbeats came through the closed doors of lecture halls of Ben-Gurion University of the Negev in Be'er Sheva. Students had read in the newspapers that riots were expected at the universities. They looked uneasily at one another. Had the dire predictions come true?.The noise, it quickly turned out, was part of the festival atmosphere of happenings organized by the Students' Union to mark the first week of studies in the new academic year. Still, there were plenty of strident notes, mainly emanating from the Arab students at the universities. In the wake of the disturbances in and around Arab villages last month, they launched a campaign for recognition of their right to organize and demonstrate on campus. So far, the Arab students have chalked up a few points in their favor, including, for the first time, support from Arab and Jewish lecturers.

Despite the directive issued by the Council for Higher Education barring political activity on all campuses during the first week of studies, Ben-Gurion University last week permitted Arab and Jewish teachers to hold a symposium entitled "Jewish-Arab Equality and Coexistence on Campus and in the State." Some 300 students and lecturers showed up for the event, which was held last Tuesday in the presence of the university's rector, Prof. Nahum Finger.

Among the symposium speakers was Mahmoud Mahamid, the chairman of the Arab Students' Union, an organization that is not officially recognized by the university.

During the symposium many of those present noticed that everything was being documented by video cameras. It turned out that the university's security unit had decided to film those in attendance "for security purposes." A petition is currently being circulated in the university - it has so far been signed by about 20 Jewish and Arab lecturers - demanding that the institution's administration ensure that "this disgraceful act" never be repeated.

Ben-Gurion University always took a relatively conservative position in regard to Arab students and issues relating to freedom of expression, as compared to other institutions of higher learning. Now its approach is apparently about to change, although the episode of the filming of the symposium attests to the ambivalence of the heads of the university toward any such change.

There are few Arab students enrolled at BGU, and this is one of the explanations offered for why they have never constituted a genuine force on the campus, in contrast to the situation at the University of Haifa and the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. There are some 750 Arab students at BGU among a student population of 15,000 or so. The majority of the Arabs (approximately 70 percent) there come from the north of the country.

Some 19 percent of the students (more than 2,000) at the University of Haifa are Arabs, and the proportion in Jerusalem is similar. "In the north they are plugged into their towns and villages, to their source of strength," explains Raif Jumaa, a member of the Culture Committee of the Students' Union at BGU and a resident of the village of Abu Snan in Western Galilee. "Here, the majority of the students are far from home and feel cut off."

The university's regulations do not permit political activity on the grounds of the campus. This effectively neutralizes the Arab students. Whereas other universities (Haifa, Jerusalem, Tel Aviv University) have allocated areas for demonstrations, in Be'er Sheva the place for a meeting or demonstration (even if it refers to the size of the tuition fees) has to be coordinated anew each time. The elections for the Students' Union are also held on the basis of rules that differ from those in the other universities - not on the basis of political parties as is the case on the other campuses, but according to departments of study. However, in the natural course of things the struggles that are waged within the Students' Union generally assume a political character. In other words, there is political activity on campus, but it is concealed.

In contrast to the other universities, where Arab student committees operate freely, at BGU the body that is supposed to look after the well-being of the Arab students functions as a kind of shadow organization. For the most part, its activity is conducted outside the university (with police authorization), sometimes right next to the main entry gate of the campus. At the end of last year, elections to the Arab students' committee were held for the first time, one reason being to consolidate it as a legitimate body that represents the Arab students. Mahmoud Mahamid, who is active in the Hadash party and is doing his M.A. in Hebrew literature, was elected chairman of the committee.

Last week Mahamid wanted to organize a meeting between freshmen and veteran Arab students in order to help the new students to acclimatize. However, Dean of Students Ya'akov Afek initially turned down his request for a room in which to hold the encounter. It was only when lecturers exerted pressure that permission was granted to hold the meeting, albeit under restrictive conditions. "They told us what we could talk about and what we were not allowed to talk about," Mahamid says. "I was forbidden to refer to the violent events or to talk about discrimination and inequality. The next thing you know, they would have written my text for me and told me to read it out verbatim."

Finally a compromise was worked out, and the encounter took place last Wednesday in the presence of the dean of students and the rector. Mahamid did not mention the "violent events" by so much as a word.

Mahamid, 26, who is from Umm el Fahm in the Galilee, has been living in Be'er Sheva for the past seven years. He intends to do his M.A. thesis on Hebrew poetry of the Middle Ages (among the paitanim - the writers of liturgical verse - he likes Ibn Gvirol and Yehuda Halevy, though he is visibly uncomfortable at mentioning this). He thinks the university ban on organizing and demonstrating violates freedom of expression and is an attempt to silence dissent.

Nowadays, many of the lecturers at the university agree with him. In the wake of the disturbances in "Israel" and the territories, a group of lecturers, Jews and Arabs alike, including heads of departments and research institutes such as Dr. Lev Grinberg, who is the head of the Humphrey Center for Social Research, and Dr. Ismail Abu Saad, who heads the Center for Research of Bedouin Society, spoke out against the ban on campus demonstrations, and activity for freedom of expression was launched. Last week's conference on coexistence and equality was the first manifestation of the new thrust. Mahamid hopes to operate openly this year, with the backing of lecturers, in an effort to put a stop to the discrimination he alleges in the awarding of scholarships and dorm space.

Dr. Amnon Raz Karkutzkin, from BGU's Department of History, maintains that the university's demand for an apolitical campus is unreasonable, particularly in the existing reality in "Israel". "The right for party activity by Arabs is legitimate," he says. "An Arab students' committee is something elementary. Obviously, in a body consisting of a national minority, national and political issues will come up for discussion, because for the Arabs even the question of dorms is a political one, and the demand for recognition is also legitimate." At the same time, he notes, an incipient change in the university's approach is discernible. "The fact that the university lent its auspices to a meeting with the participation of the Arab Students' Committee was important. It helped calm passions and also contributed to the creation of a legitimate political dialogue. That was a step toward the recognition of the Arab Students' Committee."

"This is the most open campus in terms of relations between Jewish and Arab lecturers," says Dr. Tabath Abu Ras, from the Department of Geography, "but it is the worst in terms of freedom of expression. Even in Iran universities permit students to express themselves in every sphere." Not many at the university know that 20 years ago, Abu Ras, who is today a political geographer and a member of Hadash who assists the Arab Students' Committee, was expelled from the campus for engaging in political activity. He is now in his second year as a teacher. When he was studying for his B.A. and founded an Arab-Jewish students' movement called Campus in Be'er Sheva, he was expelled. He sued the university over the expulsion; the court sought to reach a compromise in which he would resume his studies on the condition he undertook not to engage in political activity. He decided to drop out of school. He later studied in the United States.

Now, Abi Ras says that it is important for him to point out that Jewish and Arab lecturers have been acting together. Next spring the Arab students will put their case to the university's Board of Governors. "It will not be so terrible for the university to allocate an area for political activity," he says. "We could agree on controlled political activity, and of course whatever is done would have to be within the framework of the law."

Next week BGU will host a conference on the right to demonstrate. Among those expected to attend are the civil rights attorney Avigdor Feldman, Prof. Kenneth Mann from Tel Aviv University, and activists from the Adalah organization for Arab minority rights in "Israel". Meanwhile the Rector, Prof. Finger, ardently defends the university's position. A students' union that is based not on political parties but on "representation by professions and disciplines" is preferable, he says, as it enables the problems that affect students to be dealt with more effectively. The university's approach, he says, helps it preserve "a bit of civility" and enables it to be "the only sane place around."

The university, he points out, does not prevent social or cultural organizing. "If the Arab students wanted to establish an Omar Khayam fan club, would we object?
