December Saturday 02 -2000:

Television news is secret weapon of the intifada(6)

To the casual visitor, Manar Television is a run-of-the-mill professional television station, its newsroom humming with reporters, its studios hushed for hourly news reports, its state-of-the-art editing suites flanked by batteries of satellite screens, the tape library computercatalogued on dozens of shelves.

But you need only read the posters on the newsroom wall to realise this is no ordinary station. "We are with you  we will not forget you," they say, a photograph of a Palestinian boy with a slingshot superimposed on the bearded face of the chairman of the Lebanese Hizbollah, Sayed Hassan Nasrallah.

For Manar (Lighthouse in Arabic) belongs to Lebanon's Hizbollah guerrilla army. It is the secret weapon of the Palestinian intifada against "israeli" occupation, the loyal supporter of armed resistance, devoting at least half its 24-hour-a-day satellite broadcasting to the battle between Palestinians and Israelis in the West Bank and Gaza.

Live news reports, video-clips and "resistance" songs, 50 specially composed for the intifada, are slotted between quiz shows, documentaries and calls to prayer for the millions of Palestinians who tune in to Manar's satellite channel.

Nayef Krayim, the chairman of the multimillion-dollar operation in Beirut's southern suburbs, is a former guerrilla who started at Manar as a presenter seven years ago.

"Our message is very simple," he says. "Hizbollah has a lesson to teach about how land should be liberated: the only way to achieve your goal is by armed resistance, not just by throwing stones."

Manar regularly shows combat footage of its battle with "israeli" troops, a struggle that led to "Israel's" retreat from almost all of southern Lebanon in May. Mr Krayim hopes his Palestinian viewers will get the message.

Indeed, the Hizbollah casts a critical eye on the Palestinian-"israeli" mini-war, noting with scorn what it regards as the unsoldierly use of Palestinian weapons. "We think it is very bad when the Palestinians shoot their guns at funerals," Mr Krayim says. "Random firing is very unprofessional. We prefer they use their bullets somewhere else."

Enemy of "Israeli" though Manar is, the station plans to start a Hebrew language service at the end of the Holy fasting month of Ramadan, for "israeli" viewers. "We have graduates from "israeli" prisons who speak fluent Hebrew," Mr Krayim says with a grim smile.

"We already have a programme about the "israeli" media presented by an ex-prisoner called Adel Termos who translates from Hebrew. He does live translations from Hebrew into Arabic when [the "israeli" Prime Minister] Ehud Barak gives a press conference."

When I dropped by, Ghada Assaf, whose modest hejab did not obscure her obvious beauty, was reading an objective account of the "israeli" election call, much of it culled from Western news agencies.

Most of the male reporting staff  there are 40 journalists on Hizbollah's payroll  have been guerrillas. "It's harder to run a news service than a military operation," one said. "Here you've got to be responsible for political content as well as split-second timing. There's a lot of tension in running a newsroom."

Ali Haj Youssef from Baalbek is the duty editor and I cannot resist asking him if he does not wonder whether a large "israeli" aircraft will not one day come and drop a very big bomb on his station. "I'm not worried," he says. "If I become a martyr for our message, I don't have a problem with that." Which is not exactly the reaction you would get on The Independent's newsdesk. Then again, Manar's message is highly exclusive.

"Some Palestinians did ask us to produce a programme that teaches teenagers how to make explosive devices," Mr Krayim says. "We said that wouldn't be very appropriate on television." When I suggested one reason for his refusal could be the excuse it might give the Israelis to bomb the place, he gives me the long, penetrating Hizbollah look that usually means yes.

When I ask where the money comes from to run this operation  I suspect that Iran provides much of the cash  Mr Krayim raises his hands as if invoking the generosity of God.

In the days of its all-out battle with the "israeli" army in southern Lebanon, 40 per cent of Manar's production was coverage of the local war, including fearful clips of Hizbollah attacks on "israeli" artillery positions, the video-crews running in flak jackets alongside the guerrillas to get their footage.

On the wall of an editing room are photographs of Bakr Haider Ahmed and Behjet Dakroub, two Manar reporters "martyred" on consecutive days during "Israel's" 1993 bombardment of southern Lebanon.

The station's marble floors, oak security doors and staff canteen  firmly locked during Ramadan  speak of a conglomerate that intends to go on broadcasting long after Middle East wars have ended.

"But the war is not going to end before four million Palestinians have returned to their homes," Mr Krayim says. "Only then can we talk about the end of the war. We have a message and it is about Islam, not about war. This is a modern and civilised message.

"After 18 years of resistance, we finally reached the "israeli" border at Khiam," Mr Krayim adds. "But after the liberation of southern Lebanon, nothing is impossible. We are not going to need 18 more years. That's a conviction."
