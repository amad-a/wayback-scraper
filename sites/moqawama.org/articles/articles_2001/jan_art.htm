January Wednesday 03 -2001:

Al-Manar molds itself to changing situation
We used to be the television of the resistance, stations general manager tells Warren Singh-Bartlett. Now we are the television of the intifada(1)

When the Israelis finally pulled out of most of southern Lebanon last year, it was clear that Al-Manar Television was about to undergo some radical changes of its own.
As Hizbullahs media outlet and the voice of the resistance, anywhere up to 40 percent of the stations daily output was focused on the occupation.
News bulletins, political commentary, and announcements of martyrs and civilian casualties, supplemented by clips of resistance activities shot on site by Manars intrepid flack-jacketed, camouflaged cameramen, brought the battle home in an uncompromising fashion.
But it is doubtful if last May anyone could have predicted the extent of changes to come.
By the end of last summer, Manar had been granted a satellite license and, as post-liberation euphoria was subsiding at home, the station began broadcasting the Lebanese example to the rest of the Arab World.
Then the intifada erupted and Manar ­ which means lighthouse in Arabic ­ found a new purpose. We used to be the television of the resistance, said general manager Nayef Krayim. Now we are the television of the intifada.
He isnt exaggerating.
Within a matter of weeks, the stations exhaustive coverage and proudly pro-Palestinian slant had made it a prime choice throughout the West Bank and Gaza, and earned it an enthusiastic following in countries across in the region.
While most stations confined their coverage of the conflict to news bulletins and tear-stained tributes to Al-Quds, Manar opted for substance over spirit.
In addition to its own messages of support and musical tributes, some 50 tunes composed to date, the station offered endlessly looped footage of the resistances ousting of Israel, a kind of how to campaign that is intended to instruct as much as it is to inspire.
We wanted to tell the Palestinians they are not alone, that Israel can be defeated, said  Hossein Nabulsi, Hizbullahs press relations officer.
 We also wanted to make the Arab world wake up and realize what is happening.
Manar has come a long way since it first began broadcasting in 1991. At the time, the stations message was just one of the many differing opinions populating the pre-1997 broadcast shakeup of the airwaves and certainly not one everyone in Lebanon wanted to hear. Aimed solidly at a Shiite audience, it offered a muscular mixture of revolution and religion.
Today the format is somewhat different. Manar viewers may not get the usual satellite servings of US sitcom fare or the song and dance star-studded extravaganzas of terrestrial Lebanese TV ­ neither of which meet the stations strict moral guidelines ­ but they do now get in-house programming aimed at women and children, documentaries, sports coverage, even the odd game show. And these days, even Hollywood schlockbusters are screened ­ without an accompanying commentary on Western decadence.
If this sets Manar apart from its fellow broadcasters, then so much the better: Were not here to compete with other broadcasters, explained Nabulsi. Were here to provide an alternative.
Backed by wealthy expatriate Lebanese donors, increasing commercial receipts from the sale of its shows, and funds from various Iranian community organizations, Manar has blossomed into a multi-million dollar operation. The sparkling new IKEA-chic headquarters in Haret Hreik, guarded by resistance gunmen, houses several hundred employees, a prayer room, a cafeteria, and several floors of studios fitted with state-of-the-art editing and production suites.
Aside from the pictures of Sayyed Hassan Nasrallah, ass-orted Iranian ayatollahs, and posters extolling the virtues of liberation through armed resistance, and this could be the headquarters of almost any television station in the world.
But under the makeover, Manar remains a rebel at heart.
Like many of the stations employees, Krayims own involvement in the resistance began with hands-on experience. After hanging up his combat helmet, Krayim transferred his struggle from the occupied zone to the airwaves.
Everyone here considers themselves part of the resistance. Everyone has a role in liberating the land, whether they are in the military or the media.
Now Manar has decided to take this message straight to the people it refers to as the settlers. Later this year, Israeli viewers will be able to turn on and tune into Hizbullahs forthcoming assault: Manar TV in Hebrew. Its real psychological warfare, Nabulsi admitted with a cheeky grin. We arent doing this to show off. We want to get into every settlers mind and affect Israeli public opinion.
The idea is not a new one.
We first thought of it in 1996 during the Grapes of Wrath, said Krayim. The Israelis broadcast threats in Arabic, telling people to give up and abandon their homes or suffer, so we decided to use a similar tactic to get the settlers to pressure their government into leaving Lebanon.
During the Israeli occupation, Manar broadcast repeated messages in Hebrew aimed at demoralizing Israeli soldiers in the occupied zone and explaining to Israeli civilians that bombs dropped on their Lebanese counterparts would lead directly to Katyusha rockets falling on settlements over the border.
Occasional Hebrew broadcasts continued throughout the occupation and were stepped up as it became clear that Israel was going leave. As the death toll rose inexorably, Manar launched its famous Whos Next? campaign, a constantly updated photo gallery of the latest Israeli casualties that ended with a blank space and a large question mark over the silhouette of the next casualty-to-be.
Screened in both Hebrew and Arabic, Whos Next? was clearly designed to increase pressure within Israel to end the occupation and according to Krayim, created a media frenzy.
We know from monitoring the Israeli press that their government is not happy with us ­ they have blown up our transmission towers in the past, he explained with obvious relish. I wish we could start these broadcasts today.
But viewers in Tel Aviv will have to wait just a little bit longer for an alternative to CNN. Krayim is not prepared to be specific about the services launch date, but he places it shortly after the start of Manars second new addition; an English-language service, which is due to begin broadcasting in February.
Given that in Lebanon Hebrew ranks somewhere after Sumerian as a second language of choice, the scarcity of home-grown Hebrew-speakers was surprisingly not a inhibitor in putting a team together. Finding presenters was relatively easy thanks to the large numbers of Lebanese citizens detained in Israeli or Israeli-run prisons during the occupation. Manar presenter Adel Tormos, a former detainee himself, already does so in Inqilab, his regular round-up of the Israeli media.
But are Israelis really likely to tune in? While the station makes no attempt to hide its anti-Israel bias, its news bulletins are scrupulously accurate, a fact even the Israeli media has acknowledged. Nabulsi believes an audience is out there and points to the fact that Israeli TV regularly carries footage culled from Manars broadcasts.
Krayim believes interest could lie in the opportunity to understand what the other side is saying. Not that being able to understand the underlying message is necessarily going to make it easier to hear.
Jews who originate in the Middle East have the right to live here, says Krayim, but we will tell those who came from Europe they should get ready to go back to their homes.
Beyond the region, the station is out to win the hearts and minds of the world. Last December, Manar extended coverage to Africa, Europe, and North America and by the end of next month, viewers in Latin America will be able to tune in too.
Hence the English-language broadcasts. This service is aimed at two separate audiences; second- or third-generation immigrants who would like to stay informed about the Middle East but who cant speak Arabic and, more importantly, the world public at large.
Much more than the Hebrew broadcasts, the English-language service will be Manars principal platform. We have to address Western public opinion directly, says Krayim. We have to explain to them our point of view, otherwise they will never understand what is happening in this region.

January Tuesday 09 -2001:

 Inside job irks Hizbullah webmaster(2)
Hacker targets resistance, but is enemy closer than we think? Warren Singh-Bartlett investigates

The online intifada is not over yet. After the flurry of cyberstrikes last October and November during the first months of the current uprising ­ when websites in Israel, Palestine, Lebanon and eventually the United States were temporarily shut down by politically motivated hackers ­ the electronic rebellion appeared to be on the wane.
Then just after the Christmas holidays Hizbollah.org, a site that has suffered more than its fair share of attacks over the last few months, was hit again.
But this time, the attack was different. Malek Hossein, the man responsible for keeping Hizbullah online, believes the site was not disrupted by Israeli hackers but by employees at Interland.com, the Atlanta-based internet service provider (ISP) that has hosted the Hizbullah site for over a year.
Pointing to the nature of the disruption ­ the site was stripped of all its information and reduced to a page full of links that led nowhere ­ Hossein says this kind of job could only have been pulled off by someone with full access to the companys computers.
Having a website is a little like having a room filled with information, he explained. We didnt lose a file or two. What happened in our case was more like someone had come along and taken the entire room.
But could it really be that Interland, who bill themselves as the people who make the web work for you, are actually making the web work against Hizbullah? Would sabotaging a single website, regardless of its content, really be worth an ISP running the risk of ruining its reputation?
If this was an inside job, perhaps the work of a politically motivated employee acting on his or her own, wouldnt any responsible company want to find out what had happened and attempt to explain themselves to the customer? And why couldnt this have been the work of an outside hacker rather than an Interland employee? After all, hackers can and do wreak enormous damage on websites and databases all over the world; passwords can be stolen, viruses can be planted and files can be deleted.
Unfortunately, no one at Interland, which is based in Atlanta, was available to comment on these questions on Monday afternoon. Nor for that matter was anyone available for comment last Wednesday, Thursday or Friday.
The employee who answered the phone at the technical support department said the company was experiencing a post-Christmas backlog and restricted himself to saying that his department was unaware of any disruptions to their server over the holiday period but promised that he would look into the matter.
Hossein does not pretend to have the answers but says that for him, the clincher was the manner in which the ISP dealt, or rather did not deal with the problem.
The way they handled everything showed they werent really serious, he said. The (technical) support department just kept making jokes saying, we dont understand you or what do you mean? and we never got a reply to any of our e-mails.
He is adamant that the attack bears all the marks of an inside job. Were almost 100 percent sure, he said. If it had been hackers, I think the company would definitely have replied to our enquiries.
Insider? Outsider? Only Interland really knows and right now, they are not saying. In a sense, the matter is already academic. Within a day of the problem going uncorrected, the website had been moved, at first to the Moqawama website (www.moqawama.org) and then once space on a new ISP had been found, back to its original address (www.hizbollah.org).
At the moment the new site is still on a US-based server. Hossein says he plans to move Hizbollah.org to a Lebanese server in the future and while this would not make the site immune to sabotage, it would make it easier to monitor.
If this latest attack does mark a new phase in the online intifada, Zionist websites might want to start taking precautions of their own, as a recent press-release from moqawama.org pointed out that the incident will not pass by forgotten or (go) unregistered for our future plans  As they say, patience is a virtue.
