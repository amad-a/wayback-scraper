Return to Haddatha: a nightmare laid to rest
 
    Douglas Roberts is the Middle East Correspondent for The Voice of America. Based in Cairo, Roberts has more than 20 year's experience reporting on the region, with much of that time spent in Lebanon, covering the various bouts of fighting during the civil war and "Israel's" invasions.

    Roberts returned to Lebanon in March. He was here ostensibly on a reporting assignment. He wrote this for The Daily Star.

Just about every hack I know has a Lebanon story; some terrifying incident, preferably laced with the journalist's favourite sick humour, that can be trotted out at parties to impress one's friends. My own tale has its full share of terror, but after 20 years of reflection, what I remember most is the lesson in humanity learned from a dirt-poor tobacco farmer named Mohammed Fadil. It happened in a small village facing the western sector of "Israel's" occupation zone.

Haddatha, March 1978 - the three Fatah guerrillas - two men and a dark attractive young woman with a chequered keffiyeh draped around her neck - were lounging by the roadside at the northern end of the village, munching on freshly-baked bread, just delivered from the Palestine Liberation Organisation base in Tyre.

It was an incongruously bucolic scene on this day - the third day of "Israel's" invasion.
The guns had fallen silent.

A warm sun shone down on the rolling countryside and a gentle spring breeze chased the white clouds overhead and wafted through the wild flowers astride the road.

The Palestinians seemed friendly enough as a car-load of journalists drove up to their checkpoint. There were four of us that day: myself, Ned Temko of United Press International, George Semerdjian, an an-Nahar photographer, and David Hirst, The Guardian's venerable Middle East correspondent.

The guerrillas told us that Haddatha was calm for the moment after heavy fighting earlier in the day. The "Israelis", they claimed, had lost three tanks in the battle. We were given permission to check out their story.

As we reached the first house in Haddatha, we parked the car and walked into the village. It appeared completely deserted.

A dead donkey lay in the centre of the narrow village street, its head all but severed from its body. Several of the houses had been damaged by shell fire. A large blood smear adorned one wall; all that remained, as we learned later, of one of the village women. Suddenly, a face appeared at one of the windows; then another, and another. Soon we were surrounded by the villagers, many of them in tears, fear etched on their faces, pleading for help we could not provide.

The village had been under sustained "Israeli" artillery fire for most of the previous night. There have been several deaths, and many of the wounded remained in their homes, with no help in sight. They wanted most of all to show us the village school, built with their own hands and only just completed. It had taken direct hits, and as we crunched across broken glass from classroom to classroom, the shelling began again.

We raced for the basement, and sheltered in the lavatory stalls. The tank and artillery fire lasted 20 minutes. When it died down the villages raced back to their homes.
We weren't quick enough.

The tanks opened up again, and we dived over a low garden wall while Semerdjian ran to the car. Somehow, he made it back to Beirut, believing we had been killed. We were huddled under a tarpaulin within the walls of a small herb garden. The shelling continued throughout the day. Late in the day, "Israeli" ground troops moved into the village, loosing off a sustained barrage of rifle and small arms fire. What they were shooting at, we never knew. It was several more hours before we realised that they had gone. Soon after nightfall, we climbed out of our hiding place. There was a bizarre delay while Hirst combed his hair. We walked down the lane and into the village.

Attracted by a ray of light, we headed towards a basement stable, home to a couple of sheep and a cow. A narrow stairway took us up to the living quarters. It was there that we first met Mohammed. He is a lean, wiry man with a bristling black moustache and a permanent stubble on his chin. An older man lay on a mattress, his thigh heavily bandaged. Hirst, a fluent Arabic speaker, was able to converse with Mohammed, while Temko and I sat on the floor, trembling, exhausted and still terrified.

Mohammed seemed to sense our terror - perhaps it mirrored his own. He brought us plates of tuna and salad and sat by us while we ate, talking soothingly all the while.

He gave us blankets, and we slept on bales of sweet-smelling tobacco. We woke soon after dawn, determined to find our way back to Beirut. Mohammed walked with us to the edge of the village. It was then that we saw the "Israelis".

A unit of combat troops, with tanks and APC's, was deployed on a hilltop. We waved at them. They waved back, and we started to climb up. Mohammed held us back, his eyes glistening with fear. After several minutes, we persuaded him to come with us, in the hope we could help him negotiate a safe passage that would allow the villagers to head north toward Beirut.

The "Israelis" greeted us warmly enough. But for Mohammed, it was an altogether different reception. They pulled him roughly to one side, and began to beat him.

We screamed protests and abuse, but to no avail. "You don't know these people," snapped one "Israeli", "any one of them could be a terrorist."

Finally, the unit's commander, Maj Dayan (nephew of the former defense minister), showed up and he put a stop to the beating.

    Dayan said we could not head north, and our only exit was south to "Israel". As we left in the back of an "Israeli" jeep, I caught a final glimpse of Mohammed, walking slowly back down the hill.

I remember feeling that I hadn't thanked him sufficiently. That night, I had the first of many dreams about him.

Haddatha, March 1998 - I returned to the village in the company of The Daily Star's Nick Blanford.
It didn't take us long to find Mohammed Fadil. He was working on a new terrace wall when we drove up. We recognised each other almost instantly, collapsed into each other's arms, tears in our eyes.

Mohammed's wife gave us tea and baklava in the sparsely-furnished living room of his home. A young daughter played on the floor while we talked.

Little has changed in Mohammed's life in 20 years. The government's tobacco regie is paying him a little more for his crop, but the harvest remains an uncertain endeavor. On the hilltop just a few hundred metres from his home, SLA gunners look down on his fields.

"They shell us all the time". says Mohammed.

"We never know when it's safe to go out."

An Irish unit of Unifil is providing emergency medical assistance to the villagers and other humanitarian aid, but there is nothing they can do about the firing. Mohammed has tried on several occasions to leave Haddatha. One of his sons found employment at a bottling plant in Beirut, but despite repeated trips to the capital, Mohammed has found no work or assistance there.

"What can we do?" he asks, "we have no choice but to stay where we are.

"The "Israelis" have turned this into a place of evil," says the farmer, "we can't move, we can't work our fields, we can't really do anything.

"It's been like this for 20 years," he adds, "and it will only end when the "Israelis" leave." Asked what has been the worst moment of the past two decades, he merely shrugs. "Each day has been worse than the last," he says, "and each has been more difficult." "And there is no hope," he adds, "as long as we are under "Israel's" cannons and bombs."
