Rekindling the Lebanon debate

By Zvi Barel February 15,1998

If Nikolai Rapaport had been an ordinary soldier, the kind whose death does not trigger social or religious controversy, it is possible that the incident in which he was killed would have reignited the debate over whether "Israel" should leave Lebanon. After all, Nikolai we're on a first-name basis now, after his death gave us entry to his storage-bin home fell in a serious incident. Another five soldiers were wounded, but could just as easily have been killed.
We will not see his bereaved mother in demonstrations of the Four Mothers movement, which wants a unilateral pullout from Lebanon, and his father, who has returned to Russia, will no longer be a burden to the Defense Ministry. Quite a luxury for our side.
Nevertheless, Lebanon remains "Israel's" most murderous front. According to the semi-annual report of the United Nations general secretary, the period from July through December 1997 witnessed a substantial increase in the number of attacks launched by Hizbullah on the "Israel" Defense Forces (IDF)* 942 attacks, up from 451 in the preceding six-month period.
The report expressed special concern at the number of civilian casualties, who are mainly Lebanese. Together with the dry statistics, reports are coming in about Hizbullah's acquisition of sophisticated weapons, including advanced shoulder-launched missiles and long-range mortars, and about its intelligence collecting capability, which is of increasing concern to the IDF.
Night-vision equipment and listening devices are only two of the items interfering with IDF operations. It is only natural that an armed force, even one that like Hizbullah numbers only about 2,000 fighters, will become more efficient as its combat experience becomes richer.
Unlike the IDF, Hizbullah is unfettered by the need to lead convoys to bring food and laundry to troops or by a commitment to safety measures and its fighters are therefore able to wage guerrilla warfare with virtually no danger of becoming worn out.
An army in a constant state of combat readiness, focused on how to outwit and head off the "Israel" enemy, will become a quality army. Behind that force is a supportive Lebanese public. The citizens of Lebanon, who are sometimes killed by IDF fire, do not engage in a public debate over the situation. "Israel" has discovered this fact of life both in the large-scale operations it has carried out against Lebanon over the years and in the local harassment actions the IDF continues to execute.
Attacks on Lebanese civilians or on civilian targets have so far failed to produce the hoped-for results. There is no strategic answer to the Lebanon situation. Nuclear weapons are not an option against Hizbullah squads. Nor, at the moment, does there seem to be any diplomatic course of action that could resolve the Lebanon problem. Theoretically, only peace with Syria can get "Israel" a divorce from Lebanon theoretically, because the prospect of negotiations with Damascus is dim.
The only possible way is separation: the very same separation that has such high support among "Israelis" in the case of the Palestinians. After all, every attack by Palestinians immediately sparks talk of the need to build a fence, to block access routes and to prevent physical contact between the sides. In Lebanon such separation is far simpler. There are no Jewish settlements in Lebanon. There are no "Israeli" civilians or foreign tourists who might become chance targets. Lebanon is not a strategic asset and it contains no holy places that might arouse religious or national passions. The real danger is that Lebanon is on the way to becoming "Israel"'s perpetual war site, part of the formative experience of young fighters who missed the war of 1948, the Sinai Campaign of 1956 and the Six-Day War of 1967 or an opportunity for new immigrants from Russia and Ethiopia take part in the "Israeli" experience.

* This term is absolutely unacceptable. "Israel's" Army is that of war and aggression for its protects land gained by force, confiscation and war in contradiction with the simplest rights of human confessed by the international standards.
