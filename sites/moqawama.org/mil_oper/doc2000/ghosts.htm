Ghosts of times past

 

At Kibbutz Manara near the northern border, times are hard and the living is uneasy as the army prepares to withdraw from Lebanon with no agreement.
 Lilach Shai starts worrying when she recalls the roadside bomb placed near the Tziporan outpost about two months ago, very close to the Lebanese border and a five-minute drive from her home at Kibbutz Manara. The bomb was detonated on a van carrying two soldiers, one of whom received minor injuries. Military commentators said Hizbullah was signaling that after the IDF's* withdrawal its fighters could easily reach the border. Lilach, 33, heard the message loud and clear. It echoes in her head each time the issue of the withdrawal comes up at home, and recently that has been very often, almost every day.Her husband Moti, 37, kibbutz coordinator for some 18 months, is not panicked. The message did not deter him. As far as he's concerned, all the roads leading to the communities on the Ramim ridge could be closed: "Let them all travel on the Manara Cliffs cable car. That will improve the quality of the air. There are a few examples of this abroad, maybe not for security reasons, but they exist."
Lilach chuckles when she hears this idea and in the same breath asserts that "my children will not go to school in armored buses or with a military escort." No way, she emphasizes, when she hears for the first time about the idea proposed by the IDF. "They will too travel like that," her husband responds, leaving the issue open for the meantime.
They apparently will not have much time to discuss it, these parents of two children, since the political correspondents are reporting that "people in Barak's circle are talking of a withdrawal in May, or June at the latest. The moment the Geneva summit failed, there was no point in waiting until July." May-June is close, and from Manara it looks even closer, with the South Lebanon security zone on the other side of the road and the echoes of explosions clearly audible. When there are no injuries, people in Tel Aviv are not even aware that there was shooting; "daily war," the correspondents write a few days later when they report another bad incident. At Manara, at Misgav Am, at Margaliot, at Yiftach and at other "border communities," it is real.
Lilach was born on Manara, Moti came to the kibbutz from Ra'anana aged 13. They live in the second floor of a three-story building (Manara has only a few one-story homes). Lilach studies at Derby University's Tel Aviv branch two days a week, toward a tourism management degree ("because in this area there is no such course of study in any college"). The question of leaving the area in the wake of the withdrawal is always with them, especially in light of the differences of opinion within the home. Moti trusts the army, Lilach does not want her children to grow up the way that she did.
"I spent most of my childhood in going in and out of the bomb shelter. Returning the border to the international boundary and in effect to the fence across the street, across from the gate of the kibbutz, means the life I experienced as a girl. It's a fair assumption that that would be the result of a unilateral withdrawal. I am in favor of withdrawal," she explains, "but don't ask me how to do it."
A few days ago, Lilach and Moti were driving near Kibbutz Manara. The IDF had blocked the road westward, to the fields. She asked what it was, and Moti answered that "this is where the border will be."
"That was enough for me," Lilach said.

No new members

Next year the Manara kindergarten will close. This year it is barely in operation for nine children, one of them from a family that has already announced its plans to leave within a few months. In the last eight years the kibbutz has not absorbed any new members and three families have left in the past four years - without any prior announcement. That is exactly what has kibbutz secretary Ayala Kfir worried. Ayala and her husband Shlomi were interviewed together with the Shais.
"Almost no one asks me about the withdrawal," Ayala says. "It disturbs me, because for me that is a sign that families can come to me with a concrete decision to leave, without consulting me, simply to come with a fait accompli." She says the kibbutz is doing poorly and the security worries compound an already difficult situation - the crisis of values stemming from the privatization process, and the demographic problem resulting from members' children leaving combined with the lack of new members.
"That's the difference between us and Metula. There, the security problem is similar to ours, a border settlement where the border will close by. But there the population is growing, not like here. People are more confident, less pressured, the size of the town makes the difference. Manara and Misgav Am have a real sense of existential weakness. If people like Moti and Lilach were sure of their homes, I don't think the security situation would worry them so much."
Manara is an agricultural kibbutz with 110 families. Most of its income stems from poultry, field crops and a small metal shop that employs eight workers from Kiryat Shmona. The tourism industry, as in many kibbutzim, is being encouraged in an era in which agriculture is increasingly making way for other areas. But it is hard to have high expectations from tourism when even the members feel insecure.
All of these factors lead Shlomi, Ayala's husband, to the conclusion that "the kibbutz is at a very critical point. If the demographic situation worsens, that will be a great blow. In my nightmares I see a ghost settlement.
Even Ayala agrees that the kibbutz idea is "burned out," and that as a "kibbutznik in her soul" she led the privatization process "very slowly. It is obvious to me that at the end of this process Manara will be a [private] community settlement, but it will not be easy to convince the members of the benefits of the process. I didn't give up. But I feel that the expected withdrawal in the current situation will lead us to lose our people"
Her answer would be to set up a private neighborhood near the kibbutz. There are plans for 81 one-story homes, but no permits have been obtained and they are short about $1.5 million - the cost of moving the poultry coop to a nearby area. "So I am telling the state: guys, you must help us with these matters. It's not money that is going into our pockets, it's in order to save the kibbutz."
For Moti, saving the kibbutz is a national mission. Lilach is amazed. "I never heard him say such things, I'm in shock. A national mission? I never saw my presence here as a national mission. Today, when I have the option of leaving, it is really not on my scale of priorities."
Absolutely a national mission, Moti tells her, "it's one of the reasons I want to stay here and it is at the top of my scale of priorities."

We won't burn tires

Perhaps the argument results from the uncertainty. Even Ayala and Moti, who hold the most senior positions in the kibbutz, have not heard a word about the nature of the withdrawal, of the security, travel procedures after the withdrawal, and certainly nothing about any benefits package to improve the socio-economic situation at the kibbutz. The situation is similar for the heads of other communities on the Ramim ridge.
Patience has run out for members of Misgav Am, and dozens of them came down to demonstrate in Kiryat Shmona on Friday. An army of media people have invaded the kibbutz over the last few days in the wake of the demonstration. "The 'squares' have turned out to demonstrate, it brings in the media," a member of Misgav Am said this week.
But at Manara there is still no intention of getting the members out into the streets. "No one here will burn tires, it's not our style," they explain, and wonder how it is that the state does not realize that they at least should know what is happening. "We don't know anything. We only hear in the media that the withdrawal is approaching," Moti says.
"The army showed us a plan for a withdrawal with an agreement. It gave us a fair way of measuring how many of the members are worried. No event at Manara has ever brought so many people to the kibbutz center like that talk with the commander of the Eastern Brigade. They said they weren't prepared for withdrawal without an agreement. I like hearing the stories of the older people sometimes. I thought they were unmoved, but in talks with them I understood that Lilach is not alone. Many of them are worried."
Margaliot is doing this week what they started doing at Misgav Am last week - lots of media attention. Some idle threats were also voiced at the moshav next to Manara, such as to return their identity cards to the state and leave in their masses. Moti claims that "they get carried away at Margaliot," and that too provokes an argument with Lilach: "I think they are speaking from the gut, it's an expression of their trepidation. The problem is that when things aren't said in an exaggerated way they aren't heard, and it's a fact that Margaliot got newspaper headlines."
"I don't have to agree with everything she says even though I married her," Moti ends this part of the argument. In this confrontation, Lilach admits, Moti will probably win. She will probably not go all the way. "He understands me, but he is very decisive. He thinks he has no reason to be afraid. There are discussions at home, it increases when there is 'background music' on the other side of the border, such as last week when there was lots of shooting, but I am inclined to give in."
"It must be understood," Ayala insists, "I, even if I was their mother, I wouldn't worry about them if they were leaving. Despite the difficulties of the kibbutz, the people here are not poor. Everyone who left Manara has managed, wherever he went to. It's not like a poor family that leaves Kiryat Shmona and stays poor in their new home. And that's exactly what worries me: It's harder to keep the families here when the temptations outside are great. That's why it's a national mission. The state must struggle on behalf of these people, who live in the border communities, on the periphery. These are groups of people who could easily move to the center of the country without turning into welfare cases."
The idea of leaving never occurred to the kibbutz secretary, who is the first to go into the shelter during periods of tension. "I am afraid, the incidents scare me, but no place in "Israel" is completely secure, it's a question of relative security. If we were a private neighborhood with 150 families, this discussion wouldn't even be taking place."
Everyone sitting around the table - those who are definitely staying and whose who are still deciding - bring up at some stage the sign that stood at the side of the road leading to Manara until a few years ago. "Traveling in the dark is prohibited," it said.
Even Ayala could not tolerate such a situation again. "It's almost like all-out war," she says, perhaps a moment before the protection and travel procedures plan is presented to her. "I don't even imagine that the state would tolerate such a situation, that it would be impossible to travel around here freely. We live in a world of roads."
Shlomi recalls that a few weeks ago he ignored army instructions and drove on the road leading to the kibbutz that was blocked for a few hours. "I waved at them and kept going. The inhabitants will not live with closed roads, I suggest that the army not attempt to impose such a situation." Lilach had nothing more to say. Moti just smiled
