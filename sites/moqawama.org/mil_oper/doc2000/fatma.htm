Scared to death at the Fatma Gate

By Lili Galili

The day before yesterday, at 2:30 p.m., the usual time when the workers from southern Lebanon leave Israel for home, S., one of the workers, arrived at the Fatma Gate in Metula, on the way home to his village on the opposite ridge. In blue clothes, his pass in hand, he walked slowly toward the gate through which he had entered in the early morning hours, to his job as an agricultural worker in Metula. He has been doing this every day for the past 12 years. Some 150 Lebanese workers take the same route regularly.But the day before yesterday in the afternoon there was nothing regular about this procedure. In the background loud explosions could be heard from "out there," which was once very nearby, and S. walked slowly, almost dragging his feet unwillingly, as though trying to postpone the moment of return. He agreed to stop for a moment for a brief conversation: "Everything will be okay," he said with lowered eyes. "Everything is okay." When he lifted his head, there were tears in his eyes. About half an hour later, it was learned that the Hezbollah had reached the outskirts of his village, a large Muslim village, in what was immediately called "the collapse of the central sector." S. did not yet know this as he was returning home, but he probably already had a premonition.

Yesterday morning dozens of workers from southern Lebanon arrived at the Fatma gate. The gates were already closed. The only way out was through a passage in the western sector. But the workers cannot go to the western sector; the road passes through areas which have been captured by the Hezbollah. And thus the workers remained trapped, without a way out and without information. A new order in Lebanon.

They are scared to death. That is the only way to describe the atmosphere which prevailed yesterday at the usually bustling gateway. They are "scared to death" not in a metaphorical sense, but literally. The workers walked quietly, mournful and with lowered heads, refusing to speak, afraid to identify themselves. Many of them stop for a moment next to one of the three public phones there, and conduct short conversations, in lowered voices. Before they set out for home, they call to find out what has happened, if there is still a house, or who the new neighbors are.

"What's going to happen?" says one Lebanese worker with a sad smile, at the Fatma gate. "I have been with Israel since '76, and was in the South Lebanon Army** for five years. Now our army is leaving, and I'm sitting at home. I have weapons, and I'm waiting. If Hezbollah comes, we will fight." His Christian colleague, who is also an agricultural worker who has been working in Metula for years, looks at him angrily. "Waiting? If they close the passage tomorrow and I stay in the village, the Hezbollah will slaughter me. If I flee to Beirut, they'll slaughter me in Beirut." And he disappears through the gate.

On the bench next to the gate only D. remains seated. He is a Druze plasterer from one of the villages on the opposite ridge. "I have been an officer in the SLA for 11 years," he says with a vestige of pride about those days. "Now Nasrallah (leader of the Hezbollah) is not right, Israel is not right for leaving us like this." He waves his hand to demonstrate the abandonment by Israel, and sums up: "It's not so terrible, you can only die once."

Everyone here talks about death, or at least thinks about it. What they won't say to a reporter who comes for a brief visit, they tell Moshe Milstein, one of Metula's old-timers, a local restaurant owner. With undisguised pride, Milstein tells that he was the first to open a restaurant at the Good Fence on the Lebanon border, 25 years ago, and with undisguised anger, he speaks of the events of the past few days. "They come and cry on my shoulder," he says of the Lebanese workers. "Only this morning one of them showed me holes in his belly from the days when he was an SLA soldier and the Hezbollah shot at him. They were cannon fodder for the IDF, and now they are Hezbollah's cannon fodder."

"They don't tell us anything," say two female workers from a Lebanese Christian village. All the men in their families are SLA soldiers. Yesterday two of them disappeared, and they don't know where the men are. "Nobody came to tell us what happened, nobody promised that everything will be okay. Barak leaves us here and walks away. All our soldiers died for nothing, we gained nothing from it," says the older woman. "Now he is leaving, and there will be more dead." A day earlier, Nasrallah appeared on television and mentioned their village by name, and said that he knows the names of those who work in Israel, and promised to meet them with weapons.

Soon the Lebanese woman worker is going to marry off her daughter. The wedding will be in Beirut; she will not be able to participate.

Like all the workers with relatives in the SLA, she cannot move around freely in her country. Since yesterday, she has been denied entry into Israel. Yesterday her employer already said that she didn't manage to get to work. The last words she said before crossing the Fatma gate the day before yesterday were: "Our blood is on Israel's hands. Whatever happens to us, the Israeli government is to blame. I keep thinking about the slaughter in the refugee camps of Sabra and Shatila."

At the other end of Metula, in a pretty house on the Street of the Founders, lives Miriam Hod. She says that when she thinks about the fate of the SLA men she thinks of the Holocaust. These associations are natural to her, as the daughter of Holocaust survivors who is married to a native of Metula, a scion of one of the founding families. A few hundred meters from her home, and from the Beit Shalom inn, which she runs, one can see the road that will soon be the new border. The order to go down to the shelters the day before yesterday caught the residents of Metula unprepared.

Hod refused to go down to the shelter, continuing with what she has been doing constantly for the past two years: helping SLA members. She sent the first letter on the subject to Benjamin Netanyahu, when he was still prime minister and was talking about pulling out of Lebanon. Hod asked him to find a solution for the temporary absorption of the SLA members in Israel. From the reply she received then, it turned out that the Prime Minister's Office was not willing to handle the problem.

When Ehud Barak started to discuss withdrawal, she resumed her intensive activity. Now she, together with nine other families from the North, represent 194 families of SLA members who work in Israel, and hope to find a refuge for them. "Everyone who works here got a work permit from Israel, on condition that one of the members of the immediate family serves in the SLA," she says. "We have turned them into hostages. Now, when I hear them saying to themselves, 'Everything will be okay, let's wait and see, we'll decide when we know exactly what's happening,' I think of the Jews who sat and waited when Hitler came to power, until it was too late. They have no time to wait, each of them is facing a death sentence."

She sent her last letter to the Prime Minister's Office only four days ago, summarizing her recent activity: "..Since the beginning of March we have sent your five letters. The replies which we received from your representatives are unclear and insulting .... We, a group of nine residents, want to provide shelter for our southern Lebanese workers and their families, each of which includes at least one SLA soldier. These families are now requesting only temporary refuge, and when the situation becomes clear and calm, they want to return to their homes, and not to become refugees. Because we have developed special warm relationships with them over the years, we cannot stand aside and let them be killed."

The list of Lebanese workers who are interested in temporary refuge was also turned over to the IDF. "I had the impression that the army doesn't know exactly what will happen," says Hod. "They hinted that there are two groups - the senior SLA officers, to whom they will offer rehabilitation abroad, or refuge in Israel, and the bigger, larger group, which they are ignoring. What has been happening in the last few days confirms my suspicion that this is the policy: It looks as though they are going to save the officers, and let all the others be slaughtered."

Yesterday, the Lebanese woman who has been working for her for 20 years did not come to work. All the men in her family are SLA soldiers, and yesterday Hod made desperate attempts to make contact with her in her village. While the passage to Israel was opened to residents of the western sector, it may be too late already for those in the central sector. "I can't sleep at night," says Hod. "My fate is in the hands of Barak. I won't leave Metula, but these people can be moved around. Where there's a will, there's a way."

The director of the passage in Metula told her that the gate is closed. The prime minister's office sent her to General (res.) Yossi Peled, in an effort to get the gate opened to allow the entry of SLA people from the central sector as well. One gets the impression that even now, in the midst of the withdrawal from Lebanon, there is great confusion on this matter. Only a soldier who came down from Lebanon answers the question "What's happening?" with the answer "balagan" (a mess, confusion), with a huge smile of relief. It reminds us of the other aspect of this withdrawal
