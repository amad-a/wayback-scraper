Why didn't I get up?

 


    Ofer Sharon, an outstanding soldier in the paratroopers commando unit, openly campaigned for the Four Mothers' goal of immediate withdrawal from Lebanon. Then, in a night ambush, his commander and two other officers were killed. In a confession that is rare for its extreme candor, Sharon describes how he lay paralyzed and did not get up to help, partly out of fear, partly out of his deep opposition to the war. The brigade commander listened to his story, and forgave him. His comrades aren't so ready to forget .

By Avihai Becker

    It was First Sergeant Ofer Sharon's first, and last, foray deep into Lebanon. The paratrooper commando unit spent a total of six hours in Lebanon - but it appears he will spend his whole life in the shadow of that night. Until that day, February 23, 1999, he'd never crossed the red line, the northern border of the security zone. The battle orders and preparations had gone on for a week, but no one was really sure whether the operation would actually take place."You shouldn't get nervous for no good reason," the veterans said, doing their best to calm him. "Just like a hundred other missions, this one will be cancelled, too."

Anyhow, they said, it wasn't going to be anything too complicated - just a routine ambush with slim chances of seeing any action. Not long beforehand, the unit had toured the sector, and only turned up one isolated shepherd.

This was the sort of baptism by lukewarm fire that Sharon was looking for. He'd recently joined the fighting unit of the sayeret (commandos), having spent the previous six months at the unit's training base instructing cadets. Since he wasn't yet fluent in the operational lifestyle, Sharon considered the planned ambush at Qal'at Jabur a good opportunity to help in the transition. Until then, his total combat experience consisted of two ambushes at the end of his training phase, but even they were simple, straightforward affairs - inside the security zone, next to the border fence.

The column advanced on foot. Sharon, considered the best soldier of his draft intake - November '96 - carried a Negev machine-gun around his shoulder, a round in the chamber, ready for action. "As we neared the point where we were supposed to position ourselves, I moved forward," Sharon recalls. "Then Eitan (Belachson, commander of the sayeret) and Liraz (Tito, the engineering officer) passed by and went down to check out the site where we were supposed to dig in. We were running a little late. I was at the high point of the ridge, and they were on the slope below, barely 10 meters from me. I was the only one who had visual contact with them. It was 1:30 A.M. All of a sudden, this huge barrage erupted, from right to left. I saw the flashes, and I remember mumbling to myself, 'Oh no - anything but this.' They were gunned down from a distance of two meters - it was obvious to me that they were dead."

Distant sounds
First Sergeant Ofer Sharon, from Kibbutz Ashdot Yaakov Ichud, was a key member of his squad. Aside from his leadership qualities, he was also known as the unit's very own emissary from Four Mothers, the movement that favors an immediate withdrawal from Lebanon. Sharon made no effort to conceal his opinion about the need to pull out of the security zone, even though his ideas were less than popular among his fellow soldiers.

When Sharon first enlisted, he did not harbor any such ideas. He had always wanted to serve in an elite combat unit. Contrary to what one might assume, even on kibbutz army service is considered an undesirable punishment, but one that can be made more palatable by joining an intimate elite combat unit. To boost his chances of acceptance into such a unit, Sharon embarked on a grueling training regimen, and while still in high school was running 250 kilometers a week. He eventually found a niche in the paratroopers commando unit, after having failed the acceptance tests for the general staff commando unit, Sayeret Matkal.
In pre-basic training exercises, Sharon was ranked first out of hundreds of candidates, and was extremely proud of himself. There was a time many years ago that the finest young men of Ashdot Yaakov Ichud would go into the paratroopers commando unit. Then, for about 20 years, the tradition was broken. Ofer's father, Yoav Sharon, had been an officer in the unit. Yoav completed officer training school with the highest grades, sharing honors with another crack cadet named Ehud Brug (Barak).

Ofer, born in 1977, is the youngest of five brothers. While attending the regional high school at Beit Yerah, along the shores of the Sea of Galilee, he had an especially active social life. School was not a high priority; sports interested him much more. Before enlisting, he spent a year volunteering at a dormitory for troubled youth in Akko, which he describes as a remarkable experience. A self-described soccer freak, Ofer is a fan of French soccer player Zinedine Zidane and of Ukrainian forward Andriy Shevchenko, who heads up the AC Milan offense. Both of his parents were born on the kibbutz, the children of pioneers. Bruria is the kibbutz librarian, and Yoav is a quality assurance manager at Log, the kibbutz factory that manufactures plastic bottles.

Sharon's first days in the army were clouded by reports of fallen soldiers, but he did not have any direct contact with any of them, and does not remember being over-engaged in any soul searching. Three months after his enlistment, another young man from his kibbutz, Avner Alter, was killed in the helicopter tragedy. But even this death didn't really penetrate Sharon's thick shell. His upbeat feeling persisted until May 1997, and the last week of basic training. Ofer's squad leader walked into the squad tent and, tears running down his cheeks, told them that three soldiers from the unit had been killed in a clash in Lebanon - the deputy unit commander, Captain Eran Shamir; First Sergeant Zev Sommerfeld; and First Sergeant Ran Mezuman.

"It sounded so distant, as if it were three Golani soldiers," Sharon says. "It didn't really affect us, because we hadn't joined the unit proper and didn't really feel we were part of it. We were just trainees, we didn't know who these people were who had died. Also, we didn't yet have any idea what was going on in Lebanon."

Sharon was in the honor guard at the funeral of Zev Sommerfeld on Moshav Ashrat, but claims not to have felt any heavy sense of bereavement. On the other hand, he was moved by the red beret and paratroopers' wings that he wore especially for the ceremony. He was eager to earn the right to wear them for keeps.

"The most bizarre thing," Sharon says, "is that you're standing on the edge of the grave and not really absorbing how awful it all is. It seems so clear to you that this is how it is in the army." In training, he was always ready to assume responsibility, always volunteering first for any exercise that demanded a show of bravery. When he'd come home for the weekend, it would seem as though Sharon was already deep into the army routine.

It all began with mother
At this point, something happened at home that caused him to raise his perspective above his own horizons. "Thankfully, that was when my mother, Mrs. Bruria Sharon, began to get riled up," says Ofer. "She joined Four Mothers, and on my home leaves she would talk to me, and thanks to her inspiration, I gradually began to undergo a transition."

By the time Eyal Shimoni, a peer from the neighboring kibbutz, Ashdot Yaakov Meuchad, was killed near Reihan, the loose ends were all tied up. Sharon began to talk to his friends, who didn't take him seriously. On the contrary - soldier Ofer became the pet of the squad commander, Lieutenant David Granit, a religious officer from Ofra. At home, Granit would talk admiringly about his kibbutznik soldier. Despite the profound ideological gap dividing them - one a secular leftist, the other a religious settler - Granit was intensely fond of Ofer. The bond that formed between them resembled an ad for the religious-secular reconciliation campaign.

More than once during their service together, Granit's squad was invited to Sharon's kibbutz. The friends liked his vivacious mother very much. While holding conversations with them on the lawn of the kibbutz, Bruria Sharon never hid her thoughts about the missions to which they would soon be sent. She never concealed the big Four Mothers pin that she wore, not when they were around, not even at the wedding of her daughter Rachel two weeks ago at Kibbutz Ga'ash.

Bruria Sharon joined the Four Mothers movement shortly after its establishment. She sat in a protest tent opposite the Prime Minister's house, stood at intersections, was cursed at, waved signs, accompanied her friend Orna Shimoni on visits to the markets. As far as she's concerned, she is enlisted until victory is attained. Even now, when her youngest son is out of the army, she still burns with the same intensity. Bruria carries on her campaign.

Friends said to stop
At first, Sharon was a bit hesitant about voicing the ideas he learned from his mother. But over time, as he began to formulate his own opinions, he became ever more determined and vocal. Still, only a handful of friends listened. The majority responded with comments like "You're nuts, it's easy to see you haven't been to Lebanon. When you get there, you'll be talking a different story."

And he got there. The two ambushes toward the end of his training phase, near the Tziporen position, were all the proof he needed to demonstrate the justice of his cause. "Two weeks before we were there, at the same exact place, two Golani guys walked over a mine. They had barely left the gate of the position, when the radio man came to tell the commander the results of the countoff. They were standing alongside one another, someone pressed a button and they went flying - right there on the border. After that, it's stupid to say that missions deep in Lebanese territory can prevent the Hezbollah from getting through."

At the ceremony marking the end of the training phase, Bruria Sharon, the unofficial den mother of the squad, was asked to speak for the parents. "The guys want you to talk, but please, don't talk about Lebanon," was how her son transmitted his friends' request. As she stood up to speak, the crowd was on edge. Everyone expected her to make a pitch for Four Mothers. Miriam Bar-Yosef, mother of Yoni, had even prepared a rebuttal, just in case. She didn't need it. True to her word, Bruria didn't mention the word Lebanon. "Always remain steadfast, looking at the logic and rationale of the situation, and your individual and human analytic abilities," she said. "I implore us, the parents who granted you this life - not to be so free and easy about what we are giving up. It is our duty to ask, to probe, to demand and to wonder all the time, every moment: Why? For what? When? Why? Who decides? Nothing is written in stone from above." When Bruria Sharon left the podium, everyone got up to embrace her.

Ofer was assigned as an instructor of trainees in the unit, and refused to go to officer training school despite the urging of his commanders, David Granit and Alon Ron. As soon as he was transferred to the combat platoons of the company, he asked to meet with the revered commander of the sayeret, Major Eitan Belachson, so as to share his thoughts about Lebanon. "Stop," his friends told him. "Don't do it. He won't have any patience for you." They insinuated that on the issue of Lebanon, Ofer was far off the mark, that there was no one else like Belachson, raised on Kibbutz Ramot Naftali, who was convinced of the need for a security zone to safeguard the northern settlements. Sharon relented. "I decided it would be better for me to establish myself in the combat platoons first, and then, when the opportunity arose, I would speak with Eitan."

Paralyzed behind a rock
"I'm not going to leave this unit until I get a few terrorists," Eitan Belachson said, during a pep talk with his men before the force left for Qal'at Jabur. The rumor had spread among the soldiers that their commander would soon be promoted to command a battalion in the paratroopers brigade.

Three officers died in the operation: Major Eitan Belachson, Lieutenant David Granit, and engineering officer Liraz Tito. When the battle was over, five wounded men were evacuated. The Hezbollah did not suffer any losses.

Sharon describes the firefight and the fears that gripped him during it, simultaneously examining his own part in it, bitterly, pulling no punches. Some may construe it as an act of hara-kiri meant to gain absolution, others might view it as a struggle for sanity.
"As soon as the shooting began, I took cover behind a rock," Sharon recalls. "David Granit, who was lying behind me, got up, shouted, 'Eitan, Eitan,' and leapt into the breach, directly into the fire. At this point, he was fighting all on his own. He started calling up to me, 'Ofer, come on down,' but I didn't dare go. My head was filled with thoughts. I knew that anyone who goes down there doesn't come back. I knew that charging now meant dying in a senseless war."

"'I'll direct the fire from here,' I told David, to which he said, 'Okay.' I tried to persuade myself that important things could be done from above, as well, that there were ways of neutralizing the terrorists, but the simple truth is that I did not get up."

While Sharon was wedged behind his rock, the navigator of the force, First Sergeant Michael Baruchi, who was right alongside him, hurried down to join Granit, who had begun to throw hand grenades and fire his rifle in the assumed direction of the Hezbollah men.

"While I was laying down my fire," Sharon continues, "men moved up from the rear and asked me where the action was. I pointed the way, sending them to a place where I myself didn't dare go. They went down, and I didn't move. When I saw their backs, I was consumed with guilt. I felt that if I stayed behind one more minute, I'd never be able to live with it."

"In the end I got up and slid down between the rocks. It was terrifying, it was hell. Ricochets flying, bullets shrieking past, exactly like in that song about Ammunition Hill. Everything was happening within a range of a few meters, there in the dark, amid the boulders and the dense vegetation."

These were Ofer Sharon's finer moments. Lieutenant Granit hadn't yet been hit. He was kneeling with three other soldiers - Yishai Reshef, Lior Ophir and Yaron Rosental, on a stone rise, all of them firing their weapons toward an unseen enemy position at the foot of a steep cliff.

Sharon was a little higher than the others. While he was shooting, the mechanism of his Negev machine gun was hit by a round. Sharon tried to correct the problem, but was unable. "Now the only thing I cared about was getting the bodies out of there."

He straddled Liraz Tito on his back and carried him up the hill. When he came back, Sharon found Granit sprawled on the freezing ground, not breathing. Sharon shook him, "David, wake up," he shouted in desperation. While dragging him up the slope on his back, Sharon heard what he thought was a wheeze. He's alive, Sharon thought, but the doctor who checked Granit declared him dead. The battle lasted a total of 12 minutes.

"I needed someone to help me carry David, but no one would come. I looked up and saw every one standing there with night-vision devices, trying to spot the terrorists, who had run away. They were yelling, 'Shoot to the right,' and 'Shoot to the left.' They were also trying to direct combat helicopters at the enemy. I walked up to Yaron Rosental, and while he was firing his weapon toward the fleeing terrorists, I yelled at him: 'Let them run away, what do you care? Come and help me get David out of here.' At the time, I thought he could still be saved, I didn't comprehend that he was dead already. 'First we fight the terrorists, and only then we evacuate casualties,' shouted Michael Baruchi, who was lying wounded nearby.

Man enough
Ofer Sharon left on the last helicopter out, and came back to Israel a changed person. "I couldn't accept the fact that the country continues to deal with inanities while young men are being killed in Lebanon for no good reason. From then on, every new victim shocked me. It turned into a critical issue for me." Considering his next move, Sharon hit on what might be considered an inherent contradiction. "Just as it was obvious to me that I didn't want to go to Lebanon anymore, I was equally convinced that I had to do it. Otherwise, what would I say to a soldier who didn't agree to evacuate a settlement. That's not the right way, either. It's wrong for everyone to do what he sees fit and pick which mission he likes, and which he doesn't." Not for a moment did he consider refusing to serve.

Soon after the incident, First Sergeant Sharon asked to speak with his brigade commander, Colonel Gershon (Jerry) Yitzhak. The officers closest to Sharon in the sayeret, who had discerned some distress signals, tried to dissuade him, but Ofer was convinced he was doing the right thing.

"They didn't realize how serious I was about it. They even found a lot of points in my favor, tried to make life easier for me, but - like the chief of staff himself, who suspended two paratroopers who didn't chase a terrorist who had infiltrated the Sujud position - I felt that I had to be man enough to tell him exactly where and when I had turned chicken."

The brigade commander was astounded by Sharon's candor. The soldier had prepared himself for a harsh verdict, and was flabbergasted when the commander listened intently, even sensitively. "To Jerry's credit, he met me without delay. I sat with him for two hours, told him all about how this whole business - about how I don't believe we should be in Lebanon - just overcame me during the incident. I'm not sure what I expected the brigade commander to do. Did I go to him so he could tell me I wasn't suited to go into Lebanon, or was it to rid myself of this burden of how I had performed during the battle? I think it was a combination of the two."

"He told me, 'Listen, things like this happen sometimes.' He even told me about something that happened to him in the Lebanon War, how as a company commander he nearly couldn't get himself out from behind a rock. He talked about where, during the incident, I had acted as he might expect a sayeret soldier to act. Jerry gave me the feeling that it was vital for me to go back into action, partly so that the squad wouldn't be hurt by an even deeper shock. As it was, morale in the unit was low."

Sharon then engaged the brigade commander in a detailed discourse on the Four Mothers beliefs. "Convince me that I'm wrong," he told the colonel. It didn't work. After two hours of an anything-but-routine conversation, the two parted, without reaching agreement on the issue. However, they did agree that Sharon would be assigned to the next combat detail in Lebanon. The soldier addressed himself with all due seriousness to training for the next round.

Get rid of the rotten apple
Yisrael Baruchi of Alon Shvut heard about Sharon while visiting his wounded son Michael at Rambam Hospital. Baruchi senior called the brigade commander, suggesting that he get the "rotten apple" out of the barrel as soon as possible. The brigade commander listened, but did not comply with the request.

On Independence Day, Michael Baruchi received a personal commendation from the head of the Northern Command, in recognition of his part in the Qal'at Jabur battle. When Granit shouted 'After me!' he got up. Unlike Sharon, he didn't lose his composure.

"I want to believe my problem isn't about courage, although after Qal'at Jabur, I still have to prove that," says Sharon. "What happened to me doesn't fill me with pride, but it does prove what sort of mutations a war without purpose is liable to generate.

Maybe they'll hate me in the unit after this interview, but that's not enough to prevent me from saying what General Giora Inbar, Head of Military Intelligence Chaim Yifrah, and Major General Shai Avital have already said. Their arguments against staying in Lebanon cannot be dismissed by saying that they didn't perform when they had to."

Many men in the unit, Sharon claims, perhaps as wishful thinking, have changed their minds in the wake of the incident. "Now they agree that the price is too high," Sharon says, "but tragically enough, it took the deaths of three beloved officers for the unit to get in touch with reality. Persuading them became as simple as snapping a twig. People who used to argue with me had all the resistance of jello."

Two days before the mission that was supposed to be Sharon's "cleansing experience," he was injured in a soccer game at Kibbutz Kinneret, and hospitalized at Poriya Hospital. Paradoxically, the pain gave him a sense of relief. Instead of "ouch" it was "ahh." For a month and a half, Sharon was out of commission, and for several weeks essentially had nothing to do with his unit. His name was dropped from the operational roster. His confession to the brigade commander didn't help. The IDF adopted his case as a model of how Four Mothers can destroy its soldiers.

For a long time after the incident, Sharon was unable to fall asleep at night. Whenever he tried to close his eyes, he'd see the grotesque sight of the bodies of Belachson, Granit and Tito. "I was afraid to sleep in the dark, was unable to be alone. Fortunately, I have a wonderful girlfriend who truly saved me from sinking down."

After his leg healed, Sharon returned to the unit for an interview with the new commander of the sayeret, Major Amir, with the idea of turning over a new leaf. "I explained the problem I have with Lebanon. He said he wanted me to come back. 'As far as I'm concerned,' he said, 'the only people who will be sent into Lebanon are those who stand in line to say they want to go.' I replied that if he gave me an order I would carry it out to the nth degree, but as long as it was left up to me, I doubted whether I would ever be able to say such a thing."

Sharon was rotated out of the combat company and assigned as an instructor in the unit's anti-terror department, which has no connection with Lebanon. That was how it was left. Later, when he halfheartedly said he was ready to go on a mission, no one paid much attention.

Two weeks ago, the unit granted Sharon an early demobilization, two months ahead of time, in order to improve his high-school matriculation grades. While turning in his equipment at the base, he saw his sayeret comrades returning - without a scratch - from a mission in which two Hezbollah men were killed. As he parted from his friends, both sides seemed to breathe a sigh of relief.

Sharon has now taken up studies at Jordan Valley College, and works as a youth counselor on his kibbutz. He urges the future draftees with whom he works to opt for a combat unit. "My reservations about Lebanon," he says, "haven't caused me to forget that the circle of hostility around us still exists."
