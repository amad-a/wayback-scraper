Parents of perished soldiers claim: Unauthorized explosives caused helicopter crash (1)
"One of the two IDF** helicopters which crashed in February 1997 near the northern border crashed because explosives which should not have been on board went off, parents of the 73 soldiers killed in the disaster said yesterday".

"The parents are demanding the attorney-general order an investigation into the disaster so that those responsible can be prosecuted".

"At a press conference yesterday, the parents' representatives rejected the findings of the Ivry Commission, which the defense minister appointed to investigate the accident, and accused the committee of hiding the truth. The parents have just finished reviewing the commission's protocols, which they obtained following an petition to the High Court of Justice".

"We demand the investigation of the disaster and the prosecution of the persons responsible for it, persons with blood on their hands who got away scot-free. These are the people who killed my son, and some of them weren't even questioned," Yoram Alper, one of the representatives of the bereaved parents, said yesterday.

"The parents said that the crash on February 4, 1997, actually consisted of two different accidents: one helicopter fell because it hit the other, but the second one, bound for Dla'at, crashed when explosives went off on board".

"Half of the soldiers killed that night could still be alive if the procedures had been kept. The air force investigated itself, and those to blame are walking around free," Alper said.

"According to documents and testimonies, the Dla'at-bound helicopter did not crash immediately after being hit by the other but continued to She'ar Yashuv, where its landing lights went on and it started circling and lowering for landing, the parents said. When the helicopter was between 25 and 50 meters from the ground, an explosion, which eyewitnesses testified was "louder than a Katyusha," ripped the helicopter in half."

"Sources from the Ivry Commission told Channel 1 the possibility of an explosion causing the crash of one of the helicopters was looked into and ruled out by the commission, as no sign was found to substantiate it."

"Another parent, Yehuda Rothenberg, said pathologist Yehuda Hiss demanded that autopsies be conducted on all the soldiers killed on the helicopter to Dla'at that night, but the air force forbade it".

"This would have revealed the cause of death as the explosion inside the helicopter, not its fall," he said.

"The Ivry Commission ignored all the causes of the accident, and accepted all the conclusions handed down by the air force. They hid the fact - discovered later by the parents - that the explosives weren't taken off the helicopter before the flight as required by regulations, and that the regulations had been violated, as they had been on other, previous flights," Alper said.

"The parents accused the committee of regarding the killed soldiers as "inventory to be struck off the roster" and of contriving to put the entire blame for the disaster on the helicopters' pilots, who also perished, as the easiest way out, with no consideration of their families."

"The parents further charged IDF Judge Advocate-General Brig.-Gen. Uri Shoham with acting as both adviser for the supposedly independent inquiry committee and for the investigated parties (the army and air force), in a blatant conflict of interests".

"[Shoham], who was representing the army, directed and steered the investigation, which should have been independent. He passed on material from commission meetings to the air force and the IDF top brass, so they could prepare themselves in advance for the commission hearings. He and the commission collaborated to close the affair and prevent the investigation from exposing the truth," said Ya'acov Gottlieb, one of the parents.

Gottlieb said the commission's debate focused on how to cover things up and close the affair quickly.

"They never imagined we'd get hold of the protocols, with their arguments with [Shoham] and their fears that someone might appeal to the High Court of Justice. The protocols show they forgot all about the disaster and the victims. All they thought about was how to close it, quickly," Gottlieb said.

"They cast false blame on the dead pilots, telling lies about the way they functioned, regardless of the feelings of their families. 'I won't lose my son in a futile cause' (1)
(December 4) Four members of the Four Mothers group tell why they are pushing for a unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon.

"Linda Ben-Zvi's elegant living room in the upscale Herzliya neighborhood of Nof Yam seemed about as far as one could get from the turmoil and tension of south Lebanon."

"But as Ben-Zvi, her son Arik, and three other women sat down in the airy room this week to discuss their involvement in the Four Mothers movement, the loud buzz of planes headed up the Mediterranean coast to the northern border could be heard, serving to remind them of their cause."

"All four of the women - Ben-Zvi, Rachel Ginzburg-Shahar, Linda Griver and Paula Miodowik - have been active in the Four Mothers group since shortly after it was founded in the spring of 1997 by four Upper Galilee women"

"Like the northern women, they joined when their sons were serving in the army, and have had to wrestle with the challenge of supporting their children while expressing their political views".

"Ben-Zvi, an immigrant from the US, first learned of Four Mothers from an article in The Jerusalem Post in May 1997, and contacted them just before their first demonstration in Tel Aviv was scheduled to take place. A year earlier, she had moved back to "Israel" with her "Israeli"-born husband after many years in America".

When they returned, their son Arik, who had returned to "Israel" before them, was serving in the IDF*.

"It was always clear to Arik that he would serve in a combat unit. If he was going to come back to "Israel" to serve, he wanted to do something meaningful and make a contribution," she explains.

But, says Ben-Zvi, a lecturer in theater at Tel Aviv University, the fact that her son was serving in Lebanon did not change her deep opposition to the presence of "Israeli" forces in the security zone.

Despite - or perhaps because of - the fact that those forces included her own son, she felt a need to do something.

"So I went to that first Tel Aviv Four Mothers demonstration, and found two other women standing under a tree in Rabin Square," she recalls. "We debated for a while as to the best place to stand, and ended up in front of the Gan Ha'ir mall, 12 of us. We exchanged phone numbers, and that was the nucleus for Four Mothers in the center of the country."

Ben-Zvi's involvement with the group has grown ever since. She currently sits on its board and serves as its international coordinator and spokesperson, dealing with press queries from spots as far-flung as Scandinavia, Australia and Japan.

While he respects her involvement and activism, Ben-Zvi's son, Arik, admits that, during his army days, he did not agree with his mother's position favoring unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon.

"I would come home from the army on leave and we would have pretty serious discussions about it. At the time, I was basically convinced that the army's line was correct, and that we were in Lebanon because we were protecting the northern settlements."

Today, more than a year after he was discharged, Arik's views more closely resemble his mother's, enough for the Tel Aviv University political science student to have taken on the job of student organizer for Four Mothers.

"My thinking began to change after I was discharged, after I got some distance from the situation and began to think about what we were actually achieving [in Lebanon] and what kind of a price we were paying for those achievements. I reached the conclusion that, really, what I had been doing there was not protecting the country. I was protecting myself and my friends - but little else."

A moment that stood out in Arik's change of heart took place during a post-army trip to the US, during which he visited the Vietnam War memorial in Washington.

"That memorial - the striking black wall with all the names of the soldiers on it - made such a statement about the futility of that war. It leaves you asking why all those people had to die, and what they died for.

"The thought that one day we might have a memorial like that for the Lebanon war just terrified me."
Rachel Ginzburg-Shahar's decision to join Four Mothers couldn't have been more spontaneous. While she has been involved in various political groups over the years, including peace organizations and the Labor Party, her commitment to increasing her activity grew after the Rabin assassination.

"It came as such a shock to me - that this man, the first person who gave me hope for a better future, could be murdered," she says.

At the same time, her sons were in the IDF. Both sons are still currently serving in the army; the elder, an officer, "is already a graduate of Lebanon" while the younger, after serving there initially, is currently doing an officer's training course and will return to Lebanon soon.

"I felt torn about becoming active at first," admits Ginzburg-Shahar, who was born in Kiryat Shmona. A retired school principal, she is now a private educational consultant.

"On the one hand, my sons are - how shall I put it - heavily brainwashed on the subject, particularly my younger son. I don't want to break his morale. I want him to wake up in the morning and believe in what he is doing, and I want to support him.

"On the other hand, I have to do what I feel is right... I believe there is no way to win this guerrilla war, and that it is wrong to defend our borders from another people's land."

But she says, it was actually her soldier son who eased her discomfort.

When Ginzburg-Shahar shared her feelings with him in the spring of 1997, he reassured her that it was all right; he told her that they jokingly referred to their unit as the "Four Mothers unit," since so many of the soldiers' mothers felt the same way.

That was actually the first time she heard of the group.

"Then one day, I was driving home and saw a Four Mothers demonstration at the Beit Lid junction near my home. Without even thinking about it, I pulled over, parked my car, picked up a sign and joined them."

Ginzburg-Shahar rejects the argument that, by protesting, she is undermining the authority of generals who decide her son's fate. She denies she is harming his safety in any way.

"How can you sit there and do nothing when your son tells you that they feel they are sitting ducks in Lebanon - and when you know that they are sitting ducks.

"As for the generals, I'm sick and tired of the generals. I don't believe in the generals; I don't believe that only they can make the right decisions. I believe that civilians have a right and responsibility to speak up."

Linda Ben-Zvi adds: "Somehow being a general is supposed to supersede everything. Their opinions are supposed to count more than ours.

"But if you look at the people in the government defending ["Israel's"] current position - Yitzhak Mordechai, Ephraim Sneh, Ori Orr, Ehud Barak - they were the very military men who set up this security zone in the first place. Of course they are going to defend it."

For Lynn Griver, a Gedera mother whose son is serving in a combat unit, participating in this week's Four Mothers demonstration in Jerusalem gave her a distinct sense of d?j vu.

"I felt like I was standing in exactly the same place I was 30 years ago, calling for the US to get out of Vietnam," she says.

In fact, she says, it was the slogan of the critics of the anti-war movement that influenced her decision to move from New York to "Israel" 22 years ago.

"They said about the US that we should love it or leave it. I thought about it, and I realized that, for me, "Israel" was home."

However, Griver stresses that while there are many parallels between the anti-Vietnam movement and the campaign to leave Lebanon, there are also differences.

But Griver, who runs an early childhood education center in Ramle, says that a year-and-a-half ago she was "just so disgusted" with the situation in Lebanon that she responded to a newspaper advertisement by the Four Mothers movement.

"I feel a great satisfaction about being a truly grassroots movement that is making an impact. For the first time, civilians are standing up and really questioning what the policy makers and the military are doing."

Griver describes her soldier son's reaction to her activity as "cynically supportive."

"I think he's proud of the fact that I am active, but doesn't think it's going to make any difference or do a lot of good.... But he does know that, while I hold these positions, I'm fully supportive of him and his commitment to do his duty. That's important to me."

Unlike the other three women in the room, Paula Miodowik knows all too well what it is like to lose a loved one in the army. Her younger brother was killed in the Yom Kippur War, she reveals with a catch in her voice that she works hard to control.

"I know this pain of loss - it is forever, and leaves a big black hole in your family. I know there is no such thing as a soldier who is enough of a hero to ease his family's wounds. I'll never understand why we are supposed to feel that once we put our child in a uniform, he becomes expendable.

"I didn't raise my children in order to give them to the government so it could do with them what it pleases."

After the experience of losing her brother, "I have no idea how I could live with the loss of a son in a futile cause," Miodowik says. It is a feeling which has driven her to become active in Four Mothers.

When she joined the group last year, her eldest son was serving in a combat unit.

"My [second] son is not in combat. He developed a problem with migraines - thank God," she says with a laugh. "Can you imagine, a Jewish mother being glad her son gets migraines!"

Miodowik says that, despite her strong views, she has never tried to pressure her sons in their choices regarding military service - neither the two eldest, nor her 16-year-old, who has yet to enlist.
"I understand that my sons have not allowed themselves the luxury of questioning the government and the army's policy. I've tried to teach them only by example. I have never tried to bring them around to my point of view.

"I think my children respect my views, respect my position and respect the fact that I am taking a stand."

Miodowik immigrated from South Africa in 1966, and herself served in the army. While she says she has not been heavily involved in "Israeli" politics, there is a family tradition of activism. Her mother was a member of the Black Sash, a South African women's group that fought apartheid.

Miodowik, who lives in Jaffa, became active in Four Mothers after meeting Linda Ben-Zvi while teaching alongside her at Tel Aviv University.

Miodowik and the other women sense that "Israeli" public opinion has moved in their direction over the past year-and-a-half, toward favoring a withdrawal from Lebanon. "I think there's much more acceptance of the notion now," Miodowik says.

"When I joined the group, the concept of withdrawing from the security zone was quite a revolutionary thought. During our demonstrations in Jerusalem there were even people who spat on us."

Griver noted that in recent demonstrations, she has observed a much more supportive response from the public. "There is more horn honking when we stand at intersections; and more people are willing to take our material, and read it."

"It is still not enough," Ginzburg-Shahar jumps in. "I hope there are still many more people who would join us."

Miodowik is skeptical about this. ""Israeli" society is still very, very conservative. We feel too fragile to really engage in the self-criticism and soul-searching needed to make such a decision."

All the women cite the fact of the founders and leaders of the group being not only the mothers of soldiers but living on the country's northern border as helping to give their group a moral authority they wouldn't otherwise possess.

"When people say that we Tel Avivians are not from the North, not putting our own lives on the line, we can say that "we" as a group are from the North - as well as from the center and the south," Ben-Zvi says.

Nor are they all mothers, she stresses. Members are not required to be mothers of soldiers; people of all ages, including many men, have joined the group.

"We considered changing the name in order to be more inclusive," says Ben-Zvi, "but decided that the group had already caught attention with the name Four Mothers.

"Besides, we wanted to honor the founders of the group."
