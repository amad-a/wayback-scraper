  December Saturday 02 -2000:

Armed forces warn that further Hizbollah attacks on "Israeli" soldiers or civilians will lead to retaliation against troops in Lebanon(1)

"Israel" is threatening Syria with air strikes against its military bases in Lebanon if there are further border attacks on "Israeli" soldiers or civilians by Hizbollah guerrillas.

Such warnings were common during "Israel's "occupation of south Lebanon but they have resumed following fresh Hizbollah attacks. They raise anew the alarming spectre of a wider Middle East conflict.

The threat against Syria's 20,000 troops in Lebanon was outlined yesterday by a senior "Israeli" security source. He said Syria possessed the power to rein in Hizbollah, but instead Bashar al-Assad, its president, encouraged it. The Syrians did not understand the consequences of their "dangerous policies", said the source.

Hizbollah recently renewed attacks on "Israel", capturing four soldiers in the last two months and killing one with a roadside bomb. It is acting on the pretext that "Israel" is still occupying the Shebaa Farms, a small pocket of what it claims was occupied by "Israel" as part of the Golan Heights. The United Nations has ruled it to be on the "Israeli" side of the "blue line" that marks out the "Israeli"-occupied Golan from Lebanon.

"Israel's "annoyance with Syria's new young president has been heightened by disappointment that he has not proved any more likely to succumb to "Israeli" demands than his father, Hafez al-Assad, who died in June. The bizarre, but widely circulated notion that "Dr Bashar's" British training as an ophthalmologist and his interest in computers would soften his stance on Syrian interests  making him, in western eyes, a relative "moderate"  has predictably proved to be no more than diplomatic guff.

The "Israeli" military is alarmed by Bashar al-Assad's eagerness to forge closer relations with Iraq in contrast to his father, whose hostility towards Saddam Hussein reached a peak in 1991 when he supported Operation Desert Storm. Last month Izzat Ibrahim, a member of the Revolutionary Command Council, was the most senior Iraqi official for two decades to visit Damascus. Reports abound that Iraq is piping oil to Syria in defiance of sanctions.

Bashar al-Assad "was expected to be more moderate due to his western education," said the "Israeli" security source, who requested anonymity. "But he is behaving just the opposite way. He is very extreme. He has turned very hard in the direction of the Iraqis." If "Israel" gets embroiled in a conflict with Damascus, the source warned, then Iraq would be "delighted" to join in.

This week, "Israel" bombarded a Lebanese town after a Hizbollah bomb killed one of its soldiers. But Ehud Barak, the "Israeli" prime minister, has held back from retaliation over the captured men  three soldiers and a reserve colonel  because he did not want to open another front while battling the Palestinian intifada.

"Israeli" troops shot dead two more Palestinians yesterday  one, a 12-year-old boy  after unrest erupted following the first Friday prayers of Ramadan.

Although the "Israeli" Army principally blame the Syrian president for their embarrassing setbacks at the hands of Hizbollah, it is also pointing a finger at the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon. It claims that the guerrillas who seized the three soldiers were disguised as UN peacekeepers, using equipment acquired from an Indian battalion. A UN spokesman has called the report incorrect. The story does, however, smack of a larger "Israeli" publicity campaign  an effort to undermine Palestinian efforts to persuade the United Nations to send an observer force to the occupied territories.

Lebanese shepherd looses foot after stepping on landmine(2)

An 18-year-old Lebanese shepherd had to have his foot amputated Friday after he stepped on a landmine in southern Lebanon, police said.

Abbas Ali Fakih was injured when the mine exploded near the town of Bent Jbeil in the former "Israeli"-occupied border zone in southern Lebanon.

On November 14, the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon announced that its mine clearing units had removed 1,520 explosive devices.

Hizbullah scoffs at Jewish states threats to attack
Any aggression will cost the enemy a high price(1)

The mounting war of words between Hizbullah and Israel stepped up over the weekend with the resistance warning that the Jewish state would pay a high price for launching attacks into Lebanon.
The enemy is incapable of committing any stupid or hostile adventure against Lebanon, since any aggression will cost the enemy a high price via the response of the resistance, Mohammed Raad, the head of Loyalty to Resistance Bloc, said in a speech Sunday in Ainata.
The Israeli threats express the enemys fear, said Raad, that resistance operations will continue or become more intensive on the Shibaa Farms front, putting it in a bind in Lebanon as it deals with the problem it faces in Palestine due to the intifada.
Through its threats, he added, the enemy is trying to find a gap in the national consensus about the resistance, which is determined to return the last bit of our occupied land in the Shibaa Farms and elsewhere.
A senior Israeli security source delivered a stern warning to Syria on Friday that if Hizbullah continued its attacks in the Shibaa Farms, Israel might respond with assaults on Syrian troops in Lebanon.
The source said that Hizbullahs operations against Israel in the Shibaa Farms could lead to a regional conflict pitting Israel against Syria and Iraq.
The US warned Syria last week that further Hizbullah attacks could lead to massive retaliation. Lebanons ambassador to Washington, Farid Abboud, received similar warnings last week from a senior official with the National Security Council in Washington.
A European diplomat closely involved in the peace process said that the international community was seriously concerned that events in the Middle East may spin out of control. He likened the escalation to a series of expanding concentric circles.
In the center is the intifada in the West Bank and Gaza, which has encouraged Hizbullah to launch military operations against the Israelis in the Shibaa Farms. If Israel fulfills its threat to attack Syrian military targets in Lebanon, the fighting could escalate to a war between the two countries.
Iraq, which is having a rapprochement with Syria, may feel encouraged to side with Syria in a conflict with Israel. Other Arab states, including Egypt and Jordan, would face increasing pressure to ally themselves with Syria and Iraq.
The diplomat said that there was a strong mood in Israel to teach the Lebanese a lesson. This sentiment is tempered by the potential backlash against an assault on Lebanon.
The diplomat said that the air assault on Gaza on Nov. 20 in retaliation for the fatal attack on a bus carrying Jewish settlers had backfired. US Secretary of State Madeleine Albright described the air raid as inappropriate and Egypt recalled its ambassador.
Albrights response surprised the Israelis and the recall of the Egyptian ambassador was a big shock, the source said.
Consequently, Israel will carefully weigh the benefits of satisfying public opinion with an attack on Lebanon against the potential cost in diplomatic terms as well as an upsurge in violence in northern Israel.
The watchword in Israel at the moment is smart, focused action, the diplomat said.
Information Minister Ghazi Aridi accused Israeli Premier Ehud Barak of trying to distract attention from an internal political crisis by threatening to attack Syria and Lebanon.
Israel is threatening us to get out of the crisis its stuck in. We wont be surprised if something happens. Lebanon wont give in to Israeli pressure, Aridi said on Egypts Sawt al-Arab Radio.
Syrias Ath-Thawra newspaper said Saturday that Israel was preparing plans for aggression against Syria.
Israel makes threats, and we should take them seriously, it warned, because Israel has always turned to aggression to escape from internal problems.
Ath-Thawra went on to say that the Israelis prefer to ignore the fact that Syria will never compromise on national sovereignty or on (demands for) an Israeli withdrawal from the Golan to the (demarcation) line of June 4, 1967.
Meanwhile, it added, we are consolidating our defensive military capacity because no strategy, even of peace, is possible without force.
Jordanian Ambassador Anmar Hammoud, who met Premier Rafik Hariri on Saturday, said: I dont think that Israel is going to undertake any new adventure against  Syria, Lebanon or any other Arab state.
I dont think the threats are real, he said, but aim to contain the domestic situation.

 

Diplomats interpret Israeli warnings(2)

Foreign Ministry sources said Sunday that Israel had three objectives when it warned Syria to stop Hizbullah from attacking Israeli troops.
The first goal was related to Israels security. Despite increased Israeli security measures, the sources said, Hizbullah had repeatedly succeeded in carrying out operations within areas still occupied by Israel.
The sources said that a disagreement had arisen between the armys general command and the command of northern Israel, which advocates a heavy military response.
The Israeli Armys general command, meanwhile, believes that any response to Hizbullah must be limited, so as not to escalate the conflict or encourage Hizbullah to respond with more sophisticated weapons.
The sources added that an Israeli warning on Friday of a heavy strike was taken seriously, as it comes on the heels of similar US and European warnings about curbing Hizbullah attacks.
The sources said the second objective was economic: by striking at Lebanese infrastructure and utilities, Israel might hope to paralyze the states attempt to get back on its feet.
The third objective was to increase Israeli Prime Minister Ehud Baraks popularity prior to the Israeli elections next May.
But European and Arab diplomatic sources ruled out the possibility of a large-scale Israeli strike against Lebanon or Syrian troops. Such an attack, they said, would lead Hizbullah to retaliate, which would halt the regional peace process indefinitely.
Thus, instead of escalating the conflict, they said Israel would confine itself to a more limited response.
The same sources said Israels warning could lead to a temporary freeze in Hizbullah activities. It would also allow UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan to mediate a solution to both the disputed Shibaa Farms and the Lebanese detainees before the end of January, the date for renewing UNIFILs term.

 

Land mines wound five in South over weekend(3)

Luck finally ran out for Mohammed Sweid and his brother, Ali, when they tried to defuse their 76th mine near their home in Dibbine in the South. The two young boys were rushed to hospital on Saturday after a mine thought to have been left behind by Israeli troops exploded near them.
Mohammed Hijazi, 25, who detonated the mine, had his left leg amputated and was also treated for burns.
Sweid, 8, and his five-year-old brother, Ali, were near Hijazi when the mine exploded, and were injured in the face and legs. Both were taken to Marjayoun Public Hospital.
Mohammed and Ali had dismantled around 75 anti-personnel mines in a field near their home before the Israeli withdrawal in May.
From his hospital bed, Mohammed described the events that led to the explosion on Saturday. He said that he and his brother were fixing their bicycle when Hijazi, whom they did not know, asked Ali to accompany him to the mine field.
Hijazi told the boys that he wanted to inform the army about the field so that it would be cleared of mines. Hijazi and Ali had dismantled four land mines by the time Mohammed joined them. He suddenly saw Hijazi stepping on a land mine.
I told him not to lift his foot because there was a mine under it, Mohammed said. He didnt take me seriously, but I was quick enough to push my brother on the ground and lie over him before it exploded.
Hijazi had lifted his foot and the mine exploded in his face. I saw parts of his foot flying with the explosion and he fell on the ground shouting, Mohammed recalled. He then ran to his house where his mother called for help.
Mohammed said he learned to dismantle mines from a documentary on Israeli television.
I wasnt afraid, said Mohammed, because I quickly learned that the mines detonator should not be touched. The brothers stored the defused mines on the roof of their home.
On Sunday morning, after his release from the hospital, Ali was back playing near the scene of the explosion. He gestured to the mine field and said that soldiers had inspected  and cordoned off the area. About 28 land mines were found on the site.
Mohammed and Alis father Ahmad, a 55-year-old taxi driver, said he knew that his two children dismantled mines and repeatedly forbade them from doing so, in vain.
Unexploded ordnance and land mines claimed two other victims over the weekend.
Alaa Merhi, 17, was seriously wounded on Sunday by an artillery shell that exploded while he was trying to defuse it in Qaaqaaiet al-Jisr.
Merhi was taken to the Najda Shaabiya Hospital in Nabatieh where he received first-aid treatment before being rushed to Rizk Hospital in Beirut due to serious injuries to his eyes.
Security sources said Merhi found an 81mm mortar shell near his home and was trying to open it when it exploded. They said the shell had been left behind by Israeli troops.
In Braachit on Saturday, Issam Missilmani, 25, lost both feet when he stepped on a land mine near an old Israeli-allied militia outpost.
Missilmani was taken to an Irish UNIFIL position and then transferred to Jabal Amel Hospital in Tyre.

South Lebanon slowly starts to lose its mines but too late for some(3)

Mohamed Sowaid is nine and has multiple shrapnel wounds. His brother Ali  equally mutilated  is only five. Issam Missilmani is a 33-year-old fisherman who had his right foot and left hand blown off.

Alaa Hussein is 17 and Mohamed Hijazi is 22; both were blown up, Mohamed losing his left foot. And they are just the mine casualties of southern Lebanon in one day this month.

In all, 11 civilians have been killed and at least 56 seriously wounded in the fields around their homes since the Israeli army retreated out of Lebanon in May, leaving behind 70,000 mines.

The figure is official. The four volumes of documents and minefield maps, which the withdrawing Israeli army handed to the United Nations after they ended their 22-year occupation of 10 per cent of Lebanon seven months ago are a history of warfare, of No 4 Israeli antipersonnel mines and No 10 plastic mines, of booby-traps and minefields atop minefields.

In all, the UN believes there are 130,000 mines and booby-traps and unexploded bombs scattered across the harsh, brown hills of southern Lebanon, some of them dating back to the French mandate and the Vichy French-Allied battles of the Second World War.

Fresh from 10 months in Kosovo, Don Macdonald, 46, from Forres in Grampian  after 22 years as an RAF bomb disposal officer  sits in his office at Naqqoura on the Lebanese-Israeli border with computer banks that show him the location of the tiniest bits of ordnance in the wadis and thickets of this dangerous countryside. The Israelis handed over a list of 288 booby-traps. At Kfar Roumane was "a [booby] trapped vest [flak jacket] and side charge", near Yohmor was "a trapped LAW [Light AntiArmour Weapon]". Both were left during the occupation for Hizbollah guerrillas to pick up.

Intriguingly, their location proves that the Israelis sent patrols right through the UN peace-keeping lines to leave these booby-traps close to the Litani river.

"So far, the UN's Ukrainian battalion has destroyed 2,135 mines to clear land used by UN troops. The Lebanese government has to de-mine most of southern Lebanon; and, so far, the authorities have scarcely started to fence off the minefields. One of the little boys so badly wounded had seen a television programme about mines and tragically decided to try his own hand at clearance.

There are Russian PMD-6 mines  left by the Israelis or the Palestinians  and French mines from 1947, left along the coast in the very last days of the mandate. In some places, Israeli minefields are planted on top of Second World War minefields, layers of Lebanon's terrifying history waiting to explode and re-explode under future generations. A three-man UN mine action coordination cell operates with the UN Interim Force in Lebanon, its New Zealand boss, Greg Lindstrom, paying tribute to the detail of the maps the Israelis handed over, which included suspected minefields left by earlier occupiers.

"There's a peace dividend to all this," he says. "Clearing minefields means that people can come back to their lands. I saw this in Mozambique. It's a great feeling to see these people return to farm their land." But the task that faces Lebanon, let alone the UN, is awesome. Of the Israeli mines, 50,644 lie along the UN's "blue line"  roughly corresponding to the international frontier  while another 48 clusters of minefields, containing 7,529 devices, are placed elsewhere.

Around every Israeli artillery position and outpost of Israels now defunct South Lebanon Army militia lie acres of ordnance waiting to explode. Children regularly play in the old redoubts; at least 45 of these have minefields round them. More than 288 Israeli booby-traps  including bombs disguised as rocks  lie undefused among the rocks and vegetation.

Are they inherently evil things, these lethal little creatures amid the dark, beautiful hills? "All weapons are," Mr Lindstrom says. Mr Macdonald is more philosophical. "In the hands of someone in the military, used in a military sense, mapped and taken away, that's one thing. It's the mines that are unmapped and left to kill others. It's not the mine that's the problem. It's the people who use them."
