UN condemns “Israel’s” latest border violation with Lebanon…(2)

By Robert Fisk in the Wazzani river valley, southern Lebanon

With the naked eye, you can see the yellow bulldozer and the long trail of dust as it pushes the foundations for a new frontier fence north into Lebanon. The Indian UN soldiers watch it through fieldglasses. "How can we tell how far it will go?" Major Chhahal asks. "They've been doing this work for three days, right on the edge of Gaghar."

It all looks innocent. But it is not. Just days before the elections that may turn Ariel Sharon into a prime minister, the “Israeli” army is building a new border fence on Lebanese territory and in clear violation of UN Security Council Resolution 425.

The UN has condemned the "violation of the Blue Line," the UN's own south Lebanon frontier beyond which the “Israelis” agreed not to go. The Hizbollah guerrillas who hounded the “Israeli” army out of Lebanon less than a year ago, and who have their men watching scarcely half a mile away, are well aware of what is happening in Gaghar – and of the excuse it gives them to strike once more at “Israeli” troops.

Like all Lebanese dramas, this one has a beautiful complexity all its own. For when the “Israelis” retreated last May, the UN drew its blue line through the middle of the Arab village of Gaghar. Two-thirds of the village is on Lebanese territory, the lower third is in occupied Syria; the town's few hundred inhabitants are Syrian Alawites – the same sect as Syria's President, Bashar al-Assad – who cling to their Syrian citizenship but hold “Israeli” identity papers. And since the “Israelis” could not build their frontier fence through the middle of the town – and since the residents refused to accept a frontier gate on the “Israeli”-occupied Syrian side – it looks as though the “Israelis” are willing to violate the UN's demarcation to mark a border at least 150 feet inside Lebanese territory.

Because they cannot run barbed wire through the village, the “Israelis” call Gaghar a "soft underbelly" on the Lebanese border, through which guerrillas could infiltrate into Lebanon. Already, “Israel” has violated the blue line by sending ambush patrols into the northern, Lebanese end of Gaghar at night.

But that is hardly the fault of the people of Gaghar. They did not ask “Israel” to occupy their village when it captured the Golan Heights from Syria in 1967 and then annexed the land against international law. The “Israelis”' security problem, they say, is of “Israel’s” own making. If “Israel” left Syrian territory, it would not have to worry about this stretch of frontier between Syria and Lebanon.

The UN, many of whose soldiers are positioned only metres from “Israeli” bunkers, are well aware that they will be caught in a firefight if the Hizbollah decide to use this violation as a justification for attack; which is why Major General Seth Kofi Obeg, the UN's commander, went to complain to General Gaby Ashkenazi, the head of “Israel’s” Northern Command, on Sunday. Not to worry, General Ashkenazi said, it is not an annexation of Lebanese territory. It is the people of Gaghar who are building the new fence, he went on, not the “Israeli” army.

This does not fool the UN. Indeed, there is as much chance that “Israel” would allow Syrians under occupation to decide the route of the border fence as there is that General Ashkenazi keeps a crocodile in his bathroom.

So, earlier this week, Major- General Obeg's adviser, Timur Goksel, took a helicopter along the blue line to look for himself. Flying over the gorge of the Wazzani river, he hovered over the construction. "They are putting in concrete foundations to 50 metres [inside Lebanon] in what looks like preparation to extend the line northward," he said. "The “Israelis” are creating another point of friction along the border."

Gaghar lies perilously close to Chebaa farms, Lebanese territory that was policed by Syria after 1947 and then captured and, in effect, annexed by “Israel” in 1967, with Golan. Already, three “Israeli” soldiers have been captured by Hizbollah on the farmlands and there have been two infiltration attempts in the past two months.

How far will the new fence stretch into Lebanon? In all, it might take in several square miles; big enough to cause a major crisis in southern Lebanon. Meanwhile, beyond the soft waters of the Wazzani, over the long grass and buried minefields north of the village, the bulldozers roar on. In three days, the UN says, it will have a better idea of where the fence is going. Until then, Major Chhahal and his men go on watching. So do the Hizbollah.

Israelis justify draconian strategy by creating false links to Hizbullah…(2)

There has been a noticeable tendency in recent days for Israel to widen the circle of blame for planned acts of violence in the Jewish state, beyond that of the Palestinians, to include Hizbullah and by extension Iran.
The first incident was the announcement last weekend that Israel has been holding since Jan. 5 Jehard Shuman, a Lebanese-born British national, on suspicion that he was planning guerrilla attacks inside Israel on the orders of Hizbullah.
Hizbullah has refused to comment on Shuman’s arrest, neither confirming nor denying that he was working on behalf of the party.
The second incident was the assassination of Massoud Ayad, a Lieutenant Colonel in Force 17, the personal security unit of Palestinian President Yasser Arafat.
Ayad was killed in Gaza when his car was struck by a missile fired from a helicopter gunship.
The Israeli Army promptly issued a list of accusations against Ayad, charging him with having planned and carried out bomb and mortar attacks against the Netzarim settlement in Gaza, smuggling arms and drugs, and heading a Hizbullah cell in the Palestinian territories.
The Israeli Army said that Ayad visited Lebanon last summer for training and to receive instructions from Hizbullah.
“He maintained continuous contact with Hizbullah officials in Lebanon and was activated by them to carry out terrorist attacks inside the Gaza Strip in the name of Hizbullah,” the Israeli military said. “In addition, (Ayad) was involved in the planning and attempted kidnapping of Israeli soldiers,” it added.
The third, possibly related, incident were comments from the US State Department aired on CNN regarding its unease at a meeting of anti-Israeli and anti-American militant groups in Beirut last month.
In particular, one US official said that the presence of Iranian delegates to the meeting was a “cause of concern.”
Since the Al-Aqsa intifada began last September, Iran has “stepped up its terrorist activity … and stepped up its planning for terrorist attacks,” one US official maintained.
The meeting in question was a well-publicized conference in which some 200 Arab and Muslim figures met to discuss measures to protect Jerusalem from Judaization.
The gathering was organized by Hizbullah, Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and the Islamic National Conference. Among the participants was Ali Akhbar Mohtashemi, an Iranian MP, and a former Iranian ambassador to Damascus who played a major role in the founding of Hizbullah in the 1980s.
Ironically, the flurry of accusations against Hizbullah comes at a time when the resistance has been noticeably quiet on the Shebaa Farms front. The last operation carried out by Hizbullah in the area was a roadside bomb ambush on Nov. 26, in which an Israeli soldier was killed.
“There’s been too much mentioning of Hizbullah lately,” said a senior Western military source. The source suggested that the Israeli military was deliberately implicating Hizbullah and Iran in planned attacks in the Palestinian territories and Israel to serve as a pretext for any military action against the resistance in Lebanon. “They are setting up the stage, just in case,” the source said.
Neither outgoing Israeli Premier Ehud Barak nor his hawkish successor, Ariel Sharon, are expected to launch pre-emptive raids into Lebanon for the time being. Given the sentiment in the Arab world, Israel is unlikely to exacerbate hostilities by provocative actions outside the main theater of conflict in the West Bank and Gaza.
However, linking Hizbullah and Iran to acts of violence in the Palestinian territories and Israel planned or executed serves to defuse any misgivings that exist over the Israeli government’s controversial policy of assassinating Palestinian activists.
Ayad may or may not have had links with Hizbullah, but that is beside the point, said a European diplomat.
“Palestinians come in all shapes and sizes, from those you negotiate with in five-star hotels to stone-throwers,” the diplomat said. “But by renaming them collectively as Hizbullah terrorists you are discrediting them to the fullest and gaining a 100 percent acceptance rate among the Israeli public” for the Israeli Army’s actions.
Prime Minister Rafik Hariri echoed that view in his dismissal on Thursday of Israel’s claim that Ayad was working for Hizbullah.
“It’s easier for the Israelis to say that they have assassinated a Palestinian belonging to Hizbullah than an aide to Arafat,” he told reporters in Paris.
Hizbullah and Iran are regarded in Israel, correctly, as being implacably hostile towards the Jewish state. References to Hizbullah and Iran lend the impression that the Israeli Army is not only contending with the Palestinians whose plight draws sympathy domestically and internationally but also a broader, more sinister, pan-Islamist front.
The Israeli Army has long used Hizbullah as a “bogeyman” to its own advantage usually to win increases to the defense budget.
This point was recently illustrated by an Israeli columnist, Reuven Pedatzur, in an unprecedented opinion piece in the Haaretz daily newspaper.
“Where’s the wisdom in the commanders of the Middle East’s strongest and most advanced army glorifying the threat of the Hizbullah, broadcasting to the public a sense of fear in the face of a threat from such a tiny organization?” Pedatzur asked. “The absurdity of attributing to the Hizbullah a strategic threat is symptomatic of a certain routine on the part of the senior command.   February Monday 26 -2001:

Butros Ghali backs claim to Shibaa Farms …(1)

Former United Nations Secretary-General Butros Butros Ghali supports Lebanon’s stance that the Shibaa Farms belongs to the Lebanese, but remains non-commital on whether the use of force should be advocated to liberate the disputed territory.
“I believe that the Shibaa Farms are Lebanese property, and that Lebanon has every right to recuperate its territorial integrity,” Butros Ghali told The Daily Star in an interview Saturday. However, Butros Ghali refused to comment on the latest operations by Hizbullah in the disputed area or on how Lebanon should regain the Shibaa.
“I am talking about the principle of Lebanon recuperating its territorial integrity,” he said. “This goal could be achieved through negotiation, mediation, or any other means, but I have no comment at that level.”
At the same time, however, Butros Ghali said, “negotiation to recover one’s land is a must, it is a sacred duty.”
Butros Ghali left Lebanon Sunday after a five-day official visit as secretary-general of the Francophone Organization. Beirut will be hosting the annual Francophone Summit in October.

“ “Israeli” warplanes buzzed Lebanon Sunday, breaking the sound barrier over several regions and triggering panic among skiers on the slopes of Mount Lebanon, an AFP correspondent reported.

Two “Israeli” warplanes caused two supersonic bangs over the snow-covered western slopes of the Mount Lebanon range, which was crowded with Sunday skiers.
