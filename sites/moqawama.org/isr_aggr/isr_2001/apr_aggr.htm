Striking at “Israel’s” support with a FIST…(1)

With bags under her eyes, Lana Khalil leans back into the sunlight and recalls how she started an anti-Zionist “divestment campaign” on campuses across the United States.
Khalil, a Lebanese who grew up in Nigeria, has only spent two years in Lebanon ­ enough time to witness firsthand what “Israeli” aggression is all about.
It was her first year here when “Israel” began its two-week bombing campaign in April of 1996, what it named operation Grapes of Wrath. The operation was responsible for the Qana massacre, when “Israel” shelled a UN base and killed more than 100 women, children and elderly seeking shelter.
“When I moved to Lebanon, the images I saw epitomized the dispossession and imperialism of a nation. Che Guevara once said that sympathy with suffering somewhere in the world is the root of revolutionary work,” says Khalil, sitting cross-legged in her sandals and loose tank top on a bench in Sanayeh park.
Witnessing the aftermath of the Qana attack prompted her
to join a student group called Fight “Israeli” State Terrorism (FIST) at the American Community School, where she was a student, that year.
It wasn’t long before she extended her sense of compassion to the Palestinian community when she began volunteering as a teacher at the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps.
“When I started developing such close ties with my students, I wasn’t fighting simply for inalienable rights but for these people whom I was so emotionally attached to,” she says.
It was only natural for her to establish FIST at Sarah Lawrence College in New York in her freshman year. The organization focused on educating students on the Middle East and “deconstructing myths systematically propagated in the media,” Khalil explains.
FIST then began action campaigns that included teach-ins, lectures and a recreation of Khiam prison on campus.
The original 15 members were mainly comprised of Jewish, “Israeli” and other international students. Khalil is the only Arab in the organization.
The group’s dialogues with Jewish student groups even succeeded in recruiting another 10 members who considered themselves to be Zionists, according to Khalil.
With the start of the intifada  FIST began working toward starting a divestment campaign, similar to that of the anti-apartheid movement in the 1980s that occurred on college campuses nation-wide.
The group plans on lobbying the university’s administration in May to issue a statement that Sarah Lawrence supports the intifada and the Palestinian right to self-determination and condemns “Israeli” brutality.
Sarah Lawrence would then actively divest (as opposed to invest) in any company that has shares in “Israel”, as with the parallel anti-apartheid movement that managed to oust South Africa’s racist regime.
If the school doesn’t agree, FIST will occupy the university president’s home and start a hunger strike, says Khalil. Because of the level of awareness her group has created, she is expecting the majority of students on campus to participate.
“This year, we’ve become the most active group on campus,” she says. “Before people came to Sarah Lawrence, they didn’t know anything about Palestine. Now it’s become part of their everyday lives.”
With the start of the intifada, Khalil has taken FIST’s activism to a new level by focusing on a “complete bombardment of the anti-Arab and anti-Palestinian system,” she adds.
Every so often the group stands on tables in the campus cafeteria and tells diners about those who have died in the fighting, where and how, just in the time-span of their meal. Long lists of the victims are distributed at the same time.
Occasionally, these lists have also been represented through chalk outlines of corpses all over campus.
Khalil says they were especially successful in what she called “guerrilla theater.” The idea is to attract people’s attention by staging a fistfight in a public space and then breaking into an argument about stealing land and other policies of the Zionist state.
Khalil even succeeded in ensuring two hours of radio time per week and two pages in the campus’s daily newspaper dedicated to the intifada. They also started an underground bi-monthly “zine” (a small, informal magazine) that includes Palestinian poetry, political analysts’ articles, and student opinion pieces and has a circulation of about 3,000.
Their group has gained so much clout that they were able to set up two projection screens on the main campus green for a full day that displayed pictures of “Israeli” brutality.
“Everything is building up to our action in May,” she said. “It’s gotten so that FIST is a very big name on campus with two implications ­ either we’re a strong activist group fighting for a just cause, or we’re a fanatic anti-”Israeli” group.”
Student groups at 15 other universities, including Princeton University, New York University, Michigan University and Columbia University, plan on participating on their campuses on the same day.
Khalil is hoping to have another 60 universities, including some in the Arab world and Europe, join the movement. But even a handful, according to Khalil, will be enough to make the news and a statement that resistance to “Israel’s” apartheid policy does exist.
“I’m working 19 hours a day on this,” she says. “I’m completely fueled by the need to do this, because it’s still happening, and by seeing everyone else in the same struggle and giving so much to this cause.”

“ A bomb placed in a tractor failed to explode Tuesday, in the third such booby-trapped attempt in about two weeks in the former “Israeli”-occupied border zone of southern Lebanon, police said.

Land-mine awareness  at schools…( 1)

The national awareness campaign on land mines in the former occupation zone was launched at the Jaber Cultural Center in Nabatieh on Wednesday.
The ceremony, held under the patronage of Army Commander Michel Suleiman ­ represented by Brigadier George Sawaya ­ focused on the need for “tight cooperation” between the army, the National Demining Office, and civil society.
Lectures will take place in villages and schools in the South and the Western Bekaa.
Participants in the ceremony then attended a lecture at the Kfar Tibnit Public School, where explanations were provided to 600 students as part of the awareness campaign.
Students watched a documentary on land mines, while posters were distributed among them.
Islamic Health Committee member Hassan Ghandour said that most students responded well to explanations, adding that many of them were already informed about the subject, due to the presence of several minefields around the village.
Wassim Tabaja, 12, said that army officers warned residents against approaching the former “Israeli”-proxy South Lebanon Army militia outposts on Ali Taher hill and at Bourj, as they are surrounded by minefields. Tabaja recounted that a friend of his lost his leg when a mine exploded under his feet near Ali Taher.
“I’ll ask my friends not to play there,” he said. ““Israel” wants to kill us.”
Sara Faqih, 14, said that “we were pleased by what army officers and paramedics told us,” adding, “And we hope this campaign helps residents keep away from land mines.”
Students Ali and Adnan Faqih stuck a poster of land-mine varieties on the classroom wall.
Faqih said he once found a mine near his home similar to one portrayed in the poster. He said he had notified an army officer, who removed the mine.
Nabatieh’s deputy mayor, Adham Jaber, proposed that all municipalities affected by land mines should erect warning signs, while Kfar Roummane Mayor Sattam Abu Zeid said he hoped the campaign would translate into effective measures.
“Southerners should not pay the price for such weapons,” said Abu Zeid.
“I hope the demining project is not neglected.”
Elsewhere, demonstration sessions on land mines were also held at two schools in Bint Jbeil and Tibnin.

 

Students learn to live with “Israel’s” explosive legacy…(2)

Students in Qlaya set aside their mathematics and geography books on Wednesday to receive a lesson that could end up saving their lives.
As part of a national campaign on land-mine awareness, a team from UNIFIL’s Finnish battalion demonstrated to the students how land mines work and the precautions that must be taken when encountering them.
The battalion presented two 25-minute sessions of detailed explanations on land mines to 150 students at the Qlaya Secondary School.
Rusting defused anti-personnel and anti-tank mines, as well as cluster bombs and explosive charges camouflaged by fiber-glass “rocks,” were displayed on tables. Signs warning of land mines and unexploded ordinance were also presented.
Finnish Captain Jarki Polkknuen told The Daily Star that these sessions would be held every two months.
The school principal, Father Mansour Hakim, told The Daily Star that the demonstration taught students how to react if they accidentally entered a minefield and how to recognize the deadly devices.
Hakim said that each student who attended the demonstration session should pass on their newly acquired knowledge to their families and friends.
He added that some of the mines planted in Marjayoun dated back to World War II, when the German-allied Vichy French fought Free French and Australian troops.
The Finnish officer showed students “Israeli”, Russian, Yugoslav, American, and French land mines.
“If we see these land mines, we should not get near them, nor touch them,” Polkknuen warned students.
Since “Israel” withdrew its forces from the South last May, at least 10 people have been killed and 35 wounded in explosions caused by land mines and unexploded ordinance.
The UN estimates that some 130,000 land mines exist in south Lebanon, 70,000 left behind by the “Israelis”. The bulk of the “Israeli” mines are found in the 188 separate minefields the occupiers strung along the Lebanese side of the border.
One UNIFIL officer has estimated that it could take 30 years to clear all the mines.
In addition to the land mines, “Israel” planted 288 booby-trapped devices, mainly on the edges of the former occupation zone. In most cases the booby-traps consisted of ordinance, such as artillery shells, rigged to hidden explosive charges. But the “Israelis” also used mundane everyday items, including cameras and flags.
The Finnish expert explained to his audience how they could be deceived by cluster bombs which resemble small balls.
“If you see such a device that doesn’t belong to you, don’t touch it,” he said.
He also urged students to replace damaged or stolen warning signs. The army announced last week that land-mine warning signs are being stolen throughout the South, posing a risk to visitors unacquainted with the area.
Student Dani Khoury asked the officer what to do if someone stepped on a mine, but did not lift their foot.
“This happens only in movies,” said Polkknuen. “If you step on a mine, it will blow up.”

Spain to train Lebanese troops in mine removal…(2)

A group of Lebanese Army officers will travel to Spain next week to attend a land-mine clearing course given by a joint Spanish and Russian military team.
The squad of 20 officers and non-commissioned officers are scheduled to depart for Madrid on Monday for a six-week course involving all aspects of humanitarian demining.
“They won’t learn just how to remove land mines but other issues such as topography, communications … it’s an integral program,” said Guillermo Ardizone, consul at the Spanish Embassy.
The scheme for a joint training program was conceived by the Russian government which approached Spain as a partner due to that country’s considerable experience in demining.
The Spanish Army has cleared mines in Peru and Ecuador in South America and Angola and Mozambique in Africa. Some 1,200 Spanish troops are presently deployed in Kosovo on land-mine clearing duties.
Russia and Spain drew up a profile of the project which was then presented to the Lebanese authorities.
“The Lebanese reacted very fast in accepting and choosing the people. It’s been a very smooth cooperation,” Ardizone said.
Since “Israel” withdrew its forces from the South last year, at least 10 people have been killed and another 35 wounded in land-mine explosions.
The United Nations estimates there are around 130,000 land mines in South Lebanon as well as 288 booby-trap devices and an unknown amount of unexploded ordinance.
“The knowledge gained by the Lebanese officers will allow them to act as trainers when they return,” Ardizone said.
The diplomat said that there were no immediate plans to repeat the course in the future.
“We will evaluate the project when it is completed and see what is possible,” he said.
A senior military source said that the demining course in Spain was one of several overseas training programs on landmines for Lebanese soldiers. Other countries that have taught demining techniques include the United States, the United Kingdom, Italy and Syria.
The source added that a Syrian Army demining unit is presently clearing minefields in Jabal Bir al-Dahr and Kawkaba al-Arab in the Western Bekaa.

Qanso, the ex-president of the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, said the resistance was “fully backed” by the president, the speaker and the prime minister, whom he described as considering it a “strategic necessity.”
