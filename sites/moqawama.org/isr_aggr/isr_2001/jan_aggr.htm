Israel accused of staging Shibaa Farms mortar attack(1)

Citing Hebrew inscriptions on shell casings, the Interior Ministry accused Israel of responsibility for a provocative mortar attack Wednesday that targeted the Shibaa Farms area.
The unclaimed bombardment ­ the first attack in over a month ­ comes amid increased tensions along the frontier after a spate of cross-border shootings.
The attack began at 7am, when around seven mortar shells were fired from a few hundred meters east of Halta, 2 kilometers north of the Shibaa Farms. The shells reportedly exploded harmlessly between two Israeli outposts.
The Israelis responded by firing 44 tank and mortar rounds north of the Farms. At least 60 goats were killed and 20-30 were wounded when two shells struck animal sheds in Bastara, UNIFIL said.
The shelling continued until about 8am. Israeli warplanes and pilotless reconnaissance planes also flew over the area.
An Israeli Army spokesman confirmed mortar bombs fell near Israeli positions on the border and that Israeli forces returned fire.
Veterinarians from UNIFILs Indian battalion traveled to Bastara to treat the injured animals. Women with crying children were standing by the dead animals.
Qassem Zohra, carrying the severed head of a goat, told an Agence France Presse correspondent that the United Nations should protect us. You see how Israel bombards civilians.
Fatima Zohra, 40, said that all our lifetime savings had been destroyed in a second.
Lebanese soldiers found a single 81mm mortar in an olive grove in Wadi Elias on the eastern outskirts of Halta which had been abandoned by the attackers. Empty mortar casings  were inscribed in Hebrew. The mortar and casings were transferred to Beirut.
The Interior Ministry said in a statement that the Hebrew markings raise suspicion as regards who fired the shells with a view to accusing Lebanon and to justify aggressions against liberated land in the South.
An army officer who requested anonymity said that the shelling was under investigation and said that Israel or Israeli agents were suspected of carrying out the attack.
A large number of weapons were abandoned by the Israelis and their allies during their withdrawal from Lebanon. Although the army was able to gather some weapons, many items fell into the hands of civilians and guerrillas. Israeli weapons have long been available in Lebanon.
US Ambassador David Satterfield called the mortar attack provocative, saying it violated the UN-delineated Blue Line of withdrawal and threatened stability in the area.
The Cabinet did not specifically address Satterfields comments in its weekly session, but Information Minister Ghazi Aridi said: We always hope that the US will play a fair and just role in the search for a just and comprehensive settlement.
The last attack in the Shibaa Farms occurred on Nov. 26, when Hizbullah ambushed an Israeli patrol, killing one and wounding another.
Hizbullah denied any responsibility for the latest incident.
A Hizbullah source said that the party was uncomfortable with recent developments along the border, beginning with the wounding of five demonstrators last Thursday. Another civilian was shot dead in Kfar Kila on Saturday and an Israeli civilian was shot and wounded Tuesday, reportedly by a sniper.
These are very serious incidents on the border and we are studying them, the source said. He also suggested that the period prior to the Israeli elections was one of uncertainty for Lebanon.
The Israelis have committed stupid actions before, he said, and its not impossible that they will commit stupid actions again. ­

 

UN vets rush to treat Lebanese goats hit by Israeli fire(2)

 United Nations peacekeepers, including a veterinary surgeon, rushed Wednesday to treat goats injured by an Israeli bombardment on southeastern Lebanon that killed more than 100 animals.

The team of the Indian battalion of the UN Interim Forces in southern Lebanon (UNIFIL), comprising a vet and eight relief workers, inspected the site of the bombardment in Bastara, in the Shibaa Farms.

They cleaned the goats' wounds and closed them with stitches with the help of the goatherds, an AFP correspondent saw.

"At least 100 animals died and another 100 were injured, and I do not think we can finish the treatment today," Teibre (eds: one name), the UN Indian veterinary, told AFP.

Israeli gunners pounded the isolated Bastara farm, where four families of goatherds live, after mortar bombs fired from southern Lebanon landed near a military post in northern Israel early Wednesday.

Women carrying children in tears were standing by the dead goats.

Goatherd Qassem Zahra, carrying the severed head of a goat, told AFP that "the United Nations should protect us, you see how Israel bombards civilians."

Meanwhile, UN observers toured the region to compile a report on the source of fire.

Lebanese police said more than 50 Israeli tank and artillery shells rained down on Bastara in an hour-long bombardment.

A helicopter and a pilotless reconnaissance drone had flown over the area before the bombardment, the heaviest since Lebanese Hizbullah guerrillas captured three Israeli soldiers in a raid on the Shibaa farms in October.

It was not immediately known who fired the mortar bombs, although Lebanese security forces said they had found an abandoned 81mm mortar and 11 casings at Halta, two kilometres (1.5 miles) from Shibaa.

They said they were hunting those responsible, said to have been travelling in a blue 1975 Mercedes.

Nearly 200 police and troops were scouring the roads in the area in the search for the car, Lebanese police said.

An official said it was not the work of the Hizbullah, adding that the mortar attack was "contrary to Lebanese policy."

Family returns to Bastara, despite danger (2)

The sickly odor of death hung over the Bastara farmstead. Swarms of flies and wasps were drawn to pools of congealing blood and melted fat from the rotting, partially burned corpses of tens of goats that were killed by Israeli shells last week. The sight brought tears to the eyes of Ghalia Zohra.
God, I cant bear the smell, she said, sighing and dabbing her eyes with a handkerchief.
On Wednesday, Ghalias brother, Qassem, and the rest of their extended family returned to their ramshackle collection of goat pens and stone hovels perched on an isolated hilltop on the edge of the Shibaa Farms. They had collected the decomposing animal corpses and burned them in three separate pyres.
We raised all these goats with the same affection as we raise our own children, Ghalia said. How can we but hold them dear?
The goats were killed when an Israeli mortar shell exploded in their pen early Wednesday morning during a heavy bombardment of the Bastara farmstead. Estimates of the number of dead goats vary from over 60, according to UNIFIL, to at least 200, according to the Zohras.
Remarkably, all 13 members of the Zohra clan escaped the bombardment unhurt. But the Zohras decided that living on the frontline of the Shibaa Farms was simply no longer possible, and they began to look elsewhere to live and raise their goats.
Ghalia said that Qassem had searched for alternative grazing from Naqoura, on the coast, to Kfar Shuba, 5 kilometers north of Bastara.
He couldnt find anywhere for the goats. There was nowhere else for us to go, so we have no choice but to stay. Were stuck here, Ghalia said.
The traditional attachment of southerners to their land has been sorely tested in the Zohras case. While the rest of the South embraces the calm of a post-occupation existence, liberation has spelt disaster for the family.
Despite receiving routine harassment at the hands of Israeli troops stationed in the vicinity of Bastara during the occupation, the area was the quietest sector in the former enclave. But now they are caught in the middle of attack and counter-attack as the campaign over the Shibaa Farms plods on around them.
Their nearest neighbors ­ other than the Israeli troops in the Ramta outpost towering above Bastara ­ are in Kfar Shuba, a half hours drive away along a narrow rutted track.
Ghalias other brother, Hussein and her niece, Layali, were nearly killed a month ago when Israeli troops fired mortar rounds near them as they grazed their goats just south of Bastara.
We dont take our goats further than the farm anymore, Ghalia said. Its too dangerous.
Shepherding ­ that most pastoral of lifestyles ­ has become a dangerous occupation since the border heated up along the Shibaa Farms.
Saad Abdel-Aal, 18, from Halta, one of three villages facing the Shibaa Farms, said that if he approaches the border, Israeli troops open fire at him.
The last time was two days ago, he said pointing at the hilltop Jabal Summaq outpost.
The position was barely visible in the murky late afternoon light as a blanket of gray cloud slowly descended on the mountain peaks.
Abdel-Aal, who was tending his flock with the help of several siblings, said that he had lost 50 of the 800 goats in his care since military operations began in the Shibaa Farms in October.

Combating explosive legacy of occupation(3)

Tucked away in the middle of the sprawling UNIFIL headquarters in Naqoura is a simple, single-storey building that once housed meetings of the five-nation Monitoring Group. A sign reading Monitoring Group Conference Room still hangs on the front door and the table around which the delegates from Lebanon, Syria, France, Israel and the United States sat is still in place in the center of the room.
The group attempted to protect civilians on both sides of the border from violence between the resistance and the occupying forces in accordance with the 1996 April Understanding.
But today the room is used to combat a different violence that continues to kill and wound despite Israels troop withdrawal from the South last May.
UNIFIL estimates there are 130,000 land mines throughout the former border enclave ­ the deadly legacy of occupation that will take decades to eradicate.
But before mine-clearing operations can begin effectively, it is necessary to gather detailed information on the exact location and size of minefields as well as the variety of mines that might be uncovered.
The United Nations contracted the three-man Mine Action Coordination Cell (MACC) to gather and collate this data.
Piles of maps lie on tables, more large-scale maps of mine-infested areas in the South are pinned to the wall and a bank of computers hidden beneath dust covers wait for information to be punched in.
Lying open on the central table are four folders containing data on the 70,000 land mines and 288 booby-trapped devices that the Israeli Army admits having planted in Lebanon.
We have two problems, said Greg Lindstrom, a New Zealander who heads MACC. The first problem is dealing with the Israeli mines and the second are the mines about which we have no information at all.
Included in the latter category are mines planted by Palestinian and Lebanese militias, the Syrian Army and even the Vichy French Army during World War II.
The bulk of the Israeli mines are contained in 188 minefields along the border ­ a lethal uninterrupted cordon from Ras Naqoura on the coast to opposite Shibaa in the Mount Hermon foothills. Other Israeli mines surround former outposts.
The map references of each minefield is noted along with the date it was established and the quantity and type of mines planted. For example, between May and June 1994, the Israelis surrounded the Blat outpost in the western sector ­ one of Hizbullahs favorite targets ­ with 2,012 No. 4a anti-personnel mines.
Accompanying the figures are simple sketch maps with penciled measurements showing the perimeter of the minefield and the direction of the rows of mines.
Some of these maps are written in Hebrew which is causing a problem. Also we dont have maps to accompany the statistics for all the minefields, Lindstrom said.
Other than the conventional land mine data, the Israelis also listed the booby-trapped devices.
In most cases the booby-traps consisted of ammunition, such as artillery shells, rigged to hidden explosive charges. When the shell is picked up, the charge explodes, killing the victim. But the Israelis also used unconventional means to ensnare the unsuspecting, including booby-trapped flak jackets, radars and even an Amal flag. In the last few years of the occupation, cameras became a popular lure.
Two cameras, in each one, two No. 4 demo block and two RDX sticks attached to a No. 16 igniter, reads one laconic description of a booby-trap planted in June 1999.
On one of the wall maps, the locations of the booby-trapped weapons are indicated by a rash of red dots running along ­ and more often north of ­ the perimeter of the zone. The largest clusters are found in the dense undergrowth around Yater in the western sector and along Wadi Salouqi facing the central sector ­ both used by the resistance to launch attacks and for fighters to penetrate the zone.
Lindstrom explained that MACC has conducted what is known in mine clearing parlance as a Level One Survey. This entails collecting all available information to determine the whereabouts of minefields and unexploded ordinance, such as shells, bullets or hand grenades.
The team also assesses the socio-economic impact of a minefield. A minefield on agricultural property or in a populated area will receive swifter attention than one planted in a remote, unproductive location.
The MACC team is now waiting to begin the Level Two Survey which involves precisely delineating the perimeter of each minefield.
Lindstrom, a former munitions expert in the New Zealand Army, has worked in mine clearance in Cambodia and Mozambique, two of the worst mine-afflicted countries in the world.
Its important to realize the impact the fear of mines can have, he said.
Lindstrom cited the example of an area in Mozambique which had become depopulated because the local residents believed it was infested with land mines. But we discovered only one land mine there, he recalled.
The result of the operation allowed some 5,000 people to return to their land and begin  rebuilding their communities while at the same time relieving the capital, Maputo, which had been forced to accommodate the refugees.
Lindstrom said he hoped to have the Level Two Survey completed by the end of March, but it depends on funding.
The information from the Level One and Two surveys is used to draw up detailed maps and entered into a computerized database for future reference.
Not only does such a system help in mine-clearing operations, it also assists in planning infrastructure work. For instance, the system will enable a map of the South containing all minefields to be superimposed over a similarly-scaled map of a proposed water irrigation network. Planners would be able to tell at a glance which areas required mine clearance in order for pipelines to be laid and ditches to be dug in safety.
Lindstrom said that the process was slow and methodical but would eventually help make the South a safer place to live and work.
No one likes to see young people being killed and maimed after the hostilities have finished, he said. Its a needless waste of life.

Cabinet opens new chapter in suit vs. Israel
Inquiry will probe alleged use of depleted uranium in South(1)

The government ordered an investigation on Thursday into Israels alleged use of depleted-uranium (DU) ammunition during its occupation of Lebanon.
If conclusive evidence emerges that the Israelis used DU weapons, it will be added to the list of grievances that forms the basis of a pending lawsuit to win reparations from Israel.
The Health and Environment ministries and the National Council for Scientific Research, in coordination with the army, have been charged to investigate allegations that the Israeli Army fired DU shells in the South, Information Minister Ghazi Aridi said after Thursdays Cabinet meeting.
Michel Tueni, the government-appointed lawyer handling the lawsuit, said preliminary investigations suggest there is substance to speculation that DU ammunition was used in the South.
But the presence of depleted uranium will definitely be taken into account in the suit, pending the final investigation, he told The Daily Star. Its a very important part and will require modifying the suit.
Tueni said he had raised the issue in Lebanon following the outcry in Europe over the use of DU rounds during NATOs 1999 campaign in Kosovo and the 1991 Gulf War against Iraq.
According to the US-based International Action Center, an anti-war group, at least 272 tons of DU and uranium dust were left around Iraq, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia by US and British forces during the Gulf War. A symposium in Baghdad in December 1998 found higher rates of childhood leukemia and other cancers in people living around Basra, and attributed this to DU contamination.
Steffan de Mistura, the UNs south Lebanon coordinator, said Thursday that the reported use of DU ammunition by Israel should be proven and publicized. Mistura, who met Speaker Nabih Berri, added that a scientific analysis should be conducted and its results compared with the findings in other countries.
British Ambassador Richard Kinchen told Berri that his government was willing to provide any information or assistance for an investigation.
The Israeli Army admitted last week for the first time that it has made use of DU shells for years, according to Israels Yediot Ahronot newspaper.
Israels Maariv daily has reported that Arab-Israeli MP Issam Makhoul has called for an urgent meeting of the Knessets
Foreign Affairs and Defense (War) Committee to discuss the use of depleted uranium against south Lebanon and the occupied Palestinian territories.
A 1995 report from the US Army Environmental Policy Institute asserts that Israel is one of the countries with DU munitions in its arsenal. Given Israels own nuclear program and well-developed military industry, it is also suspected of manufacturing its own DU ammunition.
An Israeli military spokesman has admitted that DU weapons were used by its navy against  marine targets but had stopped using the ammunition about 18 months ago. On warships, DU ammunition is generally used by rapid-firing gatling guns, a defensive weapon against sea-skimming anti-ship missiles.
UNIFIL spokesman Timur Goksel said the peacekeeping force had no evidence of DU weapons having been fired in the South.
We couldnt tell. Were talking of tens of thousands of rounds fired from the ground, the sea and the air. Its not something we could possibly tell without a proper scientific investigation, he said.
He added, however, that the low-intensity conflict fought in the South precluded the regular use of DU ammunition. Hizbullahs lightly armed fighters adopted hit-and-run guerrilla tactics and had no need for armored vehicles.
But Goksel admitted that it was possible the Israelis made sporadic use of DU ammunition.
South Lebanon has been a weapons laboratory for a long time, he said.
The occupation zone allowed the Israeli Army to test-fire various weapons systems and conduct training exercises with virtual impunity.
Israeli air crews practiced their skills against wadis and buildings in the South with almost no risk of being shot down. Israels Spike anti-tank missile was secretly tested in the South for 16 months from the beginning of 1998.
One Lebanese civilian was killed and at least four others wounded before the Israeli Army declassified the weapon and placed it on the international arms market.
Christopher Foss of London-based Janes Defense Weekly said that there was little reason for the Israelis to have used DU ammunition in the South.
He said that Israels Merkava tanks were capable of firing DU shells but that the expensive ammunition was impractical against the targets normally found in the battlefields of the South, such as buildings or squads of resistance fighters.
A DU round would go in one side of the building and out the other, Foss explained. It wouldnt demolish the building, just punch a hole in it. It would have no effect against a group of people either.
One of the most effective DU-firing anti-armor platforms is the US Air Forces A-10 Thunderbolt II, a tank-busting warplane equipped with 30mm gatling guns.
Israel has no A-10s but the AH-64 Apache helicopter gunship, which is equipped with a 30mm machine gun, is in service with the Israelis and has been used regularly to attack targets in Lebanon.
The International Action Center has claimed that the Apaches 30mm cannon can fire DU rounds. But Foss disagreed.
I think the Apache has different ammunition. Its primary weapon (against tanks) is the Hellfire missile, he said.
The 30mm single-barreled chain gun slung beneath the nose of the Apache is designed to fire three different types of bullet: a high-explosive dual-purpose round, a high-explosive incendiary round, and a target-practice round. None of them are tipped with DU.
