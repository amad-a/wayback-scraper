Executive Summary

 

This report details the findings of Palestine Media Watch (PMW), which conducted an analysis of how the Israeli-Palestinian conflict was covered by The Charlotte Observer in their Opinion pages and news articles between April 1, 2002 and May 31, 2002.  The purpose of this report is to raise awareness of current news coverage practices and editorial policies regarding this conflict.  Our aim is not to characterize or label The Charlotte Observer, but to examine its product and the quality and variety of what it is offering its readership.

 

PMW found sporadic examples of responsible reporting during this period: Within news articles, Israeli statements were being independently verified more often than before, instead of simply being reported as fact; more human rights organizations and NGO’s were being quoted or referenced; photos showing Palestinian suffering were printed, and a couple of stories were printed showing Palestinians as more than just militants, but also as human beings.

 

While PMW is encouraged to see the above, our findings have shown an over-whelming bias in presentation during our period of analysis when a pattern of partiality emerged regarding coverage of this conflict.  Listed below are our major concerns.  Please refer to report for more details.

 

  • Balance presentation of views:  The Op/Ed pages of The Charlotte Observer presented the Israeli perspective over-whelmingly in their publishing of Forum letters, editorials, columns and cartoons.  See page 6, Appendix A.

 

  • Use neutral terms:  The words “terror”, “terrorist”, “attack” were used regularly to describe Palestinians or Palestinian violence.  The words “in retaliation”, “in response to”, “following”, “extremists”, “crossfire”, “mistake” were used when referring to Israelis or Israeli violence.  See pages 7 – 10.

 

  • Print recipricol articles:  Human interest stories solely from Israeli perspective published much more often than human interest stories from Palestinian perspective.  Articles written from “Palestinian” perspective very often portrayed them as angry, humiliated, and militant.  “Israeli” articles portrayed them as fearful, peace-seeking, victims.  See pages 10 – 16.

 

  • Use balance in reporting:  Israeli deaths and injuries reported in vivid detail, with descriptions of personal aspects of victim’s life and the scene’s gruesome destruction.  The death and injuries of Palestinians were consistently reported as statistics with dry, to-the-point lack of emotion. See pages 16 – 18.

 

  • Give background and context:  Inaccurate information was printed as fact.  Also, context was often left out, as well as, pertinent facts or background information.  Sometimes not reporting the context or background information for the reader can be so misleading as to be considered falsification of facts by omission.  See pages 19 – 22.

 

  • Avoid politically-loaded terms:  The politically loaded and contentious terms “Jewish neighborhood” and “disputed areas” were used to describe settlements and areas within occupied territories.  See pages 22 and 23.

IV.   Definition of terms used in reference to Op/Ed pages

 

When PMW classifies Op/Ed pieces as Pro-Israel, Pro-Palestinian or Neutral in this report, we will be adopting definitions established in Eric Alterman’s "Intractable Foes, Warring Narratives", MSNBC, March 28th, 2002.

 

The first category, Pro-Israel, includes Forum letters, editorials, columns and cartoons that support Israel reflexively and without qualification or those that criticize Palestinians or Palestinian supporters.  These pieces support Israeli security over Palestinian rights.  The symbol (PI) is used to classify this category in the attached pie charts and data sheets in Appendix A.

 

Pro-Israeli articles are readily identifiable, since they reflect a well-defined worldview which rarely deviates:

 

§         Palestinians are primarily to blame for the violence;

§         Israel has offered major concessions;

§         Palestinians should have accepted Israel's Camp David concessions; and

§         Palestinian refugees should have no right of return. 

 

Beyond that, pro-Israeli pieces are striking in their omission of extremely critical issues such as U.N. resolutions pertaining to the occupation and the findings of human rights organizations.

 

The Pro-Palestinian (PP) category is used to designate pieces that highlight the illegal nature of Israeli actions (e.g., settlements) and policies (e.g., political assassinations), relying heavily on internationally recognized agreements and standards.

 

Balanced columns usually avoid faulting either side; but when faulting, both sides are taken to task.  Balanced columns tend to stress the morally obvious, the legally sound, and some very basic, factual realities. These are designated (N).

V.     Findings – Op/Ed pages

 

After monitoring the Op/Ed pages of The Charlotte Observer  from April 1, 2002 to May 31, 2002 and classifying each piece pertinent to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict as Pro-Israeli (PI), Pro-Palestinian (PP) or Neutral (N), Palestine Media Watch has found that The Charlotte Observer’s readers are being presented with a predominantly Pro-Israeli perspective.  Please see Appendix A.

 

Only fifteen percent (15%) of the Forum letters, editorials, columns and satirical cartoons in TCO’s  Op/Ed pages were pro-Palestinian.  Fifty-two (52%) were pro-Israeli.  The remaining thirty-three percent (33%) were classified as Neutral.

 

Of the 52% pro-Israel items, the columns were written by well-known, conservative columnists who use strident and extreme language.  The newspaper the week of Monday, April 29, 2002 to Sunday, May 5, 2002 contained three (3) columnists that wrote over-whelmingly pro-Israel, anti-Arab, anti-Islam columns.

 

4/20/02, 15A,  Walking back the Saudi cat by William Safire

5/3/02, 15A, Saudi Arabia’s no friend  by Cal Thomas

5/5/02 (Sunday), 3D, Jenin: A phantom massacre by Charles Krauthammer

 

No reciprocal columns from a Palestinian perspective were published that week, the following week or until the end of the observation period May 31, 2002.

 

 

VI.   Findings – News Coverage

 

Palestine Media Watch monitored and analyzed the news coverage offered by TCO  from April 1, 2002 to May 31, 2002. PMW noted spots of responsible reporting during this period.

 

  • Although outside the analysis period, the March 31, 2002 Perspective section contained information that helped inform the general public about the Palestinians, the Israelis, Jerusalem and important, historical dates.

 

  • Worlds Apart, May 5, 2002 page 1A, related both the Israeli and Palestinian views to the Charlotte audience.

 

  • Parents try to halt suicide bombings, May 25, 2002 page 1A, reported the anguish and worry of Palestinian parents concerned that their children are being radicalized by current events and others’ voices.  This kind of story is rarely seen or heard in the mainstream U.S. news.

 

  • PMW found that more and more, facts are being verified by independent and Palestinian sources and witnesses rather than relying on Israeli government, Israeli military, or Israeli sources solely.  PMW believes this should be a consistent practice, but is encouraged to find it happening increasingly.

 

  • PMW is encouraged to see independent sources, such as human rights organizations being referenced or quoted more.

 

4/23/02, 6A, Standoffs hold, violence goes on, James Bennet.

5/3/02, 7A, Study: Jenin not scene of massacre, T. Christian Miller.

5/31/02, 4A, Diplomats arriving for new peace effort, John Kifner.

 

  • Photos showing Palestinians in their daily life have been included, such as the photo printed May 17, 2002 page 8A showing villagers traversing a road block.

 

  • 5/26/02, 8A, Israelis kill girl, mother, Greg Myre.  The headline stated the death of civilians (including a child), included a picture of a grieving relative, and verified information by both Israeli military and Palestinian witnesses.

 

 

 

While PMW was encouraged to see these examples of responsible reporting, there remain areas of concern.  While PMW understands that an article, a photo, a caption, a paragraph cannot be pulled out, examined and conclusions reached on that one item, we feel that there is an over-all pattern that has emerged of partiality in the coverage of this conflict.  This report outlines these concerns with examples from TCO pages from April 1, 2002 to May 31, 2002.

9.      Politically loaded term “Disputed area” used instead “occupied territory”.

 

Immediately following the appropriation of land in the West Bank, Golan Heights, Gaza Strip and East Jerusalem, Israeli policy-makers denied that the lands fell under the Geneva Conventions and that Israel was bound by these statutes or other international laws concerning the region and that they had an inherent claim to the land.  The Labor-led government began the creation of a de facto situation on the ground for settlements (and therefore concrete claims). Moshe Dayan stated that settlements throughout the territories were necessary “…not because they can ensure security better than the army, but because without them we cannot keep the army in those territories.  Without them the IDF would be a foreign army ruling a foreign population.”

 

This policy of creating an illusion of “rights” to the land continued through the decades, no matter the ruling party.  As reported in the July 7, 1987 Jerusalem Post article, ‘Facts should be created in West Bank’, says Sharon, Ariel Sharon, then Industry and Trade Minister and Likud party member said, “ ‘We have to create facts in these areas as fast as possible and show signs of activity on the ground in order to dispel any doubt’ about the future of the territories.”

 

Dore Gold, current spokesman for Prime Minister Ariel Sharon issued a brief in January 2002 to the Jerusalem Center for Public Affairs addressing the issue of Israel’s claim to these territories and asserted, “…Israel possesses legal rights with respect to the West Bank and Gaza Strip…” again trying to create a dispute where international law is clear.  He concludes, “It would be far more accurate to describe the West Bank and Gaza Strip as ‘disputed territories’ to which both Israelis and Palestinians have claims.

 

PMW concludes that calling these lands “in dispute”, “disputed territories” or “disputed areas” is not a general description of the region, but has been co-opted by one side therefore making them politically loaded terms.  Their use by neutral media outlets, when used outside quotes, should be terminated.

VII.  Conclusions and Suggestions

 

 

 

The Charlotte Observer Op/Ed Pages

 

 

Balance presentation of views:  The Opinion pages of The Charlotte Observer between April 1, 2002 and May 31, 2002, strongly reflect the official pro-Israeli position on the Middle East crisis.  In what they mention, how they mention it and in what they fail to mention – they remain faithful to the official pro-Israeli position and present very little that is original on what the root causes of the crisis are, why the violence has persisted and what may be done to bring the conflict to an end.  This imbalance in presentation is a disservice to its readership and the community at large.  The over-whelming presentation of one side of an issue does not allow the paper’s readers to hear from each perspective equally, then make informed decisions.

 

All efforts should be made to guarantee a balance in presentation of views.  A list of quality columnists, who write regularly on the Middle East and take a “neutral” view or write from the Palestinian perspective are included in Appendix D to serve as a resource by The Charlotte Observer’s  editorial staff.

 

 

 

 

The Charlotte Observer’s news coverage

 

 

 

1)  Use only neutral terms:  The use of the terms “in retaliation”, “in response to” or “following” , etc… when referring to Israeli violence, the use of “radical” or “Jewish extremists” only when referring to Israelis or Jews; and the use of the terms “terror”, “terrorist”, “attack” when referring to Palestinians or Palestinian violence can easily be remedied by becoming aware of this practice and choosing only neutral and equal terms to describe both parties of the conflict.  Unless the words are used in a quote from an official source, the paper should refrain from using them in the body of the article.

 

2) Balance story presentation:  The publishing of articles solely from the Israeli perspective should stop.  When “human interest” stories solely from the Israeli perspective are printed, an accompanying human interest story should be printed solely from the Palestinian perspective either the same day or soon after.  If the story is meant to be a news story and not a human interest story, Palestinian sources should be cited including quotes and references.  Palestinian sources should not be consistently relegated to a secondary position in the article and Israeli statements should be verified by independent and/or Palestinian sources and witnesses.

 

3) Give Palestinian deaths, same coverage as Israeli deaths:  The death and wounding of Palestinians should stop being reported as statistics and include the same humanization as afforded Israeli deaths and injuries.  If there is room in an article to describe the circumstances, the condition of the bodies, personal backgrounds and setting when Israelis are killed, this same courtesy should be extended to Palestinian casualties.  We suggest that The Charlotte Observer  request that wire service reporters spend more time in the Occupied Territories and among the Palestinians so these details can be reported equally.

 

4) Provide background/context:  Not reporting the context of an event or pertinent information concerning a story can so mislead readers that it often becomes a falsification of facts through omission.  All efforts should be made to provide context for the reader.  Many times a word or short paragraph is all that is needed.  (e.g. The stories on the Gaza Strip does not provide the reader with any idea about life on the ground there.  The short, geographical description provided in the stories is inadequate.  Also, the repeated articles reporting Sharon’s mistrust of Arafat should include the Palestinian view of Sharon.)

 

5) Avoid politically-loaded, contentious words:  The more controversial practice of calling settlements “Jewish neighborhoods” or certain locations “disputed areas” can be resolved by not using these politically-loaded terms, simply because they are politically biased terms that remain under contention, offend a great portion of the paper’s readership and do not reflect internationally accepted practice and standards.
