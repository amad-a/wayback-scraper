THE IMPORTANCE OF LETTERS TO THE EDITOR IN SHAPING PUBLIC OPINION
From: http://www.cda-cdai.ca/media101/letterstoed.htm
Major Newspapers know a great deal about their readers, including how they read the paper. They know that many folks buy a paper for reasons that have nothing to do with news and discussion of events. They may be only interested in specialized sections like the adds; sports; even comics.
But the majority---and yes, they are still that---those who actually read the news, mostly do it like this. They look at the front page and then start turning the pages, glancing at the article leads until they hit the editorial and op-ed pages. Here is the first place most of those folks will stop scanning and start reading.

That means a successful letter to the editor in a major city newspaper can reach tens of thousands, even millions of people with your views---if they can make it on the page.

To being with, somebody has to take the time to write one. One of the saddest realities is that people in the defence community are less likely to communicate with the media than just about any interest group in the country!

In Operation Call the Media, we hope to mobilize people on a cross country basis to get "the grass roots" involved. But it is also important for members of the Commentator Corps to participate in letter writing, both to set an example, and to get the volume of input up.

WRITING SUCCESSFUL LETTERS TO THE EDITOR

Editors at major newspapers get several hundred letters a day via e-mail, fax and snail mail on every subject under the sun. When the small percentage of obscene and "kook" letters are dumped, they are roughly separated by subject, and separated again into pro and con. A count is kept, and relative to the paper's circulation size, it is multiplied by a "representative factor" that can vary from a hundred to thousands.

This process give the paper a "rolling poll" of what its readers find important, and what they think on these subjects. This reader input is always on the agenda of the daily editorial meeting.

 

Obviously, organized letter writing campaigns can slew this process, and that's why newspapers look for any trace of them in that input. When they are detected, everyone goes in the waste basket.

Last year the Toronto Star ran into such a campaign organized by Tom Wappel, the Liberal M.P. for Scarborough. A staunch anti-abortion campaigner, Wappel used a letter to his constituents to urge people to write in to the papers. His error was to give a suggested text of the letter, and hundreds of people sent it to the paper practically verbatim. They wasted their stamp.

For urging people to write and give their views is OK. Telling them what to write is not. Any successful campaign depends on getting individuals to express their views.

When it comes to actually printing letters, the paper is going to take a rough sample of the subject input. Obviously, out of 60 letters on subject W, only a few will make the page. Please note that the ones that didn't make it on to the page, still made the count.

The ones that do make it will be selected by two basic criteria---length and quality.

Here are two ideal letters that deal with comment:

    Re Susan Blanks column of August 24 on the Canadian military. She
Correctly identifies funding as the 
real problem facing our defence effort.
Her support for our servicemen and women 
in these troubled times is appreciated. 
John Smith, Oshawa, Ontario

Your August 24 editorial suggesting
further cuts to Canada's military so as to 
free up more money for social programs is based
on flawed premises. First you assume Canada 
cannot look after its own people and maintain
the ability to defend itself at the same time. 
Worse, you presume a world where our interests 
and values will never again be challenged by 
force. Don't you read the international news 
in your own paper?
[text, 72 words]
Mary Smith, Calgary, Alberta

The first example is to remind us that positive comment on things we like is as essential as criticism of things we don't. Its easier to get motivated by the negative. This means those who do write positive things in the papers don't get the praise they deserve. It is also very short, it fits the slot of the average printed letter after the editors have cut it down by two thirds or more. Save them the trouble. Make it short in the first place.

The second letter shows how to pitch criticism in a constructive way. It does not imply moral and ethical failure on the part of the publication as in, "...typical, left wing, peacenik blather." It deals only with issues raised as the writer sees them. It is also about the maximum length for a published letter.

Yes, longer letters do sometimes get published, especially on the weekend. But the fact is, unless you've hit some fascinating and unique perspective, or have an important and recognized name, it won't be one of yours.

Never forget that letters to the editor are for quick comment, not full bore debate and argument. Adapting this approach makes writing them a brisk and easy task that builds the quantitative output. It is better to do six short ones over a space of months than to indulge in one large dump.

Equally important is comment on news coverage and the fairness of same. Again, long diatribes don't work. I know of recent example where a retired Admiral sent a 1,500 word critique of their coverage to a west coast newspaper. Writing it may have made the person feel good, but not a word of it ever appeared in print. Small wonder. Even if the editor was amenable, cutting those 1,500 words down to 75 words is not possible.

If he and a half dozen of his friends had each sent in a fifty word letter saying "I think your coverage of defence issues is unfair--here is an example--hope you do better in the future," one might have been published. One might have been read by a quarter million people.

As a final point, always remember that in information warfare, especially in writing letters to the editor, a good media guerilla conforms to the terrain, adapts their tactics to how the media works.

Rapid Response, brevity, simplicity and quantity are the winning tactics.
