Mission & Goals

 

Your mission is to ensure fair, honest, and accurate media coverage in both news and editorial page departments.  The goals are:

 

* Bring about equity in the space given to various Israeli and Palestinian perspectives

* Push the editorial line to adopt a human rights stand consistent with

facts

* Push the news coverage to be more journalistically solid, based on facts and not propaganda and myths.

 

Your first achievable goal is to get noticed: to become someone they

recognize and respect.  Set up a meeting with the paper’s editorial board.

 

Know your target

 

Build a map of the editorial board: who is who, who is sympathetic, who is hostile, and who is in the neutral.  Try to enlist the help of the sympathetic ones, try to convince the neutral ones with strong arguments, and try to avoid angering the ones in the hostile camp.

 

Try to learn about how editorials are written.  There is always someone in the board who is the point person on any particular issue.  Find out who they are and build a strategy: e.g., take them out to lunch, give them reports and findings, and try to get into a debate over the substance.  The main thing is to force them to justify their paradigm.  If you get them to even look at the underlying framework of thinking ? in this case, the notion

that Israel is merely defending itself -- as something challengeable you have won half of the battle.

 

There are books and web pages that will show you who are the people in the positions in the media outlet you are interested in.  We subscribe to the Media Yellow book which gives updated information quarterly (with phone numbers and email contact information).  There are also some web pages that will allow you to do that.  An example is at http://capwiz.com/adc/home/

 

Most newspapers (unless very small) have two separate groups of editors: news editors and opinion page editors.  You will usually need to meet with those two groups separately.  Contact the managing editor to set an appointment with the News Editors.  Contact the editorial page editor to set-up an appointment with the editorial page staff.  In large newspapers thsi would include assistant and associate editors.  It would include Op-Ed and letter editors.

 

Setting up the appointment

 

If you decide to make a first contact by phone, that is fine but they will usually ask you for a letter stating the same things.  Here is how we approach speaking and writing to them to get attention:

 

1) We represent a group of organizations and activists for human rights with emphasis on the Middle East.  List those groups, many of us are members of several groups, list them all (Al-Awda/Palestine Right to Return Coalition, Palestine Media Watch, ADC, Amnesty etc).

 

2) We would like to set up a meeting with the editorial board to exchange information and discuss coverage in the media outlet in question.

 

The Meeting

 

Have a get together with the activists selected to go to the meeting.

Recommend no more than 6 activists and that brief presentations be given

from no more than 3 (maximum 4 of the activists).  Time will be limited and

this will not be a history lesson or a lengthy lecture.

 

One important function of the meetings is to let the editorial board know

that we are a serious enterprise and not a transient group of malcontents.

 

Here is how we successfully did this on Palestinian issues:

 

- As you get in the room and meet people, try to relax, mingle (ask about

the paper, the building, the weather whatever).

 

- When the meeting starts (usually the chief editor wills ignal this), the

lead person (person who made the appointment) usually starts as follows.

Thank them for meeting with us, state that we really appreciate the

opportunity to exchange information and present our perspective.  State that

what you think we should do is have a round of brief introductions and that

then we have very brief areas that some of our team members will address.

 

- Team member introductions should be brief: name, what you do in life for a

living, groups you volunteer with.

 

- "Chair" then asks the first presented for 3-4 minutes.  That segment would

show maps (Salman Abu Sittas's map with all the Palestinian villages

depopulated 1947-1949, maps of the present WB/Gaza areas in perspective, the settlement blocks, apartheid).  Brief and to teh point.

 

- Presentation on current conditions: apartheid, occupations, present

reports by international human rights groups (Amnesty, HRW, PHR, UNCHR etc)

 

- Presentation on the analysis of the paper's coverage (research should have

been completed ahead of time and hand outs prepared).

 

- These three core presentations can be accompanied with handout, visual

presentations, etc.

 

- Chair sums-up, asks for feed back and Q&A from the editors.  Be very

thoughtful in thsi section as thsi is where mistakes can be made.  You can

do role playing before the actual meeting and anticipate questions and

defensive postures from the paper's editors.  Do not be too

confrontational.  Speek of teh facts and the desire for better coverage.

The past cannot be changed, the future can.

 

- At the end of teh meeting, ask if you could meet with them again in a

month or two (you may also indicate taht you get lots of good speakers nad

intellectuals and it maybe worthwhile to have a meeting with some of those).

 

- Ask for the business cards of attendees before you leave.

 

General tips for the meeting

 

Make sure they understand that you are not there simply to vent off or to

let out your anger or attack the paper.  You are there to present your case

for better coverage.

 

The tone of your requests should be:

* All you want from them is to be fair

* All you are trying to help them be a better paper to their own readers

 

Whenever you go to a meeting with them, give them the press kit. The package will contain:

 

 - Human right group reports

 - UN findings

 - Talking points handout

 - A package on the media

 

In your meetings, stress the following:

 - The great discrepancy between the amount of space give to pro-Israeli columns vs pro-Palestinian rights columns

 - Absence from mention of human rights findings

 - Absence from mention of UN findings

 - Absence of maps depicting offers made by Israel

 - The myths about the wars (48/67/73/82)

 - The myth about Israel being an underdog

 

Put them on the spot over why the discrepancy is so high between the space given to the Israeli government version of events. Ask them point blank: why the discrepancy, and wait for an answer.  They will probably come back with the following: We do no know how to get hold of pieces on demand.  When they say that, tell them that PMWatch has syndicated dozens of well-known authors and will be able to provide you well-written pieces from respected authors on demand.

 

Schedule one meeting every three months. After your initial meeting with the editorial board, you can schedule informal meetings with individual key people.

 

Make sure you send them a thank you card - preferably both via email and snail mail.  A thank you phone call is also good.

 

When possible, schedule meetings AFTER lunch.  They will try to schedule them at 11:00 am so that at 12:00 noon they will end it for lunch.

 

The Reports

 

The reports are your bread and butter: they are the evidence that gives substance to your arguments and your points.  If you accuse a paper that they never mention Human Rights findings, the other side will react by saying that no, they do.  Unless you tell them that you have examined their work closely and you know for a FACT that they don’t, they will try to contradict you, even when they have no idea what they are talking about!  So, maintain those reports and keep them updated.  You will be glad you did when you are facing the editorial board.

 

A painless way of doing this is to constantly update as opposed to piling up.  What you can do is have a hard-copy of the report around and write by hand on it any time a new column or editorial is added.  When an editorial is added, read it, highlight the important points and enter your observations into the report.  This should take you 10 minutes a shot.  You will be very thankful you did that when time comes for the report to be ready.

 

Letters

 

Make sure you keep writing your letters.  This will not only keep your name visible, but will give you a great archive to go back to for issues to raise during meetings.  Nothing impresses them more than seeing how you are in total command of your criticism when you tick off specific points in specific stories.  It will show them that you are totally on top of this issue - and that does intimidate them.

 

General advice

 

Preparation is the keyword.  Having a detailed report, and coming to meetings with specific demands, will impress them.

 

When you present an argument, make sure you simplify it and present only the strongest points.  A well-known technique of shooting down arguments (unfairly) is to latch on to the weakest part of it, attack that part (even if it is incidental to the argument) and then claim victory when you step back on that point.  So, when you are presenting a set of facts, drop anything that you are not 100% sure of.

 

Avoid embarrassing them too much when you have something embarrassing about what they have done or said.  Put them on the spot once in a while, but don't use all of your ammunition.  They will be grateful that you held back.

 

Avoid using the term "Jews" or "Zionist".  Use "Israelis" or "Israeli occupation".

 

View your task as a long term one.  They need to understand that you will always be here.  This will force them to develop a long term strategy of managing you as opposed to them trying to shoo you off - either by giving you something and expecting you thank them and go away or by intimidating you by rebuking you strongly.

 

Always be patient and polite.

 

They need to understand that you are a representative group.

 

Be persistent but don't be a pest in follow-up with them.  One mail every

two days should be the average.  But make sure you do not go too many days without having your name pop up in their mailbox.  One easy way of doing this is submitting pieces on behalf of people for publication in the paper.

You can also forward them relevant news items once in a while.

 

Be responsive and useful: column editors have to deal with hundreds of columns a month, with dozens and dozens of writers.  You don't want to be a mere voice in the crowd.  You want to be a resource for them that makes life easier and not tougher for them.  When they ask for information, help finding a piece, try to be as responsive as possible.
