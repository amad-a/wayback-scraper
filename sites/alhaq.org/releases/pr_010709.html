Al-Haq Condemns Israels Demolition of 14 Homes in Shuafat Refugee Camp

On 9 July 2001, the Jerusalem Municipality destroyed 14 buildings, containing 25 apartments, which were under construction in Shuafat refugee camp, Jerusalem. The buildings were intended to house approximately 122 people, and ranged in size from 120m sq to 700m sq. The total value of each of the buildings ranged from 30,000 NIS to 250,000 NIS. Demolition notices were delivered on Sunday, and on Monday morning 13 bulldozers accompanied by Israeli security personnel entered the camp from the direction of the nearby settlement of Pisgat Zeev and began to destroy the condemned structures. During the operation, which lasted for approximately seven hours, the camp was closed. A number of protestors were reportedly beaten by police as they sought to prevent the demolition of the properties. 

Wael Bahajat Ali gave the following Affidavit to al-Haq:

On Sunday July 8, 2001 officials from the municipality came to my house with members of the security forces and stuck a demolition notice on my home. Our house was in the last stages of construction. The next day a large contingent of border police came to the camp and 20 persons protested by my building in an attempt to stop the demolition. We were beaten by the police and forced out of the way while the house was destroyed. My family consists of 35 members and we currently live in a very small house that was provided to us by UNWRA. The Municipality doesnt provide us with any services. 

The Shuafat refugee camp is located on 35 dunums of land and has a population of approximately 30,000 people. The number of people living in the camp has increased in recent years, particularly after the Israeli Interior Ministry began to annul the residency rights of Palestinian Jerusalemites who were residing outside of the city, or had resided outside of the city for an extended period. The policy led many Palestinian Jerusalemites to returning to the city in an attempt to secure their status. The policy ID card confiscation was formally rescinded in March 2000. 

When they need to build most of the camps residents do not apply for building permits, as the Municipality hardly ever grants them permission to build. The Jerusalem municipality destroyed the structures in the camp on the grounds that they were built illegally. Illegal construction is common in East Jerusalem for a number of reasons. The first reason is that the Municipality rarely grants building permits to the Arab residents of the city. On average only 150 building permits are granted a year to Palestinian residents. In light of the increasing population growth in the city, the number of licenses granted falls way below the needs of the Palestinian population. The Municipality has also curtailed the development of the Palestinian neighbourhoods through urban planning policies. Large areas of Palestinian neighbourhoods are zoned green were construction is strictly prohibited. The Israeli authorities very rarely construct public housing for East Jerusalems residents, instead, construction is almost always a private endeavour in contrast with the elaborate financial incentives which are granted to settlers in occupied east Jerusalem. The allowed density level per dunum is lower in Palestinian neighbourhoods and the allowed construction height is lower in comparison to Jewish settlements in East Jerusalem and housing in the western parts of the city. If a building permit is granted to a Palestinian, it is usually for construction in already built up areas and not in open spaces. As a result of these planning restrictions, housing shortages are acute in East Jerusalem, which forces many residents to build without permission from the municipality. It has been estimated that the housing shortage in East Jerusalem approaches 25,000 housing units.

Municipal Planning policies are guided by a demographic policy with the aim of reducing the number of Palestinians residing in occupied East Jerusalem, and increasing the number of Jews in order to better secure the illegal annexation of East Jerusalem that took place in June 1967. The inter-ministerial committee to examine the rate of development in Jerusalem formulated this policy in 1973. It formally set the figures of 73.5 Jews to 26.5 Palestinian as a ratio that must be either maintained or changed in favour of the Jewish population, even if natural population growth should have caused it to change differently. This was the population ratio for the city in its entirety in 1972. The Jewish population was to be increased, while the Palestinian population was not intended to grow and where possible, was to be reduced. It is this demographic imperative that guides Israels policy in Occupied East Jerusalem, and accounts for why so many Palestinians are forced to build without permission from the municipality. Despite this policy, in recent years the Jewish ratio in the city has decreased while the Palestinian population makes up over 30 percent of the entire citys population. 

Whenever the term illegal construction is used to describe structures erected in East Jerusalem without municipal permission, this background must be kept in mind. It should also be acknowledged that East Jerusalem is occupied, that the Fourth Geneva Convention Relative to Civilian persons in time of War is applicable, and that Israel is under an obligation to ensure as the occupying power, that the population enjoy the right to adequate housing.
