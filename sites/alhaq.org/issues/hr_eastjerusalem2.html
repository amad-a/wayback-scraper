EAST JERUSALEM RESIDENCY RIGHTS

On 22 April 1999, the Israeli Supreme Court heard the case of Palestinians from Occupied East Jerusalem petitioning against Israel's revocation of their residency status. Their attorneys sought to annul the cancellation of residency status because the policy was carried out illegally, in terms of both the basis of the policy and the procedures used by the Interior Ministry. Al-Haq stresses that, in seeking redress through the Israeli justice system, Palestinians do not accept the applicability of Israeli law to the Occupied Palestinian Territories (OPTs), which includes East Jerusalem.

Since December 1995, the Ministry's policy has diverted radically from that applied previously. The revocation of residency was a result of retroactive application of this new approach, rather than an enforcement of old regulations. The difference is crucial, since retroactivity is only permissible in extraordinary circumstances, where there is a clear public interest proportional to the severity of the effect on the individual.

The history of residency conditions prior to 1995 shows that there existed a publicly acknowledged norm to which residents of East Jerusalem conformed in good faith. Under the 'Open Bridges' formula for movement between Israel, the OPTs and abroad, residency rights were not forfeited provided the resident adhered to the regulations of exit permit. That the policy was publicly accepted as the official one is given credibility by the fact that these conditions were put in writing on the back of the exit permit.

From December 1995, the Ministry's approach took a radically divergent course: statistical figures show a sharp and consistent upsurge in the number of residents whose status has been revoked since then. The State Ombudsman's report (1996) included a special chapter stating that the seven years maximum stay outside Israel was to be calculated cumulatively rather than continuously; - i.e., 'short visits' to Jerusalem were to be disregarded. Furthermore, residency in the OPTs was to be considered a stay 'outside of Israel' for the purpose of expiration of permits. These new parameters represent a fundamental reinterpretation of the previous status quo.

Permanent residency status was accorded to East Jerusalemites under the Law of Entry to Israel (1952). This was held to reflect the reality of life. However, the blanket application of the law ignores the clear distinction between immigrants, who requested to live in Israel of their own free will, and residents of Jerusalem, whose residency was imposed on them and whose birthplace and roots are in Jerusalem not having immigrated to Israel from any place. To define them as alien guests in Israel is a gross misrepresentation of the reality of their lives.

Given that Israel applies Israeli law over East Jerusalem, the Interior Ministry should accord Arabs the same rights and freedoms as Jewish residents. Instead the Ministry is pursuing a policy that is discriminatory. Revoking Arab residency rights should be seen in the wider context of the Israeli attempt to consolidate their 1967 occupation by creating demographic 'facts on the ground'. Interior Minister Suissa's words reinforce this: "we must increase the Jewish majority in Jerusalem to more than 80%." Thus, even if the new policy were only applied in the future it would still constitute an illegal and highly discriminatory development.

Female Jerusalemites have faced double discrimination. Until 1994, unlike their male counterparts, they were denied permission for family unification in Jerusalem if married to a non-Jerusalemite spouse and were thus obliged to make their homes outside the city's boundaries. Now they are deprived of their residency status as a result of the new policy, which punishes them retroactively for their time spent abroad.

Not only is the basis for the policy thus flawed further reference to the 1952 Law shows that the Ministry also flouted due process. While it is true that the Interior Minister may cancel a permit he may only do so under certain provisions, namely: with judicial oversight of administrative action; with reasons for the measure stated; and with the opportunity to refute the basis of the revocation.

The circumstances surrounding the implementation of this new policy disregard these requirements: it was effective both immediately and retroactively; and the Ministry issued only short statements in Hebrew with no advance hearing, nor criteria for determining the 'center of life', nor proof to support the confiscations. In doing so the Ministry ignored the right to a hearing and shifted the burden of proof such that the petitioners had to prove positively that the center of their lives was still in Jerusalem.

Since the start of the petitioning proceedings the Ministry has rescinded its revocation of the residency of seven of the petitioners - a small gesture in comparison to the 788 residency permits confiscated in 1998. However, the Supreme Court hearing of 22nd April returned the burden of proof to the Interior Ministry. The panel of five Justices ordered the State to submit factual evidence supporting the claim that their policy had not changed from 1995, postponing the hearing to a date to be set after August.

Ethnic Cleansing?

Following discussions during our work on this campaign, Al-Haq has decided to evaluate the suitability of the term 'ethnic cleansing' as a description, in the context of human rights, for the status of the Occupied Palestinian Territories. Given that the motivation for this study came from the situation in Jerusalem, we have made particular reference to this issue in the course of our research.

We recognise that given the predominantly journalistic and/or political usage of the term it lacks the precise definition that would be expected of legal terminology. Al-Haq seeks to remind the human rights community and the public at large that it currently lacks such parameters. If the notion of 'ethnic cleansing' is to hold any meaning and carry real weight in the debate over residency rights in East Jerusalem and elsewhere, then it is imperative that it first be subject to due scrutiny and precise usage. Our aim, therefore, is qualification rather than disqualification of the term.

Our research stems from the belief that the phrase has been applied perhaps indiscriminately and injudiciously to the Palestinian case; and furthermore that adding the word 'administrative' to the phrase as a qualifier neither constitutes sufficient consideration of the term's applicability nor substitutes the need for such a discussion.

Consequently, al-Haq has examined 'ethnic cleansing' from several angles: firstly, looking at the use and occurrence of the term outside the Palestinian context, especially in former Yugoslavia; secondly, reviewing the actual situation as regards the occupation of East Jerusalem; and thirdly, considering alternative terminology used to describe the policies practised by the State of Israel.

Opinion as to the applicability of the term among the sources consulted is divided principally on differences in its definition. For those who see 'ethnic cleansing' as descriptive of methods employed, the term seems inappropriate given the lack of a wartime context, in which crimes such as rape and slaughter have occurred unchecked in Former Yugoslavia. However, others, focussing on the intentions associated with the term and considering the broader, historical context of Israel's current policy, find it applicable to the situation in Jerusalem. This dichotomy between aims and methodology is fundamental to the discussion and also indicative of the dangers of employing a term that lacks official definition.

In conducting such a study we hope to provoke more judicious consideration of the situation in Jerusalem and Palestine as a whole. Moreover, we hope to widen awareness amongst Palestinians of the plight of other communities worldwide.
