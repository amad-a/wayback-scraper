Al-Haqs Relationship with the International Commission of Jurists - Geneva
 

Al-Haq, Law in the Service of Man, the first Palestinian human rights institution, began its days in April 1979 when a group of West Bank Palestinians established it to uphold the principles of the Rule of Law and to defend Palestinians human rights in the Occupied Palestinian Territories.  It became officially registered in 1980.  Shortly thereafter, it became the West Bank affiliate of the International Commission of Jurists in Geneva.   For a period of at least 10 years, Al-Haq was the sole affiliate of the ICJ-Geneva in the Occupied Territories.  This affiliation was particularly important during the upheaval of the intifada.  ICJ-Geneva stood steadfastly behind Al-Haq offering it protection as a human rights institution in the Occupied Territories during which time staff members were occasionally harassed or even arrested by the Israeli authorities.  This relationship fostered over a twenty-year period has strengthened the work of Al-Haq at the local level and has had considerable effect upon its work.  At the same time, this affiliation has assisted in spreading information related to the defense of human rights in the Occupied Territories internationally and raising awareness at the level of the ICJ regarding the rule of law.  This has been particularly important in helping the ICJ identify its priorities in the OPT.
 

In the more recent years, Adama Dieng, Secretary-General of the ICJ, has contributed significantly to strengthening Al-Haq as a human rights institution.
 

The relationship between Al-Haq and the ICJ is a very active one. The ICJ has co-ordinated with Al-Haq several missions that examined the legal structure of the Occupied Territories and the judicial system. The outcomes and findings of these missions were very essential for Al-Haqs work, and are referred to by many local and international organisations. Also, the ICJ responded positively to Al-Haqs repeated requests to arrange for jurists with outstanding experience to observe trials of special significance to Palestinians in Israeli courts. The ICJ has also intervened with the Israeli authorities on many essential issues, on the basis of information from and at the request of Al-Haq.
 

The ICJ has also provided an essential facilitating role for Al-Haq in the UN system. Through the ICJ, Al-Haq could access the UN Commission on Human Rights, in addition to many of the other specialised bodies of the UN. The activities of Al-Haq in the UN have been in total co-ordination with the ICJ, which helped in many cases to advance mutual agendas. The Vienna World Conference on Human Rights and the appointment of a Special Rapporteur on the Occupied Palestinian Territories are relevant examples.
 

Since the establishment of the PNA, Al-Haq has closely been involved in supporting the development of an independent Palestinian Judiciary.   In relation to this, Al-Haq in 1993 in cooperation with the ICJ held a seminar entitled Towards an Independent Palestinian Judiciary.  This took place at the end of a two-week fact-finding mission conducted by the ICJ and its Center for the Independence of Judges and Lawyers concerning the situation of civil courts in the Occupied Territories.  The purpose of the mission was to pinpoint the problems and obstacles facing the judicial system as a whole, in light of the accepted principles of the rule of law.  Following this mission the findings of it were set out in a book entitled The Civilian Judicial System in the West Bank and Gaza: Present and Future, as well as recommendations following the seminar. These recommendations specifically outlined suggestions on the requirements of legal and judicial functions that would help lead towards an independent Palestinian judiciary.
 

A second mission, similar to the one carried out in 1993, was carried out by the ICJ and its Center for the Independence of Judges and Lawyers from January 15-25, 2000.  This mission specifically was aimed at reviewing the independence of the Palestinian Judiciary and the legal profession in Palestine and the role of the Palestinian authorities.  It also examined the role of the international financial institutions with regards to questions related to the functioning of the judiciary and Israeli infringements of the administration of justice in the Occupied Territories.  The recommendations that resulted from this mission built upon the initial recommendations that the ICJ made during its first mission in 1993.  The role of the mission was also to examine whether the previous recommendations were carried out in the six-year period interim.   In addition, during this mission, Al-Haq in conjunction with the two other Palestinian human rights institutions affiliated with the ICJ, the Palestinian Center for Human Rights and LAW, held two workshops in the West Bank and the Gaza Strip regarding The Role of the Judiciary in Civil Society.
 

It is with great pleasure that we begin a new period in Al-Haqs history, building on our past relationships and entering into new ones.  In January 2000, the United Nations Committee on Non-Governmental Organizations recommended Al-Haq for Special Consultative Status with the United Nations Economic and Social Council.  This is truly an achievement for Al-Haq as a human rights institution not only in Palestine but also in the Arab world.  We take this responsibility seriously in continuing to persevere in our ultimate aim: the strengthening of the Rule of Law and the defense of Palestinians human rights as an important aspect of reinforcing international recognition of the rights of all peoples to self-determination.

The ability to submit written reports, oral statements and, on request, special studies and investigations directly to the relevant bodies of ECOSOC, will be a great help for Al-Haqs efforts to draw international attention to and generate international pressure against Israeli violations of Palestinian human rights in such vital issues as the denial of family reunification, house demolition and many more, as well as on matters concerning social justice and development, such as education and womens rights.

 
Interview with Raja Shehadeh, founding member of Al-Haq

 

Mr. Shehadeh, you have been one of the founders of Al-Haq, which achievements did you strive for by establishing such an organization?
 

Before the establishment of Al-Haq there was no local organization carrying out human rights work.  Al- Haq was the first organization to do this kind of work.
 

We also strove to create a committed organization that was politically independent. This too was a first since many of the active non-governmental organizations working in the occupied territories were politically affiliated and serving one or the other of the political organizations.
 

We also had the conscious objective of creating a truly democratic organization. One that was fully owned and run by its staff.
 

The spirit of voluntarism was also very important. The founding members and several of the staff volunteered their services free of charge. They strove for no other benefits other than the satisfaction to see the success of their work. There was a unique and profound commitment on the part of those involved in the creation and development of Al-Haq.
 

This is as far as the type of organization which the founding members tried to establish. As to the kind of work done by the organization, the organization tried from the beginning to fill the enormous gap in the absence of documentation of human rights violations committed by the Israeli occupation. The advocacy on behalf of the victims of human rights violations was always a primary objective as was the promotion of the rule of law. The organization also established the first public legal and human rights library in the West Bank.
 

Do you think Al-Haq had an influence on other organizations of that kind in the region?
 

The fact, that such an outspoken and active organization as Al-Haq was possible in the occupied territories had, I believe, a strong influence on the neighboring countries. They looked up to Al-Haq both because of the fact that it was able to operate at all. But they also studied carefully how it was organized. Some of the innovative ideas generated by A- Haq extended also to the legal approach it was taking to various matters it dealt with. The enforcement project, for example, produced important literature which had influence within and outside the region.
 

It is important to remember that prior to the creation of Al-Haq the Israeli Position on the nature and conduct of the occupation was the only one heard in the world. Al-Haq was able through the approach and kind of work it did to gain credibility and become an alternative voice which eventually made a complete shift in the perception of the world about the nature of the Israeli occupation.
 

Al-Haq always tried to enforce the applicability of the Fourth Geneva Convention concerning the West Bank and Gaza. Do you think it succeeded in that issue?
 

The position that Al-Haq took from the beginning was that the Fourth Geneva Convention is applicable and it used it along with the Hague Regulation as standards against which to assess the human rights situation. In this the organization had made many allies. Only a political process could have succeeded in getting Israel to concede that the Fourth Geneva Convention Applied. This was beyond the powers of the organization. But the fact that Israel refused this did not deter the organization from insisting on its principle position and forging ahead with its work.
 

Which impact did the beginning of the peace process have on the work of Al-Haq?
 

It is my belief that the organization missed an important opportunity, which the beginning of the peace process provided for the following reasons:
 

First there was the best opportunity to use the strong interest generated by the political developments to promote the positions Al-Haq had been advocating for years, and, second, because the organization should have used its good standing and substantial network of friends and allies in the international human rights movement to have more of an impact on the course and outcome of the negotiations.
 

Rather than attempt to study the new situation and see what new opportunities it provided, the organization was paralyzed by the political considerations of what political position it should take towards the negotiations. This was a very grave failure which went against the ethos and tradition of the organization.
 

As a result only lip service was paid to human rights and the accords that came out of the negotiations did not have a human rights component to them.
 

How do you see the present role of a human rights organization such as Al-Haq?
 

The present situation differs in many fundamental ways from the past. I have been an outsider to the human rights work since 1991 when I left Al-Haq. From my previous involvement I realize that only an insider is privy to all the factors and conditions that must be taken into consideration in developing a strategy for human rights work. It is difficult and perhaps not prudent for me as an outsider to pontificate on these matters. What I can say is that the important feature of the present situation is its transitional nature. Perhaps this should influence to a large degree the kind of work that the organization does. Transitional situations can provide an opportunity for establishing and fortifying traditions as well as institutions which may become much more difficult to achieve once the situation settles into a permanent political structure.
 

Do you think peoples attitudes towards human rights have changed within the last twenty years?
 

The best evidence that there has been a change in peoples attitude is the fact that when Al-Haq began no one could understand what human rights work meant. There was a lot of suspicion surrounding the work and its motivation. Challenge and suspicion remain. There will always be those who reject human rights work, but they are now in the minority.

At the same time the human rights education must be an ongoing activity. Much of Todays perceptions have also to do with how the past is viewed. One of the important developments which have occurred, I believe, is that there is an understanding now that human rights work was not only a feature of the occupation and should end with the end of occupation.

How does the fact that the PA came into power influence Al-Haqs work?
 

The emphasis in Al-Haqs work has always been the legal aspects. The PA tends to neglect the legal aspects. There is a lot of work for Al-Haq to do in developing the rule of law and acting as a watchdog to ensure that the legal developments are consistent with the human right principles. There is much work for the organization to do and much ingenuity is needed to determine where the line should be drawn between human rights work and political work.
 

At the beginning you mentioned that Al-Haq was also established as a new-idea organization quoting participation of its members and its democratic structure. Should the way the organization is structured change after this transitional period? Could a membership model comply with our needs?
 

The initial structure the organization took was related to what it aimed to achieve. There was no recognition of human rights as a profession. The organization wanted to develop human rights activists rather than employees in a human rights organization. This objective has now been achieved. The structure of the organization should develop in line with the organizations objectives.
 

Whether the organization should become a membership organization is related to whether it wants to become a pressure group which will have impact as a movement that draws strength from its membership. But if Al-Haq wants to continue to be an organization that provides very specialized work, it may not need to change into a membership organization. It should also be borne in mind that a membership organization is more susceptible to political pressure.
 
 

Lots of our work has been done in the field of woman's rights, especially through our publications. We still have not installed a woman's rights unit but we are helping other organization out in that field. So do you think that Al-Haq has contributed to the issue of woman's rights?
 

In the past there was a strong womens right movement. Al-Haqs role was to provide it with legal advice and promote the legal aspect of the work of the movement. I believe that much has been achieved in this regard. The legal considerations are fully incorporated in the activities of the womens movements. Many specialized womens rights organizations have been established which are very effective.

Under the prevailing conditions Al-Haq has to find its own niche providing services that others cannot provide due to the special nature and connections of the organization. Al-Haq should continue to work in the field of womens rights in pursuit of its general aim which is the enforcement of the rule of law.

Al-Haq used international podiums, UN-mechanisms, international campaigns and conferences as a way of promoting and defending human rights. Do you think this is a role that Al-Haq should continue to have under the PA?
 

Al-Haq must have strategy of how to help Palestine move ahead in the future as far as human rights are concerned. In future it could help Palestine as a state join the various human rights convention and afterwards help ensure that they are implemented.
 

Retaining the connection with the international bodies remains an important function for Al-Haq. You are part of the world. In former times we used these fora and institutions to advocate human rights positions. Now you have to make the necessary adjustments and continue to interact with the Human rights movement worldwide for the service of the organizations present objectives.

Torture for Security
The systematic torture and ill treatment of Palestinians by Israel
Melissa Phillips, 1995
Since its inception in 1979, Al-Haq has been concerned with the problem of torture in Israeli interrogation centers. This study represents the culmination of Al-Haqs efforts over many years to achieve a cessation of the practice, and to alert the international community to the prevalence of this systematic, widespread and gross violation of Palestinians human rights.

 

Hopes that the practice of torture would end with the peace process were frustrated. The use of torture continued, in spite of the fact, that Israel is a Party signatory to the Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT) since 1991. As recently as September 1999 and only after a long and determined campaign by human rights organizations, including Al-Haq, the Israeli High Court of Justice finally decided, that torture is indeed illegal in Israel. However, the High court decision did not rule out the possibility to legitimize the use of torture through new legislation. Thus, at the moment, four bill have been introduced to the Knesset  two with the aim to pass a law that would officially allow torture for so called security reasons, while the other two promote the implementation of a total ban on torture in Israeli legislation. The issue has yet to be decided by the Knesset. Meanwhile, the practice of torture against Palestinian detainees in Israeli interrogation centers was not brought to an end by the High Court decision, but new excuses were found for it: for example hand cuffing in painful positions is now explained to be necessary to protect interrogators from presumably aggressive and dangerous detainees.
 

In addition, the occurrence of torture against Palestinians in custody of the Palestinian Security Apparatus gives further need to Al-Haq and other human rights organizations to continue working on the subject of torture.

STOP SEPARATING PALESTINIAN FAMILIES!

An International Campaign on the Question of Family Reunification
(Based on Newsletter No 23, 1991)
THE PROBLEM.  The roots of the problem of Palestinian dispersion go back to the war of 1948, when more than one half of the population of Palestine lost their homeland and their right to live there.  This was made worse after the June war of 1967, when the remainder of Palestine was occupied and tens of thousands more were made homeless, some for the second time.

 

In the areas occupied since 1967, the Israeli authorities proceeded to implement a series of policies and measures, which resulted in the loss of the right of residency for Palestinians of the Occupied Territories.  From the Israeli authorities' point of view permanent residence and consequently family reunification is not an inalienable right for the Palestinians.  Rather, permanent residence is considered a privilege granted in accordance with certain criteria set by the authorities themselves.  This policy is detrimental to the right of a family to permanent residence and reunification and creates serious humanitarian and economic problems for tens of thousands of Palestinian families.  These problems continue unabated.
 

THE CAMPAIGN.  Given the humanitarian nature of the issue of family reunification, and the large number of people it affects, Al-Haq decided in 1990 to initiate an international campaign by mobilizing public opinion through the media, human rights and other non-governmental institutions. The public pressure exerted will hopefully succeed in effecting a change in Israeli policy towards family reunification. Further, it is Al-Haq's view that by publicizing widely the issue of Palestinian family reunification, public attention will be drawn to one of the most important aspects of the Palestinian problem: the dispersion and dispossession of the Palestinian people from their lands and homes.
 

The campaign, being the first of its kind to be adopted by Al-Haq, also represents a new, more activist approach in the organization's work. Lessons learned from this campaign will undoubtedly affect Al-Haq's approach to human rights activism, and will possibly open the way to other campaigns on important human rights issues in the future.

Campaign to End ID Card Confiscation

This campaign was initiated by Al-Haq and other Palestinian human rights groups in March 1999 to support their Israeli partners as they challenged the legality of Israeli ID card confiscation in the Israeli Supreme Court.  The campaign quickly developed monumentum and by August its membership grew to include 23 Palestinian and Israeli human rights organizations and a number of international groups and supporters.
 

The background for this campaign is Israel's aggressive policy of reducing the Palestinian population and heritage in Jerusalem, thus changing facts on the ground in order to consolidate Israeli control over Jerusalem in sight of the final status talks.  Israel forced the status of "residency" on the Palestinian population in Jerusalem after the war in 1967 and the illegal annexation of East Jerusalem.  A status that transformed Palestinians into strangers in their hometown and made their residency rights dependent on Israeli good will. In December 1995 Israel introduced the "Center of Life" policy, forcing Palestinians to prove that they reside and work or attend school in Jerusalem in order to have their ID cards renewed. This enabled the increase of ID card confiscation by 600%, affecting an estimated number of more then 8000 individuals between 1996-98.
 

Though the Campaign resulted in the Supreme Court ruling that each case of ID cart confiscation has to be discussed separately, the situation on the ground did not change so far and thousands of Palestinian Jerusalemites are still threatened with having their ID cards confiscated and consequently to lose their right to live in their home town. Thus the efforts of the campaign to put an end to the Israeli policy of ID card confiscation continue.

Al-Haq: The First Twenty Years

By Joost Hiltermann, Human Rights Watch
 

It was on a crisp November day in 1984 that I first stepped into the small apartment on Ramallahs main street that housed the offices of what was then known as Law in the Service of Man (a somewhat limping translation of the more universal Al-Qanoun min Ajal al-Insaan: Law in the Service of the? Human Being?).  The receptionist (who doubled as administrative assistant) sat in an entrance space immediately off a small glassed-in veranda.  The dining room served as meeting room-cum-library.  Two small bedrooms offered working space for researchers.  And the tiny bathroom accommodated copies of the organizations few but accumulating publications, stacked neatly on thin metal shelves that I, in an unthinking moment, once managed to collapse on top of me.  (There must have been a small kitchen as well, but I do not remember it now).
 

A handful of staff resided there in the fall of 1984.  The organizations founders, gainfully employed elsewhere, would stop by for meetings.  The three field investigators visited periodically to hand over and discuss their latest yield.  There were, if memory serves me, at most two or three computers.
 

I came to Al-Haq just at the time when it was primed for a major expansion, having got its books in order and, thanks to a handful of pioneering reports and actions, starting to receive the attention and, increasingly, accolades of key western institutions.  And it had the luck to arrive on the scene at what was from a funding perspective a particularly propitious time, the happy coincidence of the rapid growth of an international human rights movement and the equally stunning emergence of an indigenous institutional infrastructure in the Occupied Territories.  By the time the popular uprising broke out in late 1987, Al-Haq was able to deploy the strengths of one in the service of the other, and vice versa, becoming in the process an influential Palestinian voice at the height of a major human rights emergency.
 

The founders of LSM/Al-Haq, Raja Shehadeh, Jonathan Kuttab and Charles Shammas

started their professional careers in the West Bank just as a new generation of activists had begun to graduate from high schools and colleges.  This was the post-1967 generation, born perhaps before the June War but who had their formative period in the ferment that followed the 1973 War and Yasir Arafats landmark speech at the United Nations in 1974.  The external solution - a dream, really, of Arab states sweeping in to liberate the Palestinian masses - had been laid to rest, and a new national movement began to take form whose weight lay essentially inside the territory of historic Palestine.  At a grassroots level this new movement found its expression first in voluntary work committees that fanned out across villages and refugee camps to perform services that the occupying power would not.  Politically, it took the shape of student activism, including demonstrations and strikes, and the emergence of a local leadership
which was promptly subjected to threats, detention and deportation by the military government.  In the late 1970s, under the Likud partys interpretation of the autonomy plan agreed to in the Camp David Accords, the new civilian government in the Occupied Territories proceeded to expropriate vast new tracts of land and to weave a web of military orders that made a mockery of whatever law had existed in 1967.  This permitted the occupier to insinuate itself into all aspects of Palestinian society with the aim of subverting, dividing and, ultimately, pacifying it: an autonomy entirely on Israels terms.
The new laws and new violations screamed out for a new approach.  A fresh crop of recruits from schools and universities provided the intellectual core and urgent energy of an array of initiatives launched in the late 1970s and early 1980s.  The womens movement emerged in the West Bank and Gaza at this time; the trade unions became infused with new life; the student movement expanded; and a grassroots primary health movement was spawned from the ruins of the civilian governments health system.

 

Taking a nod from the international human rights movement that took off in earnest only after the 1975 Helsinki Accords, Al-Haqs founders were keenly attuned to the need for devising new strategies to contest the ever-growing reach of a predatory occupying power and its highly mobile citizenry.  The institution they established alongside other institutions that began to come into their own in this period: the universities, the Arab Studies Society, the Arab Thought Forum, and others, as well as aforementioned grassroots movements became by its very objectives a part of what sociologist Salim Tamari has referred to as a strategy of informal resistance to the military occupation.  Planted squarely in the society that had given rise to it, Al-Haq offered a glimpse to its constituency of the alternative path that might be taken to divest itself of the occupation.  It began to provide an ever-growing audience with new linguistic tools and a concrete methodology to challenge specific actions by the governing authority.  These were presented in the form of the Know Your Rights series, one of Al-Haqs most important undertakings during its first decade.  These booklets inculcated in those who read them the crucial notion that not only did they - Palestinians, victims, fighters, people - have fundamental rights, but they had a right to assert these rights, and the booklets showed them how they might begin to do so.
 

Even as Al-Haq was playing an educational role toward its home constituency, it reached out to the outside world both for a receptive audience and for protection, as the military government could never be trusted not to kick in the doors one day and shut the place down.  This, too, required a great educational effort, as a deep-seated prejudice against Palestinians dissuaded many activists in the United States and elsewhere from turning their attention to the Middle East and rendered Al-Haqs objectives and methodology immediately suspect.  If the organization succeeded in making its case abroad, this was due less to its advocacy strategy, which for a long time was barely a strategy at all, than to its professionalism in documenting and reporting what even the most skeptical observer would have to admit were unconscionable violations of human rights.  And the Israelis offered a helping hand, of course, by engaging in public acts of collective punishment, like house demolitions.
 

The Palestinian institutions and grassroots organizations that proliferated in the 1980s were served by their semi-legal status (even if the military government denied them a license to operate formally, by applying for a license they had signaled their intent to function openly and carry out legal activities), the material and political support they received from governments and institutions abroad, and the fact that Israel had an inwardly democratic and open society that was broadly permissive of outspoken criticism of the outrages committed across the Green Line.  But it was especially because of Israels outward democratic posture that the military government found it very difficult to crack down on institutions like Al-Haq, assuming for a moment that such institutions presented any sort of real threat to the occupiers hold over the territory and its population.  The Israeli state had a constant task to mollify its supporters in the U.S. and Europe, who saw little wrong with plucky groups like Al-Haq as long as such groups stayed away from advocating violence and terrorism.  The genius of the method fashioned by Al-Haqs founders was that Al-Haq took Israel at its word (of being a democracy, as well as a self-declared reluctant, tolerant and benign occupier) and, playing fully by the rules of a democratic society, held it to its declared commitments, pressing it further and further as it retreated into a growing tangle of self-generated contradictions.  How could punishing a terrorist be said to compute with destroying the home in which that persons family lived?
 

There is no doubt that the occupying power became its own worst enemy, its crude tactics exposed and exploited by Al-Haqs sophisticated reporting.  Yet the strength of Israel was such that no amount of reporting could have brought an end to the military occupation.  It took a largely nonviolent popular uprising, massive repression marked by the extremely crude and brutal tactics of a Yitzhak Rabin, and the sudden interest of the mass media in the Palestinian reality to reverse what had seemed irreversible.  The leaders of the grassroots organizations at least, those not already in prison or exile metamorphosed into the leaders of the uprising.  And Al-Haq, its credibility established by years of accurate reporting and its voice amplified by the force of the mass movement, blasted onto the international scene.  Its magnum opus on the first year of the uprising, Punishing a Nation, became a standard reference work for human rights groups, journalists and policy makers alike.  Holly Burkhalter, an early admirer of Al-Haq who as advocacy director of Human Rights Watch oversaw the production and distribution of the U.S. edition of Punishing a Nation, today sees the significance of Al-Haq as twofold: There werent at that time that many groups in the South that were doing highly sophisticated human rights investigations, she says.  The movement, even in the West, was very young.  To have that kind of quality of documentation, especially from Palestinians, was very important in dispelling the prejudice that existed.  In the United States a Palestinian was an adjective modifying the noun terrorist.  Al-Haqs work was essential in getting out the truth not only about human rights violations in the Occupied Territories, but about the Palestinians themselves.
 

That was then and this is now.  As the intifada turned inward and petered out, Al-Haq lost much of its audience and its steam, while it also began to pay the price of too rapid and uncontrolled a growth in the late 1980s.  The 1993 Oslo Accords and the arrival of the Palestinian Authority further threw the organization into confusion, and except for pursuing the brilliant innovation that was its human rights enforcement project, it was forced to spend the better part of the 1990s trying to regain its footing.  Today, as it marks its twentieth anniversary, Al-Haq is a transformed organization, a different beast altogether, set to mark a course in tune with the requirements of the new situation prevailing in Palestine.
 

To me Al-Haq will always be the Al-Haq that I knew intimately then, the Al-Haq that gave me my best friends, that taught me my passable Arabic and gave me insight and an entrée into Palestinian society, and most importantly that showed me the power of human rights.  My five years in Palestine for me were a rite of passage, my formative years, the threshold that separates the before from the after in my life.  Working for Al-Haq was frustrating at times the infuriating but indispensable legalese of its analysis; the primary institutional (elite) focus of its activism.  It often forced me to step outside and engage in extracurricular activities: writing articles for magazines in the U.S., Britain and the Netherlands, traveling throughout the West Bank to document house demolitions while delivering basic necessities to the newly homeless, and making friends among the people who were most affected by the daily depredations of the occupier

all of this on top of research and writing relating to my doctoral studies.  But my experience at Al-Haq never was anything but authentic, full of meaning, extraordinary.
When I left Al-Haq in 1990 it occupied a large office across the street from the old one, with two dozen well-trained staff, plenty of computers, and its spirit unbent despite the never-ending misery it had mandated itself to document in towns, villages and refugee camps.  I sometimes now recall, at night when my children have gone to sleep and I have a moment to myself, the sounds that uniquely evoke for me my privileged existence in what was my home for five matchless years: the constant roar of the bulldozers razing dwellings and trees to make way for new settlements, the angry bark of soldiers breaking open stores on strike, and the wailing of mothers newly bereaved.  But also, amidst the din, distinctly and unmistakably, the steadfast and rhythmic tap tap tap of the keyboards at Al-Haq.

Willful Killings: A Sustained Israeli Policy in the Occupied Palestinian Territories

 
Based on its own documentation of over 1070 killings of Palestinian civilians by the Israeli soldiers and other state agents since the Intifada began on 9 December 1987, Al-Haq fieldwork revealed that many of these deaths were willful killings within the meaning of Article 147 of the 1949 Fourth Geneva Convention. Starting with the definition of willful killing as an intentional killing which is unnecessary or disproportionate under the circumstances, Al-Haq, highlighted the continued occurrence of willful killings with alarming frequency in the Occupied Territories.

Al-Haq then presented in details three willful killing cases that provide fresh evidence that Palestinians in the Occupied Territories are continuing to face a consistent pattern of intentional violations of their right to life-- a pattern amounting to a sustained policy of willful killing sanctioned by law and carried out by agents of the Israeli state.  In all three cases presented in this focus and backed with evidence, Al-Haq concluded that the killing of these three victims during August, September and October 1992, was unnecessary and disproportionate; all three victims were unarmed and have not posed any threat to the lives of Israeli soldiers.

In the concluding remarks, Al-Haq stated that the issues discussed in the three cases were not new; for years Palestinians have suffered at the hands of Israeli state agents from willful killing, willful injury, obstruction of medical aid, the killing of children, failure to protect bystanders from harm, impermissible restrictions on the rights to freedom of expression and peaceful assembly, attacks on persons associated with "wanted people", disrespect for manners, customs, and religious practices and many other abuses. Such abuses are violations of international law legalized by military orders and decrees which criminalize many political and civil rights.  The orders and decrees are enforced by military personnel, some disguised as Palestinians, who use vague and broad Open Fire Regulations while capturing or killing Palestinians who are simply practicing the rights guaranteed them under international law.

An International Conference on the Administration of Occupied Territories and International Law

By Emma Playfair, Executive Director of Interights, The International Centre for the Protection f Human Righhts

On 22 to 25 January 1988, in the unlikely context of the first month of the Palestinian intifada, or popular uprising, Al-Haq held an international conference in East Jerusalem, convening some of the foremost world experts in the international laws of war and human rights. This was the first international conference of its kind to be organized within the Occupied Territories. Most of those attending were not supporters of the Palestinian cause but scholars who had, until then, had little contact with the Arab-Israeli conflict. They came to provide independent opinions on the international laws governing the conduct of an occupying power over the people and territories it controlled, and to review Israels administration of the West Bank and Gaza, including East Jerusalem, against international law.
 

By 1987, Israel's occupation of the West Bank and Gaza was entering its third decade, with little sign of an end. Over the previous five years, Al-Haq had established a solid reputation for its meticulous reporting of the human rights violations that formed part of the daily life of Palestinians in the Occupied Territories. As well as intervening on individual violations - either directly or by seeking the intervention of others  it had published a series of reports on such practices as deportation, administrative detention, torture, demolition and sealing of houses and separation of families. These interventions and reports were based on extensive fieldwork and always supported by carefully researched legal arguments as to why these acts constituted violations of human rights or humanitarian law. The clarity and constraint of the reports was hard to contest and contributed to a shift in international public opinion that had generally subscribed to Israel's projected image as a benevolent occupier respectful of the laws of occupation.
 

While Al-Haq had to address such violations on a daily basis, it was all too aware of the longer term threat to Palestinian rights posed by the steady transformation of the land and infrastructure of the territories themselves, altering, sometimes irrevocably, their character and integrity. Israel was introducing changes to almost every element of Palestinian life  to the economic structure, the judicial system, local government, systems of transportation, water, electricity and, most of all, to ownership and use of land. While some such changes were undoubtedly necessary with the passage of time, many appeared to be aimed at ensuring dependency on Israel or access by Israel to Palestinian resources and markets, or at serving the interests of the Israeli population being settled illegally in the Territories, rather than at serving the long-term interests of the Palestinian peoples future after the end of the occupation.
 

It is an underlying tenet of the international law governing military occupation that occupation is an inherently temporary state and that the status quo should be observed, with only those changes which are necessary for the security of the occupying forces or in the best interests of the people, so that the ability of the territories ultimately to regain their independence is not jeopardized. Al-Haq carefully documented the changes made by Israel and argued that many could not be justified on the limited grounds allowed by international law. It demonstrated further that they had profound and adverse implications for the Palestinian governance of the territories after the end of the occupation, and for the ability of the Palestinian community to exercise the fundamental human right of self-determination. Israel argued, however, that it respected the rules of international law governing its occupation, or 'administration' as it preferred to call it, but that the occupation was sui generis by reason of its length amongst other things. It relied on this firstly to argue that the main body of law relating to belligerent occupation, the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949, was not applicable, and secondly to justify its own interpretations of the applicable laws, often at variance with earlier interpretations.
 

The difficulty faced by Al-Haq in countering this claim was that the terms of the Hague Regulations of 1907 and the Fourth Geneva Convention are necessarily framed as general principles, in order to cover all forms of war. While certain provisions, such as those relating to destruction of property, deportation of peoples and treatment of prisoners, are quite clear, there are few details in the guidelines as to how the territories themselves should be governed. For this, authoritative interpretation was needed, based not only on those laws, but on the military manuals drawn up in previous wars, and on such legal precedents as the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials that followed the Second World War. Expertise was hard to find though, since the laws of war were at that time viewed as an obscure branch of the law.(1) Belligerent occupation in particular had generated little attention outside Israel since the end of the second world war, so that Israeli scholars, judges and officials, much of whose expertise was based on service within Israel's army or administration, dominated the field.
 

In the absence of independent scholarship on these issues, Al-Haq decided to seek the advice of leading scholars of the laws of war, international law and international human rights law to bring their expertise to bear on key issues relating to the administration of occupied territories. We recognized that for most this would require original research, since few had considered this issue before, but we counted on being able to stimulate the interest of academics to apply their learning to a concrete situation. We therefore designed a research project, to culminate in an international conference, through which international legal scholars, carefully selected for their independence, and in most cases without prior involvement in the conflict, would provide reasoned opinions on the current interpretations of the international laws of war and of human rights as they applied to Israel's occupation and treatment of the Occupied Territories. The legal opinions had to be based on the actual situation, but published sources on these issues are few so the international experts would be matched with local scholars and experts who would document and analyze the factual issues from within the Occupied Territories, often also requiring original research.
 

We deliberately avoided issues which normally attracted the attention of observers, in favor of the more subtle aspects of territorial, administrative and infrastructural transformation which were more complex to document and on which legal sources were few, given the rarity of long occupations. The treatment of natural resources, the power to appropriate and change usage of land, the taxation of the people, the reshaping of the legal system, the integration of the economy with that of the occupying power, the settlement of the occupiers own population in the occupied territories, the separate administrative and legal structure applied to those settlers, and the application of the various sources of humanitarian law and human rights law governing the conduct of a belligerent occupier - these issues, which were steadily eroding the potential of the West Bank and Gaza to be self-sustaining, formed the object of our inquiry.
 

Identification of leading scholars involved extensive research and consultation, and then often careful persuasion. Some were cautious about involvement in what they perceived as a political minefield, and needed reassurance about Al-Haq's credentials, as to the serious nature of the initiative and that they were genuinely being asked to voice independent opinions. International participants included Gerhard von Glahn, author of the leading work on belligerent occupation, Adam Roberts, one of the foremost scholars of the laws of war, Antonio Cassese, later President of the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia, John Dugard, an expert in human rights law from South Africa, and other eminent experts in public international law and human rights law.
 

On 9 December 1987, one month before the conference was to be held, the intifada broke out in Gaza. By the time the conference started, on 22 January 1988, the intifada had spread to all parts of the Occupied Territories. 41 Palestinians had been killed, almost half of them below the age of 18. Despite powerful press coverage of the rapid escalation of violence, none of the participants withdrew. They did, however, express concern at discussing apparently abstract questions of law while people died outside. It became quickly apparent though that the questions under discussion, however arcane, were at the heart of the intifada. It was precisely because of the actions Israel was taking in the name of the law  the expropriations of land, the annexing of Jerusalem, the absorption of the occupied territories into the occupiers state  denying a future to the people, that years of pent-up frustration exploded into the intifada. Through meetings and visits, arranged between sessions, the participants were able to witness both the causes of the intifada and its violent repression by the Israeli authorities.
 

The project had three elements. The first was to clarify the framework of international law relating to belligerent occupation, particularly the application of the Hague Regulations and the Geneva Convention, and the applicability of human rights law in times of occupation. The second element was to examine particular aspects of administration of occupied territories, namely the form an administration should take, the right of association under occupation reflecting peoples ability to participate in the administration, taxation, and the powers and duties of an occupier in relation to land. The final aim of the project was to consider methods of enforcement of humanitarian and human rights law, both through law and by mobilizing public opinion and requiring action.
 

Participants rejected resoundingly Israel's contention that the Fourth Geneva Convention does not apply to its occupation. The inherently temporary nature of occupation was emphasized again and again, as was the illegality of any steps taken that irreversibly alter the character of the territories occupied, except within well-defined situations of necessity, or which seek to assert sovereignty or to pre-empt eventual withdrawal from the territories occupied. Far from confirming Israel's contention that the length of the occupation permitted it to override these provisions, participants agreed that the longer the occupation, the more important it was to hold firmly to the provisions and to interpret them restrictively. Participants also confirmed that human rights law applies, with certain exceptions, even under occupation, supplementing the provisions of humanitarian law. Both international and national participants criticized, to varying degrees, the Israeli High Courts rulings and expressed disappointment at its role. Views differed on important points  for instance the extent to which the occupation itself is illegal (some felt that it was, while others felt that international law does not yet support this point), the weight that an occupier can attach to military necessity, and the legitimacy of a levée en masse, as an expression of will of the people.
 

Immediately after the conference, Al-Haq had necessarily to devote its attention very substantially to addressing the daily emergencies and gross violations occurring during the intifada, which continued to stretch its capacity to the limit for a number of years. While Al-Haqs Enforcement Project, develop at the same time as the Conference, drew on and developed the arguments discussed at the conference, we were not otherwise able to devote the resources needed to develop a systematic challenge to those aspects of Israeli policy which the conference had shown to be clearly violative of international law, as planned. However, the publication of the papers by a respected legal publisher ensured that the findings of the conference reached a wide audience both within Palestine and Israel, and in the international legal community.
 

The conference exemplified Al-Haqs approach, which was that of an activist organization, basing its work only on the rule of law and on internationally established standards of human rights and humanitarian law, employing the strictest professional and independent standards. Arising from Al-Haqs frustration at the lack of available independent expertise, which hindered its ability to address serious violations of human rights, the project typically relied on Al-Haqs fieldwork and brought to bear both international and local expertise to seek clarification of the crucial provisions of international law relating to a belligerent occupation.
 

A decade after the conference, its findings remain relevant not only to those seeking to address ongoing human rights violations in the Palestinian territories, but also to civilians caught in other situations of occupation or conflict. The published views of legal experts are an important source of international and customary law, so that the expert contributions to this conference will directly contribute to the development and interpretation of humanitarian law. We hope also that the conclusions of the international and local experts, documenting the effects of the illegal measures and clearly establishing the violations of international law, can be of use in negotiations towards an end to the Israeli occupation, which international law requires, and in the determination of restitution and compensation.

The Enforcement Project

By Dr. Lynn Welchman, Director of the Centre of Islamic and Middle Eastern Law, SOAS, University of London
 

Years after the enforcement project started at Al-Haq, I was having a conversation with a prominent human rights lawyer about universal jurisdiction and the deterrent potential of prosecutions, against the background of the Rwanda and former Yugoslavia tribunals.  I brought up (as one does, especially if one has worked at Al-Haq) the issue of grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions and she looked at me and said yes, you lot at Al-Haq were really ahead of your time.  It was, I felt, a well-deserved tribute to the organization, its founders, and the people who worked there. The vision of Al-Haq was so fresh and innovative, in its time and context, that for years after its establishment you still found yourself having to explain it - and often defend it - both in Palestine and abroad.  This was also the case with the enforcement project, a project born and nurtured at Al-Haq which, despite developing into a difficult child, provided a source of innovative argument and advocacy that built on Al-Haqs core strengths to take the challenge of enforcement out to those properly charged with it under the law.
 

The founder of the enforcement project was of course Charles Shammas, one of the original founders of Al-Haq.  It was Charles who recruited me to Al-Haq in Ramallah in the early months of 1983 when, after a rather unnerving interview, I found myself seated for the first time ever behind a computer (very large and complete with all sorts of suspiciously whirring noises) working on the database project.  These were very early days: Al-Haq had at that time no telephone (we would run up Main Street to Raja Shehadahs office if we needed to use one) and not very good wiring: the computer in particular was sensitive to electricity dips and I remember several occasions realizing someone was about to enter the bathroom and yelling dont turn the light on... just too late to save my day.  Also imprinted in my memory is the first time the computer faced me (and my life) with those two most chilling of words: FATAL ERROR.  It still makes me shudder.
 

By the time the enforcement project got up and running, Al-Haq had moved into the new office across the road and expanded its staff and its mandate considerably.  Preliminary work during 1987 showed a market (as Charles would, inevitably, term it, to the consternation of some of our colleagues) for the arguments and from then on, against the background of the intifada, the enforcement team pursued the arguments for international protection on the basis of third party states legal obligations.  These arguments proceeded from the fundamental premise that all High Contracting Parties to the Fourth Geneva Convention Relative to the Protection of Civilian Persons in Time of War have a direct duty under law towards the protected population of occupied territories.  This is through the inclusion in article 1 common to all four Geneva Conventions of the inter-state duty to ensure not only that a states own agents and institutions respect the terms of the Conventions, but also that other state signatories respect them in all circumstances. This duty distinguishes the position of the international community of states from the position it generally seeks to adopt vis-à-vis human rights violations that occur within the sovereign territory of another state (with certain exceptions).  This duty to respect and to ensure respect for the Convention is developed in Article 146 of the Fourth Geneva Convention, which creates an obligation to prosecute individuals of any nationality responsible for  committing grave breaches of the Convention (that is to say, war crimes, listed in Article 147).  In 1977, the duty was further developed in Article 89 of the First Additional Protocol to the Geneva Conventions by mandating HCPs to act individually and jointly in cases of serious violations of the Convention or the Protocol, in cooperation with the United Nations and in conformity with the UN Charter.
 

The members of Al-Haqs enforcement team were mandated by the organization to dedicate their work time to developing these and related legal arguments, articulating the thinking behind them, including the link between durable conflict resolution and the prohibition under the law of predatory and annexationist conduct and related violations on the part of an Occupying Power, and identifying and exploring ways and means available to powerful third party states to discharge this obligation as effectively as possible.  It was the difference between having states assert their right to comment on Israels behavior in the Occupied Territories, and having them move towards statements and actions that acknowledged a legal duty to uphold the protections of international humanitarian law in the face of the refusal of successive Israel governments to recognize the de jure applicability of the Convention and to behave accordingly.
 

Enforcement work took members of the team to Brussels and Strasbourg to meet with MEPs and members of the European Commission, producing painstakingly detailed briefings on the law and the instruments controlled by European states and the then Community through which they might fulfil their obligations.  In national contexts in Europe the team worked with domestic non-governmental organizations and parliamentarians to bring the arguments to the attention of their governments, with national symposia organized in London 1989, Dublin 1990, The Hague 1991 and London again 1992. The team worked with lawyers, academics, activists, international human rights organizations, development agencies, civil servants and politicians. Marc Stephens paper, published in 1989 as Enforcement of International Law in the Israeli-occupied Territories, provided us with an initial legal briefing; otherwise most of our writing was done in the form of targeted briefings and interventions to and often for particular third parties and in specific contexts. At home, the project became increasingly integrated into Al-Haqs main program, including contributing the final chapters to each of the second and third annual reports on human rights violations during the uprising (Nation under Siege and Protection Denied).  Enforcement arguments were incorporated into the body of Al-Haqs interventions, press releases and group briefings.
 

In pursuit of legal remedies, the enforcement team looked at setting up grave breaches files and sought advice from European lawyers as to what would be needed to activate the domestic legislation incorporating the grave breaches provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention.   The grave breaches of the Convention identified as such in the terms of the law itself (article 147) include unlawful deportation, willful killing, unlawful confinement, torture or inhuman treatment, and other crimes being carried out routinely by the Israeli occupation forces against the Palestinian population at that time.  Although quite a bit of interest was raised in the subject, it became clear (and remains so, despite recent developments and the growing consensus around the exercise of universal jurisdiction) that the legal remedies provided for under international humanitarian law -- in terms of third-state prosecutions for grave breaches -- were effectively not available for use in the protection of an occupied population unless you had real political will on your side from the state in which the evidence was going to be produced.  Even getting the right sort of evidence was going to be difficult for an  NGO; but it was important to take the prospect of legal remedy seriously.
 

Another instrument on which the project concentrated was the EUs preferential relations with Israel, on the basis that so long as Israel remained in systematic and grave violation of its existing commitments to the member states of the EU (as fellow High Contracting Parties to the Fourth Geneva Convention) there were serious obstacles to the EU considering the extension of further preferential terms to its relations with Israel.  This particular aspect of the work has new dimensions in the debate on the ratification in various EU member states of the EU-Israel Association Agreement with its human rights conditionality clause in article 2.
 

Much of the enforcement teams effort went into developing legal argument and seeking to have key actors acknowledge the implications of third party state obligations under international humanitarian law. This meant making the law and complex legal arguments meaningful to politicians and other non-lawyers, without engaging ourselves in politics.  It meant getting and keeping busy peoples attention on the strength of nothing but the rigor, integrity and innovative energy of the arguments.  In all this the enforcement team - which included many Al-Haqqers down the years - was grounded in and supported by the energy of the organization itself, with Al-Haqs core work providing the basis on which every argument was founded, and Al-Haqs vision setting the objectives.
 

There were landmarks along the way, including the Dublin Declaration of 1990, when the European Council for the first time referred to the article 1 obligation in a policy declaration on the Middle East; Security Council Resolution 681 of the same year, when the Council directly called for action in accordance with article 1 and endorsed the idea of convening a meeting of High Contracting Parties to discuss what measures might be taken under the Convention.  The idea of a conference was of course re-visited this summer.  Low points the same year, just by way of illustration, included the resolute refusal (continued till today) of powerful third parties to use the term grave breach when referring to Israels treatment of the Palestinian population protected by the Convention.  Just as, years later, the US was to hedge during crucial months on the use of the word genocide in relation to the massacres being carried out in Rwanda, so the legal implications of grave breaches were constrained to the UN resolutions regarding Iraqs conduct in Kuwait at this same time in 1990.
 

Like Al-Haq itself, and perhaps human rights workers everywhere, the enforcement project took a determinedly anti-cynical stand in the face of what were - and are - admittedly rather compelling challenges: its whole effort was premised on the possibility of getting influential third party states to move - incrementally and in ways that would serve as precedent - towards law-based enforcement of the protections enshrined in international humanitarian law, as a part of their foreign policy.  The point was to take the law at its word, to take its guarantors at their word, and to call them on it.  Without being grandiose, the whole of humanity, and sooner rather than later, has an interest in knowing that humanitarian law can and will work to protect those who have effectively no other protection.  That argument still has to be made, particularly to powerful states (specifically the EU and USA) whose record of stunning selectivity in matters of international law enforcement, combined with the weight of their power and influence, serves to undermine their own claims of universality of values, to the detriment of the struggle to promote and protect human rights around the world.
 

In Palestine, the fact that international humanitarian law has been so emphatically left out of the successive Israel-PLO agreements in the various agreements making up the Oslo Accords has presented particular obstacles to law-based action by key third party states. Since 1993, the continuation of the peace process itself -- on almost any terms, it seems -- has been the overriding political priority; and since Israel has presented any attempt to introduce the law into the equation as interference with a properly bilateral process, backed up in this by the US, the arguments for law-based enforcement action have been even harder to make than before. This summer, the debate on whether or not a conference of High Contracting Parties should be convened, as demanded by the General Assembly, to consider measures to enforce the Convention in the Occupied Palestinian Territory rekindled the chronic argument on whether the political imperative of supporting the Palestinian-Israeli peace process can be reconciled with the imperative to uphold the rule of law.  The result was, for the law, and arguably for the peace process also, not a felicitous one.
 

Whether, when the final settlement is formulated, its terms will show recognition of and deference to the regulatory framework of international humanitarian law; whether that same framework and other principles of international law will be recognized as properly governing matters of redress and reparation; in short whether, in the end, the law will finally come to the protection of the rights of the Palestinian population, albeit after the fact for many of them -- these are pressing and imminent enforcement-related questions that challenge Al-Haq and other human rights organizations today.  They will moreover stand as precedent for what the law can and cannot be made to do for those dependent upon its protection, both in Palestine and elsewhere.  These are pressing times for enforcement work.

Al-Haq and its Societal Programs: The Women's Rights Program

By Randa Seniora

"Go do something with your partner Amnesty International regarding arbitrary arrests, torture and ill treatment of Palestinian prisoners by the Israeli military occupation, and leave women's problems to be resolved privately and discretely by the extended Family".
 

This was the immediate response of a pioneering women's figure heading a charitable women's organization as I introduced to her the newly initiated Women's Human Rights Program at Al-Haq in 1987.  In her opinion, and in the opinion of many others within the Palestinian society at the time, the work of human rights organizations should only be focussed on human rights violations committed by the Israeli military occupation.  Human rights meant to them "political rights" but not "economic, social and cultural rights."  Such rights in their opinion should only be raised in the context of the Israeli violations of these rights.  Societal human rights violations committed against women and other marginalized groups within the family and the society were not seen as a priority and were kept at the very end of the Palestinian national and human rights agenda.
 

Al-Haqs Relationship with the International Commission of Jurists - Geneva
 

Al-Haq, Law in the Service of Man, the first Palestinian human rights institution, began its days in April 1979 when a group of West Bank Palestinians established it to uphold the principles of the Rule of Law and to defend Palestinians human rights in the Occupied Palestinian Territories.  It became officially registered in 1980.  Shortly thereafter, it became the West Bank affiliate of the International Commission of Jurists in Geneva.   For a period of at least 10 years, Al-Haq was the sole affiliate of the ICJ-Geneva in the Occupied Territories.  This affiliation was particularly important during the upheaval of the intifada.  ICJ-Geneva stood steadfastly behind Al-Haq offering it protection as a human rights institution in the Occupied Territories during which time staff members were occasionally harassed or even arrested by the Israeli authorities.  This relationship fostered over a twenty-year period has strengthened the work of Al-Haq at the local level and has had considerable effect upon its work.  At the same time, this affiliation has assisted in spreading information related to the defense of human rights in the Occupied Territories internationally and raising awareness at the level of the ICJ regarding the rule of law.  This has been particularly important in helping the ICJ identify its priorities in the OPT.
 

In the more recent years, Adama Dieng, Secretary-General of the ICJ, has contributed significantly to strengthening Al-Haq as a human rights institution.
 

The relationship between Al-Haq and the ICJ is a very active one. The ICJ has co-ordinated with Al-Haq several missions that examined the legal structure of the Occupied Territories and the judicial system. The outcomes and findings of these missions were very essential for Al-Haqs work, and are referred to by many local and international organisations. Also, the ICJ responded positively to Al-Haqs repeated requests to arrange for jurists with outstanding experience to observe trials of special significance to Palestinians in Israeli courts. The ICJ has also intervened with the Israeli authorities on many essential issues, on the basis of information from and at the request of Al-Haq.
 

The ICJ has also provided an essential facilitating role for Al-Haq in the UN system. Through the ICJ, Al-Haq could access the UN Commission on Human Rights, in addition to many of the other specialised bodies of the UN. The activities of Al-Haq in the UN have been in total co-ordination with the ICJ, which helped in many cases to advance mutual agendas. The Vienna World Conference on Human Rights and the appointment of a Special Rapporteur on the Occupied Palestinian Territories are relevant examples.
 

Since the establishment of the PNA, Al-Haq has closely been involved in supporting the development of an independent Palestinian Judiciary.   In relation to this, Al-Haq in 1993 in cooperation with the ICJ held a seminar entitled Towards an Independent Palestinian Judiciary.  This took place at the end of a two-week fact-finding mission conducted by the ICJ and its Center for the Independence of Judges and Lawyers concerning the situation of civil courts in the Occupied Territories.  The purpose of the mission was to pinpoint the problems and obstacles facing the judicial system as a whole, in light of the accepted principles of the rule of law.  Following this mission the findings of it were set out in a book entitled The Civilian Judicial System in the West Bank and Gaza: Present and Future, as well as recommendations following the seminar. These recommendations specifically outlined suggestions on the requirements of legal and judicial functions that would help lead towards an independent Palestinian judiciary.
 

A second mission, similar to the one carried out in 1993, was carried out by the ICJ and its Center for the Independence of Judges and Lawyers from January 15-25, 2000.  This mission specifically was aimed at reviewing the independence of the Palestinian Judiciary and the legal profession in Palestine and the role of the Palestinian authorities.  It also examined the role of the international financial institutions with regards to questions related to the functioning of the judiciary and Israeli infringements of the administration of justice in the Occupied Territories.  The recommendations that resulted from this mission built upon the initial recommendations that the ICJ made during its first mission in 1993.  The role of the mission was also to examine whether the previous recommendations were carried out in the six-year period interim.   In addition, during this mission, Al-Haq in conjunction with the two other Palestinian human rights institutions affiliated with the ICJ, the Palestinian Center for Human Rights and LAW, held two workshops in the West Bank and the Gaza Strip regarding The Role of the Judiciary in Civil Society.
 

It is with great pleasure that we begin a new period in Al-Haqs history, building on our past relationships and entering into new ones.  In January 2000, the United Nations Committee on Non-Governmental Organizations recommended Al-Haq for Special Consultative Status with the United Nations Economic and Social Council.  This is truly an achievement for Al-Haq as a human rights institution not only in Palestine but also in the Arab world.  We take this responsibility seriously in continuing to persevere in our ultimate aim: the strengthening of the Rule of Law and the defense of Palestinians human rights as an important aspect of reinforcing international recognition of the rights of all peoples to self-determination.

Al-Haq recommended for Special Consultative Status with ECOSOC
 
 
 
In July 1996 the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) of the United Nations passed a resolution, that enabled NGOs to gain an official consultative status with the bodies of ECOSOC and UN conferences, thus acknowledging the vital and ever growing role of NGOs in supporting the work of the UN. This arrangement for cooperation between the ECOSOC and NGOs gives especially national NGOs a wider and more independent access to the ECOSOC and its mechanisms to address local and regional human rights issues related to the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights in an international forum and therefore opens new ways to generate pressure for improvement at the international level.
Al-Haq was informed in January 2000 by the Committee on Non-Governmental Organizations, that it recommended to grant the Special Consultative Status in the field of human rights to Al-Haq. Though still subject to the approval of the ECOSOC, this accreditation, as it coincides with Al-Haqs 20th anniversary, comes as an acknowledgement of Al-Haqs two decades long struggle for Palestinian human rights, its legal expertise gained in this time and the high quality of its documentation and analysis of human rights violations in the Israeli Occupied Palestinian Territories. The ability to submit written reports, oral statements and, on request, special studies and investigations directly to the relevant bodies of ECOSOC, will be a great help for Al-Haqs efforts to draw international attention to and generate international pressure against Israeli violations of Palestinian human rights in such vital issues as the denial of family reunification, house demolition and many more, as well as on matters concerning social justice and development, such as education and womens rights.

 
Interview with Raja Shehadeh, founding member of Al-Haq

 

Mr. Shehadeh, you have been one of the founders of Al-Haq, which achievements did you strive for by establishing such an organization?
 

Before the establishment of Al-Haq there was no local organization carrying out human rights work.  Al- Haq was the first organization to do this kind of work.
 

We also strove to create a committed organization that was politically independent. This too was a first since many of the active non-governmental organizations working in the occupied territories were politically affiliated and serving one or the other of the political organizations.
 

We also had the conscious objective of creating a truly democratic organization. One that was fully owned and run by its staff.
 

The spirit of voluntarism was also very important. The founding members and several of the staff volunteered their services free of charge. They strove for no other benefits other than the satisfaction to see the success of their work. There was a unique and profound commitment on the part of those involved in the creation and development of Al-Haq.
 

This is as far as the type of organization which the founding members tried to establish. As to the kind of work done by the organization, the organization tried from the beginning to fill the enormous gap in the absence of documentation of human rights violations committed by the Israeli occupation. The advocacy on behalf of the victims of human rights violations was always a primary objective as was the promotion of the rule of law. The organization also established the first public legal and human rights library in the West Bank.
 

Do you think Al-Haq had an influence on other organizations of that kind in the region?
 

The fact, that such an outspoken and active organization as Al-Haq was possible in the occupied territories had, I believe, a strong influence on the neighboring countries. They looked up to Al-Haq both because of the fact that it was able to operate at all. But they also studied carefully how it was organized. Some of the innovative ideas generated by A- Haq extended also to the legal approach it was taking to various matters it dealt with. The enforcement project, for example, produced important literature which had influence within and outside the region.
 

It is important to remember that prior to the creation of Al-Haq the Israeli Position on the nature and conduct of the occupation was the only one heard in the world. Al-Haq was able through the approach and kind of work it did to gain credibility and become an alternative voice which eventually made a complete shift in the perception of the world about the nature of the Israeli occupation.
 

Al-Haq always tried to enforce the applicability of the Fourth Geneva Convention concerning the West Bank and Gaza. Do you think it succeeded in that issue?
 

The position that Al-Haq took from the beginning was that the Fourth Geneva Convention is applicable and it used it along with the Hague Regulation as standards against which to assess the human rights situation. In this the organization had made many allies. Only a political process could have succeeded in getting Israel to concede that the Fourth Geneva Convention Applied. This was beyond the powers of the organization. But the fact that Israel refused this did not deter the organization from insisting on its principle position and forging ahead with its work.
 

Which impact did the beginning of the peace process have on the work of Al-Haq?
 

It is my belief that the organization missed an important opportunity, which the beginning of the peace process provided for the following reasons:
 

First there was the best opportunity to use the strong interest generated by the political developments to promote the positions Al-Haq had been advocating for years, and, second, because the organization should have used its good standing and substantial network of friends and allies in the international human rights movement to have more of an impact on the course and outcome of the negotiations.
 

Rather than attempt to study the new situation and see what new opportunities it provided, the organization was paralyzed by the political considerations of what political position it should take towards the negotiations. This was a very grave failure which went against the ethos and tradition of the organization.
 

As a result only lip service was paid to human rights and the accords that came out of the negotiations did not have a human rights component to them.
 

How do you see the present role of a human rights organization such as Al-Haq?
 

The present situation differs in many fundamental ways from the past. I have been an outsider to the human rights work since 1991 when I left Al-Haq. From my previous involvement I realize that only an insider is privy to all the factors and conditions that must be taken into consideration in developing a strategy for human rights work. It is difficult and perhaps not prudent for me as an outsider to pontificate on these matters. What I can say is that the important feature of the present situation is its transitional nature. Perhaps this should influence to a large degree the kind of work that the organization does. Transitional situations can provide an opportunity for establishing and fortifying traditions as well as institutions which may become much more difficult to achieve once the situation settles into a permanent political structure.
 

Do you think peoples attitudes towards human rights have changed within the last twenty years?
 

The best evidence that there has been a change in peoples attitude is the fact that when Al-Haq began no one could understand what human rights work meant. There was a lot of suspicion surrounding the work and its motivation. Challenge and suspicion remain. There will always be those who reject human rights work, but they are now in the minority.

At the same time the human rights education must be an ongoing activity. Much of Todays perceptions have also to do with how the past is viewed. One of the important developments which have occurred, I believe, is that there is an understanding now that human rights work was not only a feature of the occupation and should end with the end of occupation.

How does the fact that the PA came into power influence Al-Haqs work?
 

The emphasis in Al-Haqs work has always been the legal aspects. The PA tends to neglect the legal aspects. There is a lot of work for Al-Haq to do in developing the rule of law and acting as a watchdog to ensure that the legal developments are consistent with the human right principles. There is much work for the organization to do and much ingenuity is needed to determine where the line should be drawn between human rights work and political work.
 

At the beginning you mentioned that Al-Haq was also established as a new-idea organization quoting participation of its members and its democratic structure. Should the way the organization is structured change after this transitional period? Could a membership model comply with our needs?
 

The initial structure the organization took was related to what it aimed to achieve. There was no recognition of human rights as a profession. The organization wanted to develop human rights activists rather than employees in a human rights organization. This objective has now been achieved. The structure of the organization should develop in line with the organizations objectives.
 

Whether the organization should become a membership organization is related to whether it wants to become a pressure group which will have impact as a movement that draws strength from its membership. But if Al-Haq wants to continue to be an organization that provides very specialized work, it may not need to change into a membership organization. It should also be borne in mind that a membership organization is more susceptible to political pressure.
 
 

Lots of our work has been done in the field of woman's rights, especially through our publications. We still have not installed a woman's rights unit but we are helping other organization out in that field. So do you think that Al-Haq has contributed to the issue of woman's rights?
 

In the past there was a strong womens right movement. Al-Haqs role was to provide it with legal advice and promote the legal aspect of the work of the movement. I believe that much has been achieved in this regard. The legal considerations are fully incorporated in the activities of the womens movements. Many specialized womens rights organizations have been established which are very effective.

Under the prevailing conditions Al-Haq has to find its own niche providing services that others cannot provide due to the special nature and connections of the organization. Al-Haq should continue to work in the field of womens rights in pursuit of its general aim which is the enforcement of the rule of law.

Al-Haq used international podiums, UN-mechanisms, international campaigns and conferences as a way of promoting and defending human rights. Do you think this is a role that Al-Haq should continue to have under the PA?
 

Al-Haq must have strategy of how to help Palestine move ahead in the future as far as human rights are concerned. In future it could help Palestine as a state join the various human rights convention and afterwards help ensure that they are implemented.
 

Retaining the connection with the international bodies remains an important function for Al-Haq. You are part of the world. In former times we used these fora and institutions to advocate human rights positions. Now you have to make the necessary adjustments and continue to interact with the Human rights movement worldwide for the service of the organizations present objectives.

Torture for Security
The systematic torture and ill treatment of Palestinians by Israel
Melissa Phillips, 1995
Since its inception in 1979, Al-Haq has been concerned with the problem of torture in Israeli interrogation centers. This study represents the culmination of Al-Haqs efforts over many years to achieve a cessation of the practice, and to alert the international community to the prevalence of this systematic, widespread and gross violation of Palestinians human rights.

 

Hopes that the practice of torture would end with the peace process were frustrated. The use of torture continued, in spite of the fact, that Israel is a Party signatory to the Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT) since 1991. As recently as September 1999 and only after a long and determined campaign by human rights organizations, including Al-Haq, the Israeli High Court of Justice finally decided, that torture is indeed illegal in Israel. However, the High court decision did not rule out the possibility to legitimize the use of torture through new legislation. Thus, at the moment, four bill have been introduced to the Knesset  two with the aim to pass a law that would officially allow torture for so called security reasons, while the other two promote the implementation of a total ban on torture in Israeli legislation. The issue has yet to be decided by the Knesset. Meanwhile, the practice of torture against Palestinian detainees in Israeli interrogation centers was not brought to an end by the High Court decision, but new excuses were found for it: for example hand cuffing in painful positions is now explained to be necessary to protect interrogators from presumably aggressive and dangerous detainees.
 

In addition, the occurrence of torture against Palestinians in custody of the Palestinian Security Apparatus gives further need to Al-Haq and other human rights organizations to continue working on the subject of torture.

STOP SEPARATING PALESTINIAN FAMILIES!

An International Campaign on the Question of Family Reunification
(Based on Newsletter No 23, 1991)
THE PROBLEM.  The roots of the problem of Palestinian dispersion go back to the war of 1948, when more than one half of the population of Palestine lost their homeland and their right to live there.  This was made worse after the June war of 1967, when the remainder of Palestine was occupied and tens of thousands more were made homeless, some for the second time.

 

In the areas occupied since 1967, the Israeli authorities proceeded to implement a series of policies and measures, which resulted in the loss of the right of residency for Palestinians of the Occupied Territories.  From the Israeli authorities' point of view permanent residence and consequently family reunification is not an inalienable right for the Palestinians.  Rather, permanent residence is considered a privilege granted in accordance with certain criteria set by the authorities themselves.  This policy is detrimental to the right of a family to permanent residence and reunification and creates serious humanitarian and economic problems for tens of thousands of Palestinian families.  These problems continue unabated.
 

THE CAMPAIGN.  Given the humanitarian nature of the issue of family reunification, and the large number of people it affects, Al-Haq decided in 1990 to initiate an international campaign by mobilizing public opinion through the media, human rights and other non-governmental institutions. The public pressure exerted will hopefully succeed in effecting a change in Israeli policy towards family reunification. Further, it is Al-Haq's view that by publicizing widely the issue of Palestinian family reunification, public attention will be drawn to one of the most important aspects of the Palestinian problem: the dispersion and dispossession of the Palestinian people from their lands and homes.
 

The campaign, being the first of its kind to be adopted by Al-Haq, also represents a new, more activist approach in the organization's work. Lessons learned from this campaign will undoubtedly affect Al-Haq's approach to human rights activism, and will possibly open the way to other campaigns on important human rights issues in the future.

Al-Haq's "Women, Justice and Law" Campaign

(Based on Newsletter No. 33, 1994)

A conference took place between 16-19 September 1994 arising out of the project entitled: Women, Justice and Law: Towards the Empowerment of Palestinian Women.  The conference brought together more than 500 women and men to discuss and adopt strategies for securing the rights of Palestinian women through law.  It marked the culmination of a yearlong campaign throughout the Gaza Strip and West Bank to raise awareness and promote wide discussion of women and the law.
 

The conference and the campaign were organized by a steering committee of women activists under the auspices of Al-Haq, designed to bring together and build upon campaign activities and to formulate clear and effective action plans to promote the rights of women in the areas of health, education, work and social welfare, civil and political life, personal status law, and protection against violence.  The campaign included a number of different activities ranging from presentations and public discussions, local workshops, producing and distributing educational material to articles in the local press.
 

The wide participation of over 200 women in the campaign and conference preparation and the rich input by way of grass-roots participation ensured that efforts to develop strategies for the empowerment of women through law were rooted in and responsive to the day-to-day realities that characterize Palestinian women's lives.  The results of the campaign were used to draw up the program plan for Al-Haq's following Women's Rights Project.
 

Al-Haq's "Stop Destroying Palestinian Homes" Campaign
(Based on Newsletter No. 27, 1992)
 
Al-Haq continues to pursue its campaign to halt house demolition and sealing.  The aim of this campaign is to stop the destruction of houses in the Occupied Territories, which are demolished or sealed for "security" reasons, and to obtain compensation for the victims of these measures. Al-Haq has completed the preparations for all of the further requirements of the campaign, including explanatory booklets, T-shirts, and posters.

Al-Haq: The First Twenty Years

By Joost Hiltermann, Human Rights Watch
 

It was on a crisp November day in 1984 that I first stepped into the small apartment on Ramallahs main street that housed the offices of what was then known as Law in the Service of Man (a somewhat limping translation of the more universal Al-Qanoun min Ajal al-Insaan: Law in the Service of the? Human Being?).  The receptionist (who doubled as administrative assistant) sat in an entrance space immediately off a small glassed-in veranda.  The dining room served as meeting room-cum-library.  Two small bedrooms offered working space for researchers.  And the tiny bathroom accommodated copies of the organizations few but accumulating publications, stacked neatly on thin metal shelves that I, in an unthinking moment, once managed to collapse on top of me.  (There must have been a small kitchen as well, but I do not remember it now).
 

A handful of staff resided there in the fall of 1984.  The organizations founders, gainfully employed elsewhere, would stop by for meetings.  The three field investigators visited periodically to hand over and discuss their latest yield.  There were, if memory serves me, at most two or three computers.
 

I came to Al-Haq just at the time when it was primed for a major expansion, having got its books in order and, thanks to a handful of pioneering reports and actions, starting to receive the attention and, increasingly, accolades of key western institutions.  And it had the luck to arrive on the scene at what was from a funding perspective a particularly propitious time, the happy coincidence of the rapid growth of an international human rights movement and the equally stunning emergence of an indigenous institutional infrastructure in the Occupied Territories.  By the time the popular uprising broke out in late 1987, Al-Haq was able to deploy the strengths of one in the service of the other, and vice versa, becoming in the process an influential Palestinian voice at the height of a major human rights emergency.
 

The founders of LSM/Al-Haq, Raja Shehadeh, Jonathan Kuttab and Charles Shammas

started their professional careers in the West Bank just as a new generation of activists had begun to graduate from high schools and colleges.  This was the post-1967 generation, born perhaps before the June War but who had their formative period in the ferment that followed the 1973 War and Yasir Arafats landmark speech at the United Nations in 1974.  The external solution - a dream, really, of Arab states sweeping in to liberate the Palestinian masses - had been laid to rest, and a new national movement began to take form whose weight lay essentially inside the territory of historic Palestine.  At a grassroots level this new movement found its expression first in voluntary work committees that fanned out across villages and refugee camps to perform services that the occupying power would not.  Politically, it took the shape of student activism, including demonstrations and strikes, and the emergence of a local leadership
which was promptly subjected to threats, detention and deportation by the military government.  In the late 1970s, under the Likud partys interpretation of the autonomy plan agreed to in the Camp David Accords, the new civilian government in the Occupied Territories proceeded to expropriate vast new tracts of land and to weave a web of military orders that made a mockery of whatever law had existed in 1967.  This permitted the occupier to insinuate itself into all aspects of Palestinian society with the aim of subverting, dividing and, ultimately, pacifying it: an autonomy entirely on Israels terms.
The new laws and new violations screamed out for a new approach.  A fresh crop of recruits from schools and universities provided the intellectual core and urgent energy of an array of initiatives launched in the late 1970s and early 1980s.  The womens movement emerged in the West Bank and Gaza at this time; the trade unions became infused with new life; the student movement expanded; and a grassroots primary health movement was spawned from the ruins of the civilian governments health system.

 

Taking a nod from the international human rights movement that took off in earnest only after the 1975 Helsinki Accords, Al-Haqs founders were keenly attuned to the need for devising new strategies to contest the ever-growing reach of a predatory occupying power and its highly mobile citizenry.  The institution they established alongside other institutions that began to come into their own in this period: the universities, the Arab Studies Society, the Arab Thought Forum, and others, as well as aforementioned grassroots movements became by its very objectives a part of what sociologist Salim Tamari has referred to as a strategy of informal resistance to the military occupation.  Planted squarely in the society that had given rise to it, Al-Haq offered a glimpse to its constituency of the alternative path that might be taken to divest itself of the occupation.  It began to provide an ever-growing audience with new linguistic tools and a concrete methodology to challenge specific actions by the governing authority.  These were presented in the form of the Know Your Rights series, one of Al-Haqs most important undertakings during its first decade.  These booklets inculcated in those who read them the crucial notion that not only did they - Palestinians, victims, fighters, people - have fundamental rights, but they had a right to assert these rights, and the booklets showed them how they might begin to do so.
 

Even as Al-Haq was playing an educational role toward its home constituency, it reached out to the outside world both for a receptive audience and for protection, as the military government could never be trusted not to kick in the doors one day and shut the place down.  This, too, required a great educational effort, as a deep-seated prejudice against Palestinians dissuaded many activists in the United States and elsewhere from turning their attention to the Middle East and rendered Al-Haqs objectives and methodology immediately suspect.  If the organization succeeded in making its case abroad, this was due less to its advocacy strategy, which for a long time was barely a strategy at all, than to its professionalism in documenting and reporting what even the most skeptical observer would have to admit were unconscionable violations of human rights.  And the Israelis offered a helping hand, of course, by engaging in public acts of collective punishment, like house demolitions.
 

The Palestinian institutions and grassroots organizations that proliferated in the 1980s were served by their semi-legal status (even if the military government denied them a license to operate formally, by applying for a license they had signaled their intent to function openly and carry out legal activities), the material and political support they received from governments and institutions abroad, and the fact that Israel had an inwardly democratic and open society that was broadly permissive of outspoken criticism of the outrages committed across the Green Line.  But it was especially because of Israels outward democratic posture that the military government found it very difficult to crack down on institutions like Al-Haq, assuming for a moment that such institutions presented any sort of real threat to the occupiers hold over the territory and its population.  The Israeli state had a constant task to mollify its supporters in the U.S. and Europe, who saw little wrong with plucky groups like Al-Haq as long as such groups stayed away from advocating violence and terrorism.  The genius of the method fashioned by Al-Haqs founders was that Al-Haq took Israel at its word (of being a democracy, as well as a self-declared reluctant, tolerant and benign occupier) and, playing fully by the rules of a democratic society, held it to its declared commitments, pressing it further and further as it retreated into a growing tangle of self-generated contradictions.  How could punishing a terrorist be said to compute with destroying the home in which that persons family lived?
 

There is no doubt that the occupying power became its own worst enemy, its crude tactics exposed and exploited by Al-Haqs sophisticated reporting.  Yet the strength of Israel was such that no amount of reporting could have brought an end to the military occupation.  It took a largely nonviolent popular uprising, massive repression marked by the extremely crude and brutal tactics of a Yitzhak Rabin, and the sudden interest of the mass media in the Palestinian reality to reverse what had seemed irreversible.  The leaders of the grassroots organizations at least, those not already in prison or exile metamorphosed into the leaders of the uprising.  And Al-Haq, its credibility established by years of accurate reporting and its voice amplified by the force of the mass movement, blasted onto the international scene.  Its magnum opus on the first year of the uprising, Punishing a Nation, became a standard reference work for human rights groups, journalists and policy makers alike.  Holly Burkhalter, an early admirer of Al-Haq who as advocacy director of Human Rights Watch oversaw the production and distribution of the U.S. edition of Punishing a Nation, today sees the significance of Al-Haq as twofold: There werent at that time that many groups in the South that were doing highly sophisticated human rights investigations, she says.  The movement, even in the West, was very young.  To have that kind of quality of documentation, especially from Palestinians, was very important in dispelling the prejudice that existed.  In the United States a Palestinian was an adjective modifying the noun terrorist.  Al-Haqs work was essential in getting out the truth not only about human rights violations in the Occupied Territories, but about the Palestinians themselves.
 

That was then and this is now.  As the intifada turned inward and petered out, Al-Haq lost much of its audience and its steam, while it also began to pay the price of too rapid and uncontrolled a growth in the late 1980s.  The 1993 Oslo Accords and the arrival of the Palestinian Authority further threw the organization into confusion, and except for pursuing the brilliant innovation that was its human rights enforcement project, it was forced to spend the better part of the 1990s trying to regain its footing.  Today, as it marks its twentieth anniversary, Al-Haq is a transformed organization, a different beast altogether, set to mark a course in tune with the requirements of the new situation prevailing in Palestine.
 

To me Al-Haq will always be the Al-Haq that I knew intimately then, the Al-Haq that gave me my best friends, that taught me my passable Arabic and gave me insight and an entrée into Palestinian society, and most importantly that showed me the power of human rights.  My five years in Palestine for me were a rite of passage, my formative years, the threshold that separates the before from the after in my life.  Working for Al-Haq was frustrating at times the infuriating but indispensable legalese of its analysis; the primary institutional (elite) focus of its activism.  It often forced me to step outside and engage in extracurricular activities: writing articles for magazines in the U.S., Britain and the Netherlands, traveling throughout the West Bank to document house demolitions while delivering basic necessities to the newly homeless, and making friends among the people who were most affected by the daily depredations of the occupier

all of this on top of research and writing relating to my doctoral studies.  But my experience at Al-Haq never was anything but authentic, full of meaning, extraordinary.
When I left Al-Haq in 1990 it occupied a large office across the street from the old one, with two dozen well-trained staff, plenty of computers, and its spirit unbent despite the never-ending misery it had mandated itself to document in towns, villages and refugee camps.  I sometimes now recall, at night when my children have gone to sleep and I have a moment to myself, the sounds that uniquely evoke for me my privileged existence in what was my home for five matchless years: the constant roar of the bulldozers razing dwellings and trees to make way for new settlements, the angry bark of soldiers breaking open stores on strike, and the wailing of mothers newly bereaved.  But also, amidst the din, distinctly and unmistakably, the steadfast and rhythmic tap tap tap of the keyboards at Al-Haq.

An International Conference on the Administration of Occupied Territories and International Law

By Emma Playfair, Executive Director of Interights, The International Centre for the Protection f Human Righhts

On 22 to 25 January 1988, in the unlikely context of the first month of the Palestinian intifada, or popular uprising, Al-Haq held an international conference in East Jerusalem, convening some of the foremost world experts in the international laws of war and human rights. This was the first international conference of its kind to be organized within the Occupied Territories. Most of those attending were not supporters of the Palestinian cause but scholars who had, until then, had little contact with the Arab-Israeli conflict. They came to provide independent opinions on the international laws governing the conduct of an occupying power over the people and territories it controlled, and to review Israels administration of the West Bank and Gaza, including East Jerusalem, against international law.
 

By 1987, Israel's occupation of the West Bank and Gaza was entering its third decade, with little sign of an end. Over the previous five years, Al-Haq had established a solid reputation for its meticulous reporting of the human rights violations that formed part of the daily life of Palestinians in the Occupied Territories. As well as intervening on individual violations - either directly or by seeking the intervention of others  it had published a series of reports on such practices as deportation, administrative detention, torture, demolition and sealing of houses and separation of families. These interventions and reports were based on extensive fieldwork and always supported by carefully researched legal arguments as to why these acts constituted violations of human rights or humanitarian law. The clarity and constraint of the reports was hard to contest and contributed to a shift in international public opinion that had generally subscribed to Israel's projected image as a benevolent occupier respectful of the laws of occupation.
 

While Al-Haq had to address such violations on a daily basis, it was all too aware of the longer term threat to Palestinian rights posed by the steady transformation of the land and infrastructure of the territories themselves, altering, sometimes irrevocably, their character and integrity. Israel was introducing changes to almost every element of Palestinian life  to the economic structure, the judicial system, local government, systems of transportation, water, electricity and, most of all, to ownership and use of land. While some such changes were undoubtedly necessary with the passage of time, many appeared to be aimed at ensuring dependency on Israel or access by Israel to Palestinian resources and markets, or at serving the interests of the Israeli population being settled illegally in the Territories, rather than at serving the long-term interests of the Palestinian peoples future after the end of the occupation.
 

It is an underlying tenet of the international law governing military occupation that occupation is an inherently temporary state and that the status quo should be observed, with only those changes which are necessary for the security of the occupying forces or in the best interests of the people, so that the ability of the territories ultimately to regain their independence is not jeopardized. Al-Haq carefully documented the changes made by Israel and argued that many could not be justified on the limited grounds allowed by international law. It demonstrated further that they had profound and adverse implications for the Palestinian governance of the territories after the end of the occupation, and for the ability of the Palestinian community to exercise the fundamental human right of self-determination. Israel argued, however, that it respected the rules of international law governing its occupation, or 'administration' as it preferred to call it, but that the occupation was sui generis by reason of its length amongst other things. It relied on this firstly to argue that the main body of law relating to belligerent occupation, the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949, was not applicable, and secondly to justify its own interpretations of the applicable laws, often at variance with earlier interpretations.
 

The difficulty faced by Al-Haq in countering this claim was that the terms of the Hague Regulations of 1907 and the Fourth Geneva Convention are necessarily framed as general principles, in order to cover all forms of war. While certain provisions, such as those relating to destruction of property, deportation of peoples and treatment of prisoners, are quite clear, there are few details in the guidelines as to how the territories themselves should be governed. For this, authoritative interpretation was needed, based not only on those laws, but on the military manuals drawn up in previous wars, and on such legal precedents as the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials that followed the Second World War. Expertise was hard to find though, since the laws of war were at that time viewed as an obscure branch of the law.(1) Belligerent occupation in particular had generated little attention outside Israel since the end of the second world war, so that Israeli scholars, judges and officials, much of whose expertise was based on service within Israel's army or administration, dominated the field.
 

In the absence of independent scholarship on these issues, Al-Haq decided to seek the advice of leading scholars of the laws of war, international law and international human rights law to bring their expertise to bear on key issues relating to the administration of occupied territories. We recognized that for most this would require original research, since few had considered this issue before, but we counted on being able to stimulate the interest of academics to apply their learning to a concrete situation. We therefore designed a research project, to culminate in an international conference, through which international legal scholars, carefully selected for their independence, and in most cases without prior involvement in the conflict, would provide reasoned opinions on the current interpretations of the international laws of war and of human rights as they applied to Israel's occupation and treatment of the Occupied Territories. The legal opinions had to be based on the actual situation, but published sources on these issues are few so the international experts would be matched with local scholars and experts who would document and analyze the factual issues from within the Occupied Territories, often also requiring original research.
 

We deliberately avoided issues which normally attracted the attention of observers, in favor of the more subtle aspects of territorial, administrative and infrastructural transformation which were more complex to document and on which legal sources were few, given the rarity of long occupations. The treatment of natural resources, the power to appropriate and change usage of land, the taxation of the people, the reshaping of the legal system, the integration of the economy with that of the occupying power, the settlement of the occupiers own population in the occupied territories, the separate administrative and legal structure applied to those settlers, and the application of the various sources of humanitarian law and human rights law governing the conduct of a belligerent occupier - these issues, which were steadily eroding the potential of the West Bank and Gaza to be self-sustaining, formed the object of our inquiry.
 

Identification of leading scholars involved extensive research and consultation, and then often careful persuasion. Some were cautious about involvement in what they perceived as a political minefield, and needed reassurance about Al-Haq's credentials, as to the serious nature of the initiative and that they were genuinely being asked to voice independent opinions. International participants included Gerhard von Glahn, author of the leading work on belligerent occupation, Adam Roberts, one of the foremost scholars of the laws of war, Antonio Cassese, later President of the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia, John Dugard, an expert in human rights law from South Africa, and other eminent experts in public international law and human rights law.
 

On 9 December 1987, one month before the conference was to be held, the intifada broke out in Gaza. By the time the conference started, on 22 January 1988, the intifada had spread to all parts of the Occupied Territories. 41 Palestinians had been killed, almost half of them below the age of 18. Despite powerful press coverage of the rapid escalation of violence, none of the participants withdrew. They did, however, express concern at discussing apparently abstract questions of law while people died outside. It became quickly apparent though that the questions under discussion, however arcane, were at the heart of the intifada. It was precisely because of the actions Israel was taking in the name of the law  the expropriations of land, the annexing of Jerusalem, the absorption of the occupied territories into the occupiers state  denying a future to the people, that years of pent-up frustration exploded into the intifada. Through meetings and visits, arranged between sessions, the participants were able to witness both the causes of the intifada and its violent repression by the Israeli authorities.
 

The project had three elements. The first was to clarify the framework of international law relating to belligerent occupation, particularly the application of the Hague Regulations and the Geneva Convention, and the applicability of human rights law in times of occupation. The second element was to examine particular aspects of administration of occupied territories, namely the form an administration should take, the right of association under occupation reflecting peoples ability to participate in the administration, taxation, and the powers and duties of an occupier in relation to land. The final aim of the project was to consider methods of enforcement of humanitarian and human rights law, both through law and by mobilizing public opinion and requiring action.
 

Participants rejected resoundingly Israel's contention that the Fourth Geneva Convention does not apply to its occupation. The inherently temporary nature of occupation was emphasized again and again, as was the illegality of any steps taken that irreversibly alter the character of the territories occupied, except within well-defined situations of necessity, or which seek to assert sovereignty or to pre-empt eventual withdrawal from the territories occupied. Far from confirming Israel's contention that the length of the occupation permitted it to override these provisions, participants agreed that the longer the occupation, the more important it was to hold firmly to the provisions and to interpret them restrictively. Participants also confirmed that human rights law applies, with certain exceptions, even under occupation, supplementing the provisions of humanitarian law. Both international and national participants criticized, to varying degrees, the Israeli High Courts rulings and expressed disappointment at its role. Views differed on important points  for instance the extent to which the occupation itself is illegal (some felt that it was, while others felt that international law does not yet support this point), the weight that an occupier can attach to military necessity, and the legitimacy of a levée en masse, as an expression of will of the people.
 

Immediately after the conference, Al-Haq had necessarily to devote its attention very substantially to addressing the daily emergencies and gross violations occurring during the intifada, which continued to stretch its capacity to the limit for a number of years. While Al-Haqs Enforcement Project, develop at the same time as the Conference, drew on and developed the arguments discussed at the conference, we were not otherwise able to devote the resources needed to develop a systematic challenge to those aspects of Israeli policy which the conference had shown to be clearly violative of international law, as planned. However, the publication of the papers by a respected legal publisher ensured that the findings of the conference reached a wide audience both within Palestine and Israel, and in the international legal community.
 

The conference exemplified Al-Haqs approach, which was that of an activist organization, basing its work only on the rule of law and on internationally established standards of human rights and humanitarian law, employing the strictest professional and independent standards. Arising from Al-Haqs frustration at the lack of available independent expertise, which hindered its ability to address serious violations of human rights, the project typically relied on Al-Haqs fieldwork and brought to bear both international and local expertise to seek clarification of the crucial provisions of international law relating to a belligerent occupation.
 

A decade after the conference, its findings remain relevant not only to those seeking to address ongoing human rights violations in the Palestinian territories, but also to civilians caught in other situations of occupation or conflict. The published views of legal experts are an important source of international and customary law, so that the expert contributions to this conference will directly contribute to the development and interpretation of humanitarian law. We hope also that the conclusions of the international and local experts, documenting the effects of the illegal measures and clearly establishing the violations of international law, can be of use in negotiations towards an end to the Israeli occupation, which international law requires, and in the determination of restitution and compensation.

The Enforcement Project

By Dr. Lynn Welchman, Director of the Centre of Islamic and Middle Eastern Law, SOAS, University of London
 

Years after the enforcement project started at Al-Haq, I was having a conversation with a prominent human rights lawyer about universal jurisdiction and the deterrent potential of prosecutions, against the background of the Rwanda and former Yugoslavia tribunals.  I brought up (as one does, especially if one has worked at Al-Haq) the issue of grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions and she looked at me and said yes, you lot at Al-Haq were really ahead of your time.  It was, I felt, a well-deserved tribute to the organization, its founders, and the people who worked there. The vision of Al-Haq was so fresh and innovative, in its time and context, that for years after its establishment you still found yourself having to explain it - and often defend it - both in Palestine and abroad.  This was also the case with the enforcement project, a project born and nurtured at Al-Haq which, despite developing into a difficult child, provided a source of innovative argument and advocacy that built on Al-Haqs core strengths to take the challenge of enforcement out to those properly charged with it under the law.
 

The founder of the enforcement project was of course Charles Shammas, one of the original founders of Al-Haq.  It was Charles who recruited me to Al-Haq in Ramallah in the early months of 1983 when, after a rather unnerving interview, I found myself seated for the first time ever behind a computer (very large and complete with all sorts of suspiciously whirring noises) working on the database project.  These were very early days: Al-Haq had at that time no telephone (we would run up Main Street to Raja Shehadahs office if we needed to use one) and not very good wiring: the computer in particular was sensitive to electricity dips and I remember several occasions realizing someone was about to enter the bathroom and yelling dont turn the light on... just too late to save my day.  Also imprinted in my memory is the first time the computer faced me (and my life) with those two most chilling of words: FATAL ERROR.  It still makes me shudder.
 

By the time the enforcement project got up and running, Al-Haq had moved into the new office across the road and expanded its staff and its mandate considerably.  Preliminary work during 1987 showed a market (as Charles would, inevitably, term it, to the consternation of some of our colleagues) for the arguments and from then on, against the background of the intifada, the enforcement team pursued the arguments for international protection on the basis of third party states legal obligations.  These arguments proceeded from the fundamental premise that all High Contracting Parties to the Fourth Geneva Convention Relative to the Protection of Civilian Persons in Time of War have a direct duty under law towards the protected population of occupied territories.  This is through the inclusion in article 1 common to all four Geneva Conventions of the inter-state duty to ensure not only that a states own agents and institutions respect the terms of the Conventions, but also that other state signatories respect them in all circumstances. This duty distinguishes the position of the international community of states from the position it generally seeks to adopt vis-à-vis human rights violations that occur within the sovereign territory of another state (with certain exceptions).  This duty to respect and to ensure respect for the Convention is developed in Article 146 of the Fourth Geneva Convention, which creates an obligation to prosecute individuals of any nationality responsible for  committing grave breaches of the Convention (that is to say, war crimes, listed in Article 147).  In 1977, the duty was further developed in Article 89 of the First Additional Protocol to the Geneva Conventions by mandating HCPs to act individually and jointly in cases of serious violations of the Convention or the Protocol, in cooperation with the United Nations and in conformity with the UN Charter.
 

The members of Al-Haqs enforcement team were mandated by the organization to dedicate their work time to developing these and related legal arguments, articulating the thinking behind them, including the link between durable conflict resolution and the prohibition under the law of predatory and annexationist conduct and related violations on the part of an Occupying Power, and identifying and exploring ways and means available to powerful third party states to discharge this obligation as effectively as possible.  It was the difference between having states assert their right to comment on Israels behavior in the Occupied Territories, and having them move towards statements and actions that acknowledged a legal duty to uphold the protections of international humanitarian law in the face of the refusal of successive Israel governments to recognize the de jure applicability of the Convention and to behave accordingly.
 

Enforcement work took members of the team to Brussels and Strasbourg to meet with MEPs and members of the European Commission, producing painstakingly detailed briefings on the law and the instruments controlled by European states and the then Community through which they might fulfil their obligations.  In national contexts in Europe the team worked with domestic non-governmental organizations and parliamentarians to bring the arguments to the attention of their governments, with national symposia organized in London 1989, Dublin 1990, The Hague 1991 and London again 1992. The team worked with lawyers, academics, activists, international human rights organizations, development agencies, civil servants and politicians. Marc Stephens paper, published in 1989 as Enforcement of International Law in the Israeli-occupied Territories, provided us with an initial legal briefing; otherwise most of our writing was done in the form of targeted briefings and interventions to and often for particular third parties and in specific contexts. At home, the project became increasingly integrated into Al-Haqs main program, including contributing the final chapters to each of the second and third annual reports on human rights violations during the uprising (Nation under Siege and Protection Denied).  Enforcement arguments were incorporated into the body of Al-Haqs interventions, press releases and group briefings.
 

In pursuit of legal remedies, the enforcement team looked at setting up grave breaches files and sought advice from European lawyers as to what would be needed to activate the domestic legislation incorporating the grave breaches provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention.   The grave breaches of the Convention identified as such in the terms of the law itself (article 147) include unlawful deportation, willful killing, unlawful confinement, torture or inhuman treatment, and other crimes being carried out routinely by the Israeli occupation forces against the Palestinian population at that time.  Although quite a bit of interest was raised in the subject, it became clear (and remains so, despite recent developments and the growing consensus around the exercise of universal jurisdiction) that the legal remedies provided for under international humanitarian law -- in terms of third-state prosecutions for grave breaches -- were effectively not available for use in the protection of an occupied population unless you had real political will on your side from the state in which the evidence was going to be produced.  Even getting the right sort of evidence was going to be difficult for an  NGO; but it was important to take the prospect of legal remedy seriously.
 

Another instrument on which the project concentrated was the EUs preferential relations with Israel, on the basis that so long as Israel remained in systematic and grave violation of its existing commitments to the member states of the EU (as fellow High Contracting Parties to the Fourth Geneva Convention) there were serious obstacles to the EU considering the extension of further preferential terms to its relations with Israel.  This particular aspect of the work has new dimensions in the debate on the ratification in various EU member states of the EU-Israel Association Agreement with its human rights conditionality clause in article 2.
 

Much of the enforcement teams effort went into developing legal argument and seeking to have key actors acknowledge the implications of third party state obligations under international humanitarian law. This meant making the law and complex legal arguments meaningful to politicians and other non-lawyers, without engaging ourselves in politics.  It meant getting and keeping busy peoples attention on the strength of nothing but the rigor, integrity and innovative energy of the arguments.  In all this the enforcement team - which included many Al-Haqqers down the years - was grounded in and supported by the energy of the organization itself, with Al-Haqs core work providing the basis on which every argument was founded, and Al-Haqs vision setting the objectives.
 

There were landmarks along the way, including the Dublin Declaration of 1990, when the European Council for the first time referred to the article 1 obligation in a policy declaration on the Middle East; Security Council Resolution 681 of the same year, when the Council directly called for action in accordance with article 1 and endorsed the idea of convening a meeting of High Contracting Parties to discuss what measures might be taken under the Convention.  The idea of a conference was of course re-visited this summer.  Low points the same year, just by way of illustration, included the resolute refusal (continued till today) of powerful third parties to use the term grave breach when referring to Israels treatment of the Palestinian population protected by the Convention.  Just as, years later, the US was to hedge during crucial months on the use of the word genocide in relation to the massacres being carried out in Rwanda, so the legal implications of grave breaches were constrained to the UN resolutions regarding Iraqs conduct in Kuwait at this same time in 1990.
 

Like Al-Haq itself, and perhaps human rights workers everywhere, the enforcement project took a determinedly anti-cynical stand in the face of what were - and are - admittedly rather compelling challenges: its whole effort was premised on the possibility of getting influential third party states to move - incrementally and in ways that would serve as precedent - towards law-based enforcement of the protections enshrined in international humanitarian law, as a part of their foreign policy.  The point was to take the law at its word, to take its guarantors at their word, and to call them on it.  Without being grandiose, the whole of humanity, and sooner rather than later, has an interest in knowing that humanitarian law can and will work to protect those who have effectively no other protection.  That argument still has to be made, particularly to powerful states (specifically the EU and USA) whose record of stunning selectivity in matters of international law enforcement, combined with the weight of their power and influence, serves to undermine their own claims of universality of values, to the detriment of the struggle to promote and protect human rights around the world.
 

In Palestine, the fact that international humanitarian law has been so emphatically left out of the successive Israel-PLO agreements in the various agreements making up the Oslo Accords has presented particular obstacles to law-based action by key third party states. Since 1993, the continuation of the peace process itself -- on almost any terms, it seems -- has been the overriding political priority; and since Israel has presented any attempt to introduce the law into the equation as interference with a properly bilateral process, backed up in this by the US, the arguments for law-based enforcement action have been even harder to make than before. This summer, the debate on whether or not a conference of High Contracting Parties should be convened, as demanded by the General Assembly, to consider measures to enforce the Convention in the Occupied Palestinian Territory rekindled the chronic argument on whether the political imperative of supporting the Palestinian-Israeli peace process can be reconciled with the imperative to uphold the rule of law.  The result was, for the law, and arguably for the peace process also, not a felicitous one.
 

Whether, when the final settlement is formulated, its terms will show recognition of and deference to the regulatory framework of international humanitarian law; whether that same framework and other principles of international law will be recognized as properly governing matters of redress and reparation; in short whether, in the end, the law will finally come to the protection of the rights of the Palestinian population, albeit after the fact for many of them -- these are pressing and imminent enforcement-related questions that challenge Al-Haq and other human rights organizations today.  They will moreover stand as precedent for what the law can and cannot be made to do for those dependent upon its protection, both in Palestine and elsewhere.  These are pressing times for enforcement work.

Al-Haq and its Societal Programs: The Women's Rights Program

By Randa Seniora

"Go do something with your partner Amnesty International regarding arbitrary arrests, torture and ill treatment of Palestinian prisoners by the Israeli military occupation, and leave women's problems to be resolved privately and discretely by the extended Family".
 

This was the immediate response of a pioneering women's figure heading a charitable women's organization as I introduced to her the newly initiated Women's Human Rights Program at Al-Haq in 1987.  In her opinion, and in the opinion of many others within the Palestinian society at the time, the work of human rights organizations should only be focussed on human rights violations committed by the Israeli military occupation.  Human rights meant to them "political rights" but not "economic, social and cultural rights."  Such rights in their opinion should only be raised in the context of the Israeli violations of these rights.  Societal human rights violations committed against women and other marginalized groups within the family and the society were not seen as a priority and were kept at the very end of the Palestinian national and human rights agenda.


 When I joined Al-Haq in August 1987 as the coordinator of the Women's Rights Program, I was given free hand to develop the project as I saw
most suitable. No clear vision was still there: in no more than few words, I was told that "women's rights were human rights", and therefore "its
due time to integrate women's rights into human rights and see them as an integral part of human rights." It was obvious that the organization
was motivated by its strong commitment to the idea of human rights and its conviction of the interrelated and invisible relationship between
civil, political rights and economic social and cultural rights. But there was no clear vision as to how to transfer such an idea from theory into
practice; much has been left for future brainstorming within the organization and the initiative of the coordinator. 

Prior to that, there were two other societal programs started by Al-Haq; "The Labor Rights Program initiated in 1985 and the "Rights to
Development Program" initiated in 1986. All programs were structurally within the Research Unit of the Center. Despite that, coordinators of all
three programs although researchers themselves, had to strive for their projects within the unit and with other units of the organization. Being
under occupation meant that despite the significance of the issues raised, and despite realizing the impact of social on the political and visa
versa, monitoring and working on Israeli violations of Palestinian rights was given priority over any other work. This was even more so during
the Palestinian Intifada in December 1987. It was not until a couple of years later that we realized that the Intifada was a matter of everyday life
within the Palestinian society, and that we needed to focus on some problematic human rights issues that are not necessarily related to Israeli
violations of human rights. 

From the early nineties, Al-Haq's societal programs have witnessed a significant turning point. Much more attention was given to societal
problems. With regard to the Women's Rights Program, much more emphasis was given to violations of women's rights within the family and
society. Different projects were initiated. Some merely focussed on issues related to women within the Palestinian society, such as training and
legal literacy programs to highlight sexual discrimination in existing law' especially Family Status Law. While other projects focussed on the
impact of Israeli violations of human rights on women; such as the impact of Israeli policies related to family reunification, house demolition and
other violations of human rights on the lives of Palestinian women. Others were issues related to gender based violence; the impact of the
Intifada on the exacerbation of this problem within the Palestinian family. 

The early nineties have generally witnessed an important development within the Palestinian society. 
Both women's organizations and specialized professional women's centers started to address legal and social problems related to women. The
societal problems related to clear discrimination in existing laws and practice within the society meant that problems of marriage, divorce,
custody of children, the enforcement of the veil on women and violence cannot go unnoticed. Different professional organizations initiated
projects to address these issues. Al-Haq started to function within a Palestinian environment much more sensitive to women's human rights
issues.
The peak was the 1994 "Women, Justice and Law: Towards the Empowerment of Palestinian Women". The project aimed at initiating social
discussion within the Palestinian society on the legal and social status of Palestinian women. Although initiated by Al-Haq, the project was
carried out in collaboration with different women's organizations. Numerous workshops and discussions were undertaken in different places in
the West Bank and Gaza. The work culminated by an international conference held in September 1994, and plans of action were developed. 
Later such plans of action were adopted and implemented by other women's human rights organizations such as the Women's Center for Legal
Aid and Counseling. The political transformations within the Palestinian society after Oslo, and the transfer of some powers to the Palestinian
Authority, and the election of a Palestinian Legislative Council in 1996, has opened new options for Palestinian women. Today we see Al-Haq
and other human rights and women's rights organizations lobbying for legislation and policies based on equality, non-discrimination and
respect for women's human rights. 

Working with Al-Haq on the Women's Rights Program for over ten years has meant a lot to me. Al-Haq was the initiator of the idea of human
rights within the Palestinian society and in this sense was in and by itself a school for many of us who are currently active in the field of human
rights within the Palestinian society. It is there at Al-Haq where I started with a job but ended with a life-long commitment to the idea of human
rights.
