Sebastia

(Samaria)
Location:

The modern Palestinian village of Sebastia is located 10 km north west of Nablus. The ancient royal city (Sebastia or Samaria) lies in ruins at the hill overlooking the village from the west at an altitude of 430m above sea level. One can reach Sebastia from two directions via Nablus-Jenin road: preferably through the western gate which leads to the colonnaded street; or through the modern village by a road that leads to the forum. The city benefited from its location at the junction of two important ancient trade routes: the Nablus-North of Palestine route, and the Palestinian coast-Jordan valley route. It also benefited from the fertile plateaus around it. This strategic location has compensated the city for its scarce water resources.

History of the site:

Archaeological excavations at Sebastia indicate that it was first settled during the Chalcolithic period (4000 BC). It did not, however, gain political importance until 876 BC when King Omri built his royal city on it. He named the city Samaria after the man who sold him the hill. It is said that this man, shomer or Samer, sold the hill for two talents of silver. Samaria became the third capital of the northern kingdom of Israel after Tell Balata (Shechem) and Tell El Far'ah-north (Tirzah). The city gave its name to the kingdom and the region as a whole. During the reign of Urba'am II 784-748 BC, both the city and kingdom of Samaria flourished. According to the Old Testament the kingdom expanded greatly; its borders extended as far as Aleppo in the north and Aqaba in the south (II Kings 14:23-29).

In 722 BC, Sarjon II occupied the city and took a great number of its people captive (about 30,000 prisoners). This represented one third of the inhabitants of the region. He expelled them to Assur and replaced them by settlers loyal to him from the distant countries of the Assyrian Empire. Those banished people have come to be known in the Jewish traditions as the "Ten Lost Tribes."

The city was destroyed in 331 BC after been occupied by Alexander the Great. It was rebuilt as a Hellenistic city, and Macedonian soldiers settled there. Its inhabitants, the Samaritans, were expelled to Shechem (Nablus). The Hazmonian leader John Hercanus destroyed Samaria in 108 BC. After the Roman occupation of the city in 63 BC, the Roman Emperor Augustine gave it as a gift to Herod the Great, who rebuilt the city and renamed it Sebastia in honor of his lord Augustine. Herod lodged his followers, the atheists in the city, but it was destroyed again by the Jews during their revolt against Rome between 66 and 70 AD.

In an effort to counterbalance the pressure of Nablus, whose people were opposed to Rome, the Roman governor of Palestine, Sebtimus Severos, rehabilitated Sebastia in the second century AD, adding many new buildings to it. In 200 AD the city was granted the status of a Roman city and enjoyed the right to issue its own currency. After the spread of Christianity in Palestine and the Roman Empire during the forth century, the city started to weaken. It is thus possible to divide the history of Sabastia into the following periods:

Omri to the Assyrian conquest (876-724 BC)

This era is represented at the site by layers 1-6. The city in this period consisted of a castle at the top of the hill (Acropolis) which included the Royal Palace and its annexes. It was surrounded by a casemate wall 1.6m thick; this was the Upper city. The lower city spread on the western slopes of the hill. Archaeologists have not yet excavated the whole city which is believed to have extended from the western gate to the forum in the east;. it was approximately 800 meters long. Researchers do not agree on the surface area of the city at that time. While some believe that it did not exceed 18.5 dunums, others think that it had the same size of the Roman city (160 dunums).

Assyrian conquest to the Hellenistic period (724-63 BC.)

This is represented at the site by layers 7 and 8, including the remains of the Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, and the Hellenistic eras. During the Hellenistic era, the older walls were strengthened by circular towers. Excavations have revealed two of these towers near the south-western corner of the castle; they were wrongly dated to the Israelite period (Late Iron Age). The second excavation unveiled a third tower on the north-eastern side of the wall. Other parts of the Hellenistic wall, which was destroyed by John Herkanios, are still visible in the western gate. The towers of the gate were built on a square basis of Hellenistic time. It is possible that the Hellenistic gate of the city was here too.

The Roman Period (63 BC-324 AD)

The city reached its climax during the Roman period. Most of the remaining ruins of Sebastia date back to this period, particularly the reign of Herod the Great 37-44 BC. The city became an ideal Roman city, including all the components of a city: walls, gates, towers, collonaded streets, temples, a theater, a stadium, a Forum, Hippodrome, Basilca, etc.

History of archaeological research on the site:

The first expedition to the site was that of the Harvard University from 1908 to 1910, first under the direction of the German scholar G. Schumacher, and then the American archaeologist C.S. Fisher. This excavation concentrated on the top of the hill (Acropolis) and revealed the western part of the castle and the casemate walls dating back to the reign of king Omri (ninth century BC). It also exposed parts of the Roman wall, western gate, Hellenistic tower, Augustus temple, the Forum, the Basilica, and the theater as well as some private houses and manuscripts.

The second archaeological expedition to the site was the combined mission of the Harvard University, the Palestine Exploration Fund, the Hebrew University, and others under the supervision of G.M. Crawfot (1931-1935). This mission cleared the castle and discovered the collonaded street, the forum, and the stadium. The Curi temple, the theater, a group of Roman tombs, the Roman hydraulic system and the Byzantine church were also discovered.

Between 1965-1967, the Jordanian Department of Antiquities conducted a small excavation of the site. This was under the supervision of F. Zayadin. The work was concentrated in the region of the theater, the collonaded street, the western gate, and the temple of Augustus. A tomb of the Late Iron Age was discovered as well. In 1968, J.B. Hennessy conducted an excavation in the western part of the hill. He discovered several layers of Hellenistic and Roman ages. Archaeologists faced real problems in distinguishing the different layers at Sebastiah. This is due to the fact that the city was destroyed several times. Builders often destroyed existing buildings and used their materials in new ones; they deepened the foundations to reach bedrock. As a result, things are currently mixed up at the site.

Description of the site

The Western Gate And The City Wall

The first thing that attracts the attention of the visitors of the city, when approaching the city from the western gate, is the gate itself. It is a wide door supported by two circular towers. The diameter of each is 14 meters. These were built in the Herodion period. They were built on a square base dating to Hellenistic time. These towers are still standing at a height of 8-11m. The city wall that we see today was built around the end of the second or the beginning of the third century BC. It is irregular and supported by a number of circular towers. The wall is 1 km long from east to west and a little less from north to south. Only a small part of it is still standing; this is because a lot of the stones of the wall were reused in the construction of the Roman city of Nablus in first century AD.

The Collonaded Street

The collonaded Street starts at the western gate; its width at this point is 12.5m and decreases progressively to the end. It dates back to the end of the second century and the beginning of the third century AD. It extends to a distance of 800m in an eastern direction. It extends at the eastern gate and the public yard. Six hundred stony pillars stand on both sides of the street. The height of each, with the crown, is 5.5m. Only two of these crowns are found in the site, whereas the rest of the crowns have disappeared. At one time, these pillars carried a roof. Two rows of shops, on both sides of the street, served as a market in the Roman era.

The internal plan of the city does not differ from any other Roman city. It consists of straight parallel streets, branching from a public yard. The city includes the following public buildings:

  • The Forum

Via the pillars street, the visitor can reach the forum. Its dimensions are 128x72.5m. It is used today as a parking lot for visitors It includes a number of venires shops and tourist restaurants. Some of its foundations are built on the rocks at a depth of 4-6m. These were leveled by artificial fillings supported by giant terraces. The forum is closed from the four sides by arches. It was originally surrounded by pillars. Nowadays, only seven out of twenty-four pillars of the western side are left. The rest of the pillars disappeared, and only their bases remained. At the western wall, there is a door leading to the Basilca which is connected with the forum.

  • The Basilca

The archeologist did not unveil the totality of the Basilca. However, its plan is clear. It is 68 m long, and its width is 32.6m. Three rows of pillars divide the Basilca into a yard and vestibules. Some of these pillars are still found, but most of it consist only of the bases. The height of the pillars is 6 m. Two of them bear Coronation Crowns. On the northern side of the Basilca exists the altar, crook, and four chairs. Most of the exciting seen remnants belong to the second century after Christ There are also remnants of Herodion time. There is a canal flowing under the southern extremity of the wall, beside the Basilca, and is used to bring water to the city. In earlier periods, the city depended on the rainfall harvest in the canals and cisterns.

  • The Theater

Near the northern end of the Basilca, there is a narrow earthy path leading to the Roman theater. It is a small theater of 65 m in diameter. Its lower part contains 14 rows of desks. It is divided into six sections of desks making seven groups of assemblies. The theater was built in the first quarter of the third century AD.

  • The Hellenistic Tower

At the eastern side of the theater, there lies a magnificent tower that was built in the Alexander era. Its diameter is 13 m and is still standing in a good condition at a height of 8.5 m. It is formed of 19 well built blocks. It is totally made of corner stones. Perhaps, it is the greatest ruin left from the Hellenic era (the end of the 4th century BC) in Palestine.

  • Augustine's Temple

Near the eastern end of the theater, an earthy road ascends in a western direction, to reach Augustine Temple and Amri Castle, on the summit that overlooks the whole city and the Mediterranean Sea. There the great temple, which was built by Herod honoring and commemorating the Roman emperor Augustine, stands. The temple is composed of a large front yard and a building of 35 x 24 m in dimension. This yard is leveled and its dimensions are: 83 x 72 m. Both the building and the yard are found in north-south orientation. The outer northern wall is still standing at a height of 7 m. Its original height was about 15 m. There is a wide stair leading to the front yard of the temple which stands at a height at about 4.4 m above the yard.

It is possible to enter the temple through a loggia. The foundations of the temple were built on rocks at a depth of 9 m. What is left from the walls of the temple indicates that it was built in the Herodion period. The remaining stones in the walls are traditional Herodion stones with protruding margins at the outer faces. The temple is divided into a grand hall and two pillared aisles. In the second century AD, the temple was rebuilt according the original plan. However, there have been some extension added to the length. The stairs that are found to clay and the alter were also added. Beside the altar exists the base of giant statue. Perhaps that was for the Emperor Augustine.

  • Curi Temple

To the North of the Augustine Temple, a temple for the goddess Curi was built during the Herodion period. Its dimensions are: 36 x 15.5 m. with its base dimensions at 84 x 45 m. Perhaps, it was surrounded by pillars. Only the foundations of this temple were unveiled. This was destroyed by John Hercanus and was built on the ruins of an earlier temple belonging to the third century BC. It was dedicated the Greek goddess Isis.

  • The Fortress Palace

A few meters to the South of the Augustine temple appears the walls of the Castle Palace. This belongs to the period of King Amri and his son Ihab. It is believed that this temple was specified for the worship of the Canaanite's gods Baa'l and Ishtarot. According to the Torah, Ihab built this temple to content his Phoenician wife Jezebel.

The prospectors have discovered, in the zone of the palace, a statue for the Phoenician goddess Ishtarot. The castle and the palace were built of refined stones, a skill the Samaritans learnt to do from the Phoenicians.

Also a beautiful group of ivory sculptures and manuscripts were discovered at this site.

  • The Stadium

The stadium is found in the north eastern part of the city. Some of its pillars appeared before the first excavations had started. One of its buildings belongs to the Herodion ages while the second is related to the second century. It is built in the Corinthian style. Near the stadium, a statue for the god Kori and a manuscript written in Greek were discovered.

Other Discoveries at the Site

Few ruins from the proceeding periods exist. There is a church in the lower city, and another subcity in the upper section on the southern part of the hill. The church, existing in the main yard of the present village, was built during the Crusader's time at about 1160 AD. This was converted to a mosque in the time of Saladin. It is known today as Al-Nabi Yahia mosque, and this is in reference to John the Baptist who is thought to be buried here. The church on the hill is also named after him. It is believed that his head was hidden in this church. The church also dates to the Crusaders period and stands on the ruins of a Basilica church belonging to the Byzantine era. It consists of a main hall and two pillared vaults on the left and right sides.

Numerous coins have been discovered in Sabastia. Most of these belong to the period of Batlimos-3rd century BC and to the Siloqian period in the second century. Other coins date to the Hezmonian and Herodion era-1st century AD. Coins that were issued by Sebastia itself in the Roman era were also unveiled, in addition to many imported pottery belonging to Herodion and other periods. In addition to many imported pottery belonging to Herodion and other periods, the discoveries include Byzantine, Arabic, and Crusaders coins.

The Tombs

In the southern corner of Sabastia village, there is a Roman cemetery belonging to the end of the second and the beginning of the third century. It is sculptured in the rock and adorned externally by plaster. Other sarcophaguses were also discovered. In the eastern part of the village, Roman tombs were also discovered. In these tombs numerous glass utensils and jewelry were found. Potter utensils, particularly lamps belonging to the 2nd century which continued to be used in the 3rd century, were discovered at the site.

Actual Situation Of The Site

In spite of the negligence that affected Sabastia during the long Israeli occupation, the site remains highly attractive. It is one of the biggest archeological sites in the West Bank. In contrast to its potential and its beauty, its visitors are few. However, its capacities to attract tourism is very important. What it lacks is the development of the available services. Also, provision of pamphlets and programs that encourage visiting the site need to be created. This brochure is a trial in that direction. No doubt, there is a need to ensure the protection for the site to prevent the abuse of the treasures of this archeological site. It may be necessary to apply reasonable entrance fees that will help develop the level of services at the site.
