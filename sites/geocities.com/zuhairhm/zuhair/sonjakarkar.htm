AUSTRALIANS FOR PALESTINE   WOMEN FOR PALESTINE

                      MELBOURNE – AUSTRALIA

 

 

 

PRESENTATION 

Friends of Nazareth/Doncaster Church of Christ

Churches of Christ Theological College, Mulgrave

 

by SONJA KARKAR

Women for Palestine/Australians for Palestine

 

11 October 2008

 

 

 

About 130 people came to a function at the Churches of Christ College in aid

of the Nazareth Work Party led by Dr Paul and Rev Merrill Kitchen AO, which

will see a group of Australians go on a “travel, work and learning” trip to

the Holy Land in December this year.  Their mission is to help re-paint

Nazareth Hospital and the Mar Elias educational institution in Israel, but

also to see what life is like for the Palestinians living under occupation

in some West Bank towns like Bethlehem.  Many of the participants have only

a rudimentary knowledge of the history that has seen the Palestinians

dispossessed of their land and deprived of their freedom.

 

Dr Paul Kitchen thought that an evening where people are brought together

for some wonderful Arabic food, music and even belly-dancing would be

enhanced by getting to know some of the Palestinians living here.  It

was also a way of bringing a captive audience to hear a presentation about

Palestinians and their long and uninterrupted connection to the Holy Land,

the catastrophic events that has seen the fragmentation of their society

over the last sixty years, and how Australians can help to raise awareness

about their predicament.

 

I was asked to give a presentation that would help people better understand

the situation, and so, on behalf of Australians for Palestine and Women for

Palestine, I prepared a lightning overview of some 5000 years of history

under the title From Canaan to Palestine and the new via Dolorosa. 

As I told the audience, if we don’t understand the history of this place, we

cannot possibly understand the terrible injustice that occurred in the 20th

century creating a divided, tortured land, frozen cities and shadow people

whose existence the world barely recognises. It is the “new via Dolorosa” -

a term coined by Palestinian Christian Samia Khoury from the Sabeel Centre

in Jerusalem – that is the path of suffering that saw Jesus die for our sins

now extended out and beyond the Holy City into all of Palestine.

 

The presentation is divided into three parts:

·     Historical overview

·     Spotlight on two Christian Palestinian towns – Nazareth inside Israel and

      Beit Jala in the Bethlehem Governorate of the Israeli occupied West Bank

·     How people are helping Palestinians

 

The following is text is accompanied by just some of the slides that were

shown in the presentation.

 

 

HISTORICAL OVERVIEW

 

Beginning with “time immemorial”, I presented the rarely heard narrative

that new archaeological discoveries have gradually pieced together showing a

continuous Palestinian lineage to the Canaanites who lived in that part of

the world long before the ancient Hebrews came there. In fact, Palestinian

embroidery today still incorporates Canaanitic symbols and in many of the

northern villages, there are Palestinians who carry the name, Cana’an.

 

 

 

 

The land and its people saw a constant influx of populations – Assyrians,

Babylonians, Persians, Egyptians, Philistines, Hittites, Greeks, Arabs,

Armenians - all of whom contributed to Palestine’s remarkable civilisation,

and as Professor Edward Said observed  “centuries before the first Hebrew

tribes migrated to the area.”

 

Contrary to what has been popularly espoused, the Hebrews did not arrive

until 1800BC and it would be another 800 years before the Hebrews formed a

United Kingdom of Israel.  It was, therefore, only to be expected that in

that expansive time frame, there would have been assimilation, intermarriage

and various beliefs - Judaism included –taken up by the people of the land.

 

The Canaanites were still there under the rules of Israelite King David

and then King Solomon whose reigns spanned a mere 73 years, and they were

there when that kingdom split into two tiny states – Israel and Judah.  But,

by the time Jesus was born and had begun his ministry, the land had seen

many more waves of people come and go and empires rise and fall, that

everything we have been told of Israel’s ancient history is best described

by Professor Keith Whitelam as “a moment in the vast expanse of Palestinian

history.”

 

Under the Romans, the land became known as Palestine. Pagan beliefs were

rife, but some Canaanites had also adopted Judaism in its various forms,

that is, until Jesus’ message brought a new way of thinking that saw many of

them become the first Christians. This would again change in the 7th century

when the Arab Islamic Empire reached Jerusalem and the people embraced

Islam.  Such was the melting pot, that Jewish communities and a strong

Christian community existed harmoniously under the umbrella of Islam for

more then 1200 years, which belies the inter-religious hostilities being

stoked in the current conflict.

 

Moving quickly to the 20th century, the Palestinians suddenly found

themselves in real jeopardy. Never before in millennia past, had their very

existence been threatened as it was when the Western world decided to

partition their land in 1947 in favour of European Jews whose only

connection to the land came from the stories familiar to us from the Bible.

“It is important to realise,” said Edward Said, “that not until the few

weeks immediately preceding the establishment of Israel in the spring of

1948 was there ever anything other than a huge Arab majority.”

 

Is it any wonder then that initial disbelief was followed by absolute

outrage when the Palestinians whose ancestry was rooted in the land, were

forced to give up more than half of their country to foreigners? The idea

that an exclusivist ideology formed by a group of Zionists in Europe decades

earlier could persuade the British government to sign off on a homeland for

the Jews in an already populated land was so preposterous that few believed

it could succeed, and yet that is precisely what happened. By the end of WW2

and the ghastly revelations of the Holocaust, a guilt-stricken Western world

was ready to push through the partition plan in the United Nations that

would actually see Palestine carved up without any consideration for the

people, their society or their heritage.

 

 

 

The stroke of the pen though was not enough to push through the Zionist

agenda, not when the Palestinians were resisting this takeover.  Already

Zionist gangs had been executing terror campaigns  and    Other methods had

to be employed and campaigns of terror, and by the time the Arab-Israeli war

erupted, more than 750,000 Palestinians had already been forced to flee home

and country to live as refugees until the present day.  None of them have

been allowed to return to their homes or have ever been compensated contrary

to all the tenets of international law.

 

To illustrate the enormity of the crimes committed, Sonja gave examples of

the Palestinian cities that will be visited by the Australian work party –

Nazareth and Bethlehem/Beit Jala – affected by the Partition and Israel’s

war.  The former now part of Israel and the latter under Israel’s military

occupation. One, where daily life has the veneer of normality, and the

other, where realities on the ground clearly show the effects of an

unrelenting and escalating military occupation. While these cities have

considerable religious significance to Christians around the world, Sonja

emphasised that their trials are by no means the whole story or even the

most tragic.

 

 

 

 

 

NAZARETH

 

Nazareth of 1948 was an all-Arab town of some 15,000 people more than 60

percent of whom were Christian and the rest Muslim.  It had been assigned to

the Arab part of the divided land, but during the Arab-Israeli war, it fell

under Israeli domination.  Unlike the devastation wrought in other towns and

villages, Israel decided not to decimate Nazareth because it knew that if it

destroyed any of Nazareth’s Christian educational and medical institutions

it would anger the powerful churches that had funded them from abroad and

bring them international condemnation.

 

So Nazareth and its population were spared, but thousands of displaced and

fleeing Palestinians from the Galilee region poured into Nazareth as the

full force of Israeli occupation was unleashed.  It was a chaotic situation

for which no one was prepared, but then no one believed that they would be

permanently displaced from their homes until some of the Palestinians tried

to go back and found that their villages had been razed and people

massacred, and in many other cases, Jewish families living in their homes.

 

It is difficult to convey the enormity of the crimes carried out. 750,000

Palestinians were driven from their homes and lands and only 160,000

remained in that part of Palestine that was made Israel.  They became  the

Arab minority in the new Jewish state.

 

 

 

 

 

Suddenly, they were strangers in their own land.  Not only did Israel

confiscate their private property and agricultural land, it brought in laws

and regulations that restricted their movement, classified them as absentee

even if they had never left the country, refused them automatic citizenship

while Jews of any nationality were granted this unconditionally and

discriminated against them in employment, education, health services and

political representations.  Worse still, no one felt that they could stand

up for their rights for fear of being informed on, much like what happens in

a police state, and it certainly put a stop to any kind of political dissent

in those early years.

 

Today, only 12% of the Palestinian population is Christian and there are

suggestions that they are being driven out by Muslims.  However, if Muslims

had really wanted to quash Christianity, they could have done it long ago. 

Instead, under centuries of Islamic rule, Christians and Jews enjoyed

complete freedom to practice their beliefs and lived together in harmony and

mutual respect.

 

It is very important to understand this in any discourse or even when

talking about peace and reconciliation. It is Israel’s occupation that has

created a climate of fear, distrust and hostility and it is Israel’s

occupation that must end for the good of all people  - Christian, Muslim or

Jewish.  The Palestinians in Israel are second-class citizens in a state

that has so-called “normalised” their status while in actual fact there is

nothing normal about their existence.

 

The tenuous situation can best be described by a true story told by Merrill

at a Women for Palestine forum in 2002.

 

A Muslim taxi driver had found an Israeli soldier shot on the roadside and

he put him bleeding in his taxi and brought him to Nazareth hospital.  That

very humane act had repercussions unthinkable in any normal society.   The

doctors and nurses – who are wonderful examples of how Christians, Muslims

and Jews can work together peaceably - were petrified to operate in case the

soldier died. However, if they did not operate, he would die and if they did

operate he could still die and if that happened, the reputation of Nazareth

Hospital would come under suspicion and possibly face grave danger. If they

turned the taxi driver away with the dying soldier, the taxi driver would be

blamed for killing him and he would have no recourse to a fair hearing. 

Morally and ethically of course the hospital could not refuse to take in the

soldier.

 

The story does have a happy ending – the soldier lived and a friendship

developed with the taxi driver.  But the implications behind this story show

just how precarious life is for the Palestinians even in what appears to be

the “normal” city of Nazareth inside Israel.

 

 

 

BEIT JALA – Bethlehem Governorate

 

The trip to Bethlehem from Nazareth is relatively easy if one is travelling

inside Israel, but a nightmare if one wants to get there from any of the

Palestinian towns inside the Occupied West Bank.  That route really is

today’s via Dolorosa endured by some 3 million Palestinians.   But even

approaching the Bethlehem Governorate from Israel, the first signs off

occupation are clearly evident – checkpoints, watchtowers, razor wire and

Israeli soldiers.

 

Beit Jala is an ancient Canaanite city overlooking Bethlehem from the slopes

of one of the Judean Hills and has been particularly targeted by Israel

because it lies in close proximity to Jerusalem and the Green Line which

separates the occupied West Bank from Israel.  Moreover, two Israeli

settlements have been built on land belonging to Beit Jala – Gilo and Har

Gilo and these form part of the ring of settlements on the hilltops dividing

Bethlehem from Jerusalem.

 

 

 

The Palestinians of Beit Jala have been there for centuries – a once rich

and vibrant society in a thriving centre of Christianity.  Today the picture

is somewhat different.  Twenty-two percent of Beit Jala’s agricultural land

was confiscated by Israel in 1967 for the illegal Israeli settlements and

this land theft continues as Israel builds the Wall without any regard for

people’s homes, farmlands, businesses or even the historical heritage of the

place.

 

Nowhere is Israel’s Separation Wall more overwhelmingly ominous than in

Bethlehem and the two towns on either side – Beit Jala and Beit Sahour.   I

saw it for myself and was shocked by the sheer enormity of its height and

its purpose. It runs across 11.7km of Beit Jala lands and when finished, it

will isolate 45% of agricultural lands from the farmers on the other side. 

In other words, it separates Palestinians from Palestinians, and not as

Israel contends, Palestinians from Israelis for security reasons.

 

 

 

Thousands of bullets and bombs have landed on Beit Jala because Israel

claims they have to defend Gilo from attacks.  We have to remember that Gilo

has been illegally built on Palestinian land for a specific purpose.  It is

not a friendly neighbourhood, but part of a strategic government plan to

surround and divide Palestinian towns and villages.  Some 31,500 foreigners

were encouraged to take up residence there while the population of Beit Jala

according to last year’s statistics has dwindled to 16,700 people.

 

 

 

I say a strategic plan because in June 2004, Israel announced a plan for

another settlement to be built on Beit Jala lands called Giv’at Ya’el and

since then the land has been stripped of its trees to make way for the

20,000 housing units intended to accommodate another 55,000 incoming Israeli

settlers. This will complete the isolation of the Bethlehem area and

permanently break its links with Jerusalem only 8 kilometres away.  The Beit

Jala tunnel is just one of the many bypass roads that Palestinians cannot

use.  It towers over the Beit Jala olives groves and penetrates more Beit

Jala lands connecting the settlement blocs with Jerusalem.  Palestinians who

have valid permits to Isarel have to go through the crossing terminal Gilo

300.

 

 

 

Thousand of fruitful olive trees have been slated for clearance to make way

for the Apartheid Wall. Since 2000, nearly half a million olive tress have

been uprooted. This uprooting of Palestine’s oldest trees, the providers of

Palestinian society’s most staple food, has inspired people around the world

to form action groups that come to re-plant olive trees and also help with

the olive harvest.

 

Thousands of homes have been demolished and Beit Jala has some really

beautiful old homes – stone masonry for which the people of this town are

famous.  These homes are part of the 18,000 houses that have been demolished

throughout Palestine since the year 2000.  In yet another civil action that

has galvanised international volunteers, peace activist Israeli professor

Jeff Halper has set up the Israeli Committee Against House Demolitions to

help rebuild these homes.

 

One has to see for oneself the devastating effects of Israeli efforts to rid

themselves of the Palestinians – razed lands, miles of razor wire to stop

Palestinians from working their farms, expanding illegal settlements

creating a permanent blot on the landscape, a Wall that is literally

imprisoning the Palestinian population, curfews, raids, bombardments.

 

 

 

Again, I’d like to make the point that unless Israel ends the occupation,

life for Palestinians whether Muslim or Christian, has no happy ending.

There is nothing temporary about these measures and former US president

Jimmy Carter and Archbishop Tutu are just two of many eminent persons who

have described the situation as nothing less than apartheid, occupation and

colonisation.   Archbishop Tutu in his report only last month after visiting

Gaza said that he was appalled by the failure of the international community

to bring an end to the suffering.

 

 

 

The evidence can be openly seen throughout the West Bank, Gaza and East

Jerusalem if one chooses to travel along today’s via Dolorosa – a

confronting reality that would help all of us better understand the true

nature of Israel’s relationship with the people of the land.

 

 

HOW PEOPLE ARE HELPING PALESTINIANS

 

The Palestinian existential struggle has momentous implications for world

peace.  More than that, it is a matter of justice and human rights.  Actions

to free the Palestinians are springing up in every country, and only ten

weeks ago, a small group of internationals including an Israeli and an

elderly Jewish holocaust survivor set sail for Gaza to break a draconian

blockade that has devastated the entire population and to bring this

humanitarian crisis to the world media’s attention.

 

 

 

This venture saw ordinary people from all different backgrounds take time

out from their normal lives to embark on something quite dangerous because

the Palestinian predicament was something they could no longer tolerate as

human beings. Because after years of UN resolutions supporting the

Palestinians being vetoed by the US and just as many years of Israel

violating international law without being held to account, desperate times

called for desperate measures.

 

Despite Israel saying that it has withdrawn the Jewish settlers from Gaza

and that it no longer occupies this tiny strip of land, Israel in fact has

turned Gaza into the world largest prison, and worse still, is withholding

food, fuel, electricity, water and medical supplies from the population. 

The Palestinians are no longer allowed out for work or study or to visit

their families in the West Bank, and the critically ill are denied emergency

medical attention outside Gaza.  Fishermen are not allowed to fish within

7kms of the shore despite the sea beyond Gaza’s shoreline being

international waters and all these prohibitions are having a catastrophic

effect on every aspect of life for the Palestinians who are prisoners in

their own land.

 

 

 

The boats did get through and more are going.  But, dramatic actions are not

the only way to make a difference.  Some amazing Australians have done

things in their own way.

 

 

Dr Jean Calder AC

 

Three years ago, Dr Jean Calder was awarded the Companion of

the Order of Australia, our country’s highest civil honour for inspirational

humanitarian service in the Middle East.   For 27 years, she has lived and

worked with the disabled, disadvantaged and traumatised in Palestinian

refugee camps and from an old garage in Gaza, established the Al-Amal City

Centre of Ability Development which has now become internationally-

recognised as a rehabilitation centre for men, women and children suffering

from mental or physical disabilities.  Not only that, she adopted two

handicapped Palestinian children from the refugee camps in Lebanon and

added a third badly crippled young boy to her family in Gaza. 

Where once Jean helped them, now they are helping Jean in her work.

 

 

 

Her work requires her to travel frequently between Gaza and the West Bank.

Being a foreigner does not make getting around any easier.  Like the

Palestinians, she has to go through the checkpoints and road blocks that

make each trip a gruelling exercise in patience and endurance where endless

waiting is just part of the game to enable you to get through.  And after

all this time of living in Palestine, Jean Calder still only has a

three-month tourist visa that she needs to renew each time by leaving Gaza

and then applying for a new visa from the Israeli authorities.  The

Palestinians are not given visas at all anymore if they have to leave for

work, study or other reasons and this could easily happen to Jean because

there are no guarantees.

 

 

 

She has said: “Many people who are concerned about human rights have found

it difficult to deal with the Palestinian issue because of the

misrepresentation. To indicate that you are concerned about Palestinians

often brands you as being something bad  . . . I'm Australian and proud to

be. I'm also just as proud to be standing beside the Palestinians. I think

the fact that I am a foreigner here, they feel it's moral support. They're

not alone in the world.”

 

 

Moira Kelly AO

 

Moira Kelly from the Children First Foundation first went to Gaza with

Philip Sacca in 2004 to bring badly damaged children to Australia

for operations that give them a new lease on life.  Phillip who has been

tireless in his efforts to help Moira’s mercy missions, is actually here

tonight and with the Beit Jala Association has been instrumental in bringing

Palestinian children from Gaza. This is never easy because exiting Gaza is

dependent on Israel’s permission and if Israel closes the main border

crossings, that permission is only remotely possible after exhaustive

negotiations with the Israeli Embassy and our Department of Foreign Affairs

as Phillip knows only too well.  Moira and her team assess the children

needing surgery for devastating injuries or deformities, find doctors

willing to donate their services and then provide rehabilitation at the

Foundation’s farm in Kilmore.

 

 

 

 

Dr Paddy Dewan

 

Dr Paddy Dewan, a prominent pediatric uro-surgeon was sent to

the Gaza Strip by the “Kind Cuts for Kids Foundation”  which has been

sending two missions a year for the last few years to operate on patients

requiring specialist treatment and to give local doctors and nurses advanced

training to cope with a situation he describes as “overwhelming”. Dr Dewan

has personally seen about 165 patients and has operated on about half of

them with complex problems.   These are children in desperate need of care

that is not available in Gaza and because of Israeli travel bans, they are

unable to get help anywhere else.

 

 

 

 

 

 

Dr Geoff Bird AM and Beris Bird AM

 

Some people do things so quietly that when you hear about

what they do to ease the suffering of others, you want to shout

it from the rooftops. Plastic surgeon Dr Geoff Bird and his wife Beris,

who is a palliative care nurse, are on yet another trip to Gaza and

are working in the hospital there supported by Anglicord.  For seven

years, Geoff worked as Chief Surgeon of Nazareth Hospital, but with

this new trip, there is no sign that Geoff and Beris are intending to

retire anytime soon.

 

 

 

 

Dr Francis Nathan and Merlin Nathan

 

Francis and Merlin Nathan – a surgeon and an occupational

therapist from Adelaide – regularly volunteer to go to Gaza and

the West Bank to work in the hospitals and medical centres there

with the support of the US Palestine Children’s Relief Fund and Australians

Friends of Palestine in Adelaide.  Their latest expedition in April this

year took them to Nablus in the north where Francis worked with an

international team to help reduce the cataract surgical load and Merlin says

she had the rewarding opportunity of working in the Rehabilitation Centre in

the new Asker Camp on the outskirts of Nablus.  Both are constantly trying

to raise funds for medical equipment and supplies to take back with them on

each trip. 

 

 

 

 

Dr Paul Kitchen and Revd Merrill Kitchen AO

 

Surgeon Paul Kitchen, and his wife Merrill Kitchen, who has degrees in

medical science and theology and is currently the Principal of the Churches

of Christ Theological College, have spent the last 30 years making regular

working trips to Nazareth Hospital as well as working in Gaza and on other

projects in the Holy Land. Whenever they return home to Australia, they

continue with advocacy work for Palestine and interfaith initiatives, and

organise tours to the Holy Land like the one coming up in December.  Paul

was the main surgeon in Nazareth Hospital for three years in the early 1970s

and also worked in the former Baptist Hospital in Gaza.  At the time, they were

battling an epidemic and Paul found a way of stemming the outbreak

with some help from Merrill who was then working in microbiology.  They

have been tireless in their advocacy for Palestine and have shown that

they are ready to do anything that is useful, even rolling up their sleeves

and painting.

 

 

 

 

 

Mary Baxter

 

Mary Baxter, a retired university lecturer in her 70s and widow

of an Anglican minister decided that she could no longer live out her

retirement pottering around at home and so she took herself off to the

occupied city of Hebron where she has spent months every year since 2003

helping Palestinian children just make the daily trip to school and home. 

It was impossible for her to accept that young children are too afraid to

venture out of their homes because every day they are being stoned by

Israeli settler children while the soldiers do nothing.

 

 

 

 

 

Emeritus Professor Stuart Rees AM

 

Emeritus Professor Stuart Rees, head of the Sydney Peace Foundation

refused to cave in to the Jewish lobby’s demands that he withdraw the

peace prize awarded to Palestinian peacemaker Hanan Ashrawi in 2003

because the lobby claimed she was a terrorist. Stuart Rees said that he

would not be able to sleep at night if he allowed himself to be intimidated

by threats of sponsorship withdrawal.  Even though he did lose funding

from major sponsors, others came forward and contributed because

they were inspired by Professor Rees’ courage.

 

 

 

The Right Honourable Malcolm Fraser AC CH

 

Our former prime minister Malcolm Fraser wrote a wonderful article in

The Age this year when we asked if he would support a Palestine Motion

in our parliament recognising the suffering of Palestinians after the

parliament had moved a bipartisan motion congratulating Israel on 60

years since its creation.  Unfortunately, our motion did not succeed, but

we received many signatures from judges, academics, church leaders

and ordinary Australians who wanted to see justice done.

 

 

 

 

What you can do

 

There are myriad ways that you can help.   In particular, I want to emphasise

the laudable recommendations that some of our most senior

Australian church leaders put forward after visiting Israel and Palestine

last year.  Merrill Kitchen was amongst them. 

 

Their report includes the following:

 

joining groups like Paul and Merrill’s Nazareth Work party;

inviting speakers to church or community groups;

organising or attending film nights on Palestine;

arranging talks for visiting peace workers and advocates for Palestine;

holding vigils;

joining public actions for peace;

writing letters to the Prime Minister, members of parliament and

 

         church leaders and also the media.

 

The Nazareth Work party 

 

So far,  260 people from around Australia have gone on these trips,

some more than once.  Those taking part know it involves travel, work and learning,

giving up summer holidays here, having practise runs at work camps to condition

themselves to painting in the cold weather, cultivating a church base so

that news of their time with the Palestinians gets back to the

congregations.  It is selfless work, but also enormously rewarding as

everyone who has volunteered his or her services knows. 

 

Concluding remarks

 

Speaking up for justice and human rights is our duty not only as Christians or

Muslims or Jews or any other faith, but as human beings. We ask that you take

the time to learn more about what is really happening to the Palestinians and

contrary to what many think, it does not take long to learn.

 

The conflict is only 60 years old, not centuries old. The conflict is

political not religious. The conflict is simple, not complicated. There is a

solution – end the occupation. There is a way forward – acknowledge the

injustice done. There is a chance for peace  - speak out and act.

 

The words of the Melkite Archbishop Father Chacour from the

Galilee whose knowledge of Aramaic breathes new life into the familiar

Beatitudes are powerful in reminding us that we must do more than speak. 

Instead of “Blessed are the peacemakers”, he says the actual meaning is

“Get up, go ahead, do something, move, you peacemakers, and you

shall be called children of God.”  The Palestinians have surely suffered

enough.
