IN WASHINGTON: WHO'S MINDING THE POLICY STORE?
They're talking about it in Europe and in Israel-and not just in
whispers. Political commentators outside the United States are talking
loudly, and often with notable chagrin, about the Bush administration's
enthusiastic embrace of Israel and the harshly anti-Palestinian policies
of Israeli Prime Minister Ariel Sharon. In Europe, they talk about an
"unreserved acceptance of Israeli policy" and a partisanship that
"rattles" U.S. allies. In Israel, the concern is reaching a higher
level. "When it comes to terror and the Arabs," writes Gideon Samet,
editorial board member at Israel's leading liberal newspaper, Haaretz,
the United States has become "more Israeli than the Israelis....rag[ing],
raining abuse on 'the other,' and demonizing the Palestinians."
The language being used abroad to describe the near frenzy of the
administration's anti-Palestinian campaign and the near totality of its
embrace of Israel and Sharon is unusually colorful and conveys the
perception among those who observe the U.S. from afar of an
unprecedented and almost irrational exuberance in the Bush approach.
Strangely, American commentators seem barely to have noticed any change
in policy-perhaps because most leading commentators are themselves so
stridently anti-Palestinian and uncritical of Sharon's tactics that the
administration's grant of carte blanche arouses no curiosity.

Where did the heightened enthusiasm for Israel and Sharon originate?
Such close identification with Israel's anti-Palestinian brutality is
unseemly even for a very pro-Israel Washington. This Bush
administration, moreover, is a descendant of the first Bush
administration, and the Bush I team regarded Sharon and the Israeli
right wing with real hostility. Given this earlier animosity, it is
also natural to wonder whether the administration is acting in concert
now on Israeli-Palestinian policy or is encountering significant policy
disagreements among its senior members similar to those well publicized
differences between Secretary of State Colin Powell and Undersecretary
for Defense Paul Wolfowitz over Iraq.


The answer to both questions lies in the fact that in the foreign policy
arena Bush II is a descendant not primarily of his father but more
directly of the far more conservative Reagan administration. A group of
fervent and highly ideological Israel supporters, almost all Reaganite
"neoconservatives," has gained positions throughout Bush administration
policy councils. Led by Wolfowitz, they are widely believed among
Washington insiders to have a powerful and influential voice in
policymaking.


During the Reagan years, the neoconservatives-so called because most
were once Democrats who switched to the Republican party in the
1970s-trumpeted Israel's virtues as a barrier to Soviet advances in the
Middle East and scorned the Palestinians as Soviet tools. Their
ideological fervor went beyond merely being conservative and anti-Soviet
to a fierce support for Israel and an equally fierce opposition to
Palestinian nationalism. They are no different today-only older, more
powerful and, without a Soviet focus, better able to concentrate on
advancing Israel's interests.

Although Wolfowitz himself appears to be dealing primarily with broader
Middle East issues, particularly targeting Iraq, he is described by one
Washington insider as "over-the-top crazy when it comes to Israel," and
he is abetted in propagating his views among senior policymakers by a
large, very dense network of pro-Israel officials at the subcabinet
level. These include, at the Defense Department, Deputy Secretary for
Policy Douglas Feith and Assistant Secretaries Peter Rodman, and Dov
Zackheim. A few junior officials at Defense, including the Israel and
the Syria/Lebanon desk officers, are imports from the Washington
Institute for Near East Policy, a think tank spun off from the
pro-Israel lobby organization, AIPAC. One source says that the entire
Office of the Secretary of Defense is highly politicized and that
getting a job there is impossible unless the applicant shares the
office's hard-line views.

Elsewhere in the administration, another former Washington Institute
fellow, John Hannah, is a key member of Vice President Richard Cheney's
personal national security group. Cheney's chief of staff, Lewis Libby,
is a protégé of Wolfowitz, first as a student and in the 1980s as a
subordinate at both the State and the Defense Departments. On the
National Security Council staff, Elliott Abrams, a leading Reagan
administration official, is said to be "noisy" in his support for Israel
and opposition to Arab interests. Undersecretary of State John Bolton
is another Reagan holdover, perhaps the lone neoconservative voice at
the State Department, who networks with his old colleagues elsewhere in
policymaking councils.

Pro-Israel activists are also leaders in the information-and
disinformation-arena, which the administration has lately been
emphasizing heavily in an effort to improve the United States' image
abroad. The head of Radio Liberty, an old Cold War propaganda holdover
now converted to service in the "war on terrorism," is Thomas Dine, who
was the very active head of AIPAC throughout most of the Reagan and the
Bush I years. Brigadier General Simon Worden, who directed the Office
of Strategic Influence at the Pentagon until it was recently disbanded
in a storm of controversy over its plans to launch a campaign of
deliberate distortion in foreign media, is a protégé of Douglas Feith.

On the periphery, William Kristol, a second-generation neoconservative,

and his magazine The Weekly Standard are closely linked to and serve as
cheerleaders for the Israeli coterie inside government. Former Bush
speechwriter David Frum, who coined the term "axis of evil," is a former
Weekly Standard editor. Richard Perle, a former Reagan administration
assistant secretary of defense and one of the coterie's most ardent and
longstanding Israeli supporters, still keeps his hand in from his
position as chairman of the Pentagon advisory body, the Defense Policy
Board. Frank Gaffney, another Reagan defense official, keeps his hand
in from his think tank, the Center for Security Policy. Reagan's UN
ambassador Jeane Kirkpatrick also makes her views known from the
sidelines.

Most of these individuals are prominent in the campaign to target Iraq,
but their pro-Israel role, just below the radar screen, is equally
significant. Their views are not always accepted, but they are so
ardent and well placed that they inevitably carry great weight in
policymaking circles. They are consistently able to put their point
across by suggesting ideas and policy approaches to senior
policymakers. This has already been evident. They suggested, for
instance, much of the attitude toward Islam that Bush enunciated in his
first post-September 11 speech to Congress. Hannah reportedly
originated the idea of the recent policy reassessment toward the
Palestinian Authority.

There are some differences of nuance among them-the difference between
an Israeli Labor and a Likud orientation. But the most prominent
members of the group follow a hard right-wing line. Feith, for
instance-perhaps the most prolific of the network-has a two-decade-long
record of policy papers, many co-authored with Perle, propounding a
strongly anti-Palestinian, pro-Likud view. According to James Zogby,
President of the Arab-American Institute in Washington, who has analyzed
Feith's writings in depth, he views the Palestinians as not constituting
a legitimate national group, believes that the West Bank and Gaza belong
to Israel by right, and advocates that the U.S. abandon its mediating
effort altogether and foreswear the land-for-peace formula.

At the cabinet level, there is little real difference on how to handle
the Israeli-Palestinian problem. One well placed source dismisses
reports of intense in-fighting as "inside-the-beltway gossip" that makes
good copy but is overdrawn. Others confirm that "there's not much
light" between any of the top-level people. Where there is debate, this
tends to pit Powell against his colleagues, but most insiders see little
significance in any split. All members of the senior team are perceived
to be consummate team players, and Powell is thought to retain
considerable power because he is popular and well regarded by Bush.
Although Cheney and National Security Adviser Condoleezza Rice are more
intolerant toward the Palestinians, particularly Palestinian leader
Yasir Arafat, Powell has carried the day thus far on the issue of
maintaining ties with the Palestinian Authority.


The differences are small at most, and any notion that the
administration might bring greater even-handedness to its dealings with
Israel and the Palestinians is fast going by the board precisely because
there is little divergence. Senior policymakers are widely described as
"quintessential pragmatists," unsentimental about both Israel and the
Palestinians, but often with a hawkish bent. Exasperation with the
Palestinians is high even for Powell, higher still for the others.

Since September 11, Bush and his senior team have all bought the view
pressed by Sharon that the U.S. and Israel face identical terrorist
threats and that all Palestinian resistance to the occupation
constitutes terrorism. They do not distinguish between suicide bombings
and attacks on Israeli soldiers, and they do not condemn Israeli actions
against Palestinians. Nuances have been lost in the war on terrorism.
Bush "is not a very nuanced person," one former diplomat notes. "Issues
are black and white for him, but this is not a black and white
situation." Haaretz reports that Bush has adopted Rice's approach,
which advocates total dedication to the war on terrorism and the effort
to target Saddam Hussein rather than futilely devoting attention to
"marginal issues" like the Israeli-Palestinian situation.

If there is sharp disagreement within the administration, it emerges at
a lower level between career diplomats at the State Department with a
strong substantive background in the Middle East and the ideologically
motivated group of pro-Israel officials. Whereas career diplomats have
some appreciation for Israel's occupation as the source of Palestinian
grievances and ultimately of Palestinian violence, the neoconservatives
focus on ending Palestinian violence while maintaining the occupation.
In the broader arena, the diplomats are aware that Palestinian
grievances roil the Arab "street" and can inspire further anti-American
terrorism, while the Israeli advocates see no link between the
Israeli-Palestinian conflict and U.S. relationships throughout the Arab
world, and actively try to downplay any link between the Palestinian
plight and the root causes of terrorism.

Thus far, the pro-Israel group is winning these battles. Following the
upsurge in Palestinian terrorism, the State Department is on the
defensive and finds itself unable to press for a more balanced policy.
Palestinians have "muddied their message," says the former diplomat, by
mixing terrorism with other forms of resistance. Arafat's perceived
duplicity, compounded by the intercepted arms shipment smuggled from
Iran to the Palestinian Authority, seems to have undermined any State
Department inclination to urge a return to peacemaking.

One Washington observer classifies Bush policymakers, at the cabinet and
the subcabinet level, as either pro-Israel, open to the Palestinian
perspective but too timid to take a position, or entirely political and
not substantively knowledgeable on any foreign policy issue. This last
group, which includes Bush's long-time political advisers, regards the

Israeli-Palestinian situation as a headache-and a politically risky one
at that-and strives to keep Bush well away from the political morass.
It seems clear that in the aftermath of the upsurge in Palestinian
terrorism and the arms smuggling incident, Powell and much of the State
Department, always timid and disinclined to take bold action even before
September 11, have largely been won over to the political group and at
least for now believe U.S. intervention is futile. All of which
essentially leaves the field to those in the pro-Israel camp who are
fervent, vocal, and utterly lacking in timidity.

Washington's embrace of Sharon comes, ironically, at a time when
opposition to the occupation is growing in Israel, among military
reservists, peace activists, and commentators. What are the
implications for the peace process of the U.S. refusal to take heed?
Gila Svirsky is a long-time peace and human rights activist in Israel,
co-founder of the Coalition of Women for a Just Peace. "For decades,"
she says, "we in the Israeli peace movement have been struggling to get
Israelis to compromise on the issues that feed the conflict with the
Palestinians. And then our work for peace gets doused twice: once by a
prime minister who believes brutality will convince the Palestinians to
give up, and then by a U.S. president who supports him on this.
"Bush has become a big part of the problem. He has to make up his mind:
either he's for peace, or he's for Sharon. He can't be both."
