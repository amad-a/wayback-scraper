NABLUS TO BRISTOL - AN APPEAL FOR PEACE
Ringed by the mountains, with its narrow alleys of ancient stone, Nablus is a beautiful city. Even with its shops closed up under a nearly twenty-four hour curfew, where for most of the time only tanks and ambulances move on the empty streets. The curfew has lasted for nearly three weeks now.
We're staying in the Askar refugee camp, on the edge of Nablus. After half a century of refugee life, Askar is a crowded little village, nearly bursting with its 8,000 people who have nowhere else to live. Four of us - three Swedes and myself - have volunteered with the International Solidarity Movement, and are trying to show what little support four very ordinary foreigners are able, for a whole people living under occupation.
Four days ago we visited a family imprisoned in the ground floor of their house by the Israeli army. The army often holds hostage the people living in homes they occupy, as shield against a possible attack. On this occasion, we were able to deliver urgently needed medicines. Though we couldn't stay more than a few minutes, for fear of being arrested, the hostages were delighted to have our whirlwind visit.
The following day, we were less successful. A family of six had been driven out of their elegant home, whose location at the very top of a mountain makes it an ideal lookout for the army. We found the evicted family, plus cooking utensils and a couple of mattresses, in the unfinished house of a friend, lower down the hill. All the family's possessions are left behind in their occupied home. The oldest daughter, a university student, said the soldiers had torn up her textbooks. The new accommodation has a giant pile of broken cement in the middle of the open plan living room, and no window panes. It will do for summer camping - but what about when winter arrives? It snows in Nablus in the winter.
At the family's request, we went up to their home, to see if the army would allow them to move back into one or two rooms of it. As four of us trudged, ankle deep in white dust, along the ridge leading to the house, gleaming white and perched squarely on the mountain top, we saw the soldiers, moving on the verandah above us. We knew that by then they had seen us, too. Arms hanging by our sides, palms outward to show we held no weapons, we walked forward slowly. The crack of a rifle shot stopped us in our tracks. They were firing over our heads. Half a dozen soldiers, guns unslung, came strolling down towards us. Half a dozen more watched us from the roof. Here was our chance to plead the family's case. "We're here to ask you if the family can come back into the house," the woman we had chosen as our negotiator said. "There's nothing we can do," one of the soldiers answered. "Go away now, and don't come back. If you try to come back here, you will be arrested." That, as far as the army was concerned, was the end of the matter. It was left for us to go back and break the news to the despondent family.
Much of the work of ISM involves recording what happened here. We have been into homes and photographed the holes, typically three to four feet in diameter, dynamited in the interior walls of houses during the invasion of Nablus in April. The Israeli soldiers prefer this method to that of searching house to house along the open street, where they might be exposed to snipers. They work their way down a ground floor row, blasting their way from room to room. Some of the families have money and skills to repair these holes. One father proudly showed us the crude plaster job he had done himself. In other, poorer household, repair is not an option. A family member will pull back a curtain to reveal the still-existing window between themselves and the neighbours.
We visited the parents of a seven-year-old boy shot this year on May 17, behind the gates of his house, and fatally wounded. Mohamed was waiting with his father for the tanks to pass by, so that they could go out to the mosque. A passing tank blasted bullets through the closed metal gates from the street side. After hours of agony, the little boy died on the operating table. The tank crew will never know who they killed behind the gates. Mohamed's father still carries shrapnel in his legs from the shooting. "All the time in my head I hear two things - the call to prayer from the mosque, and the sound of bullets."
Ten-year old Amjad Abdeh was shot in the street by a tank while picking up spent bullets and electrical wire to sell for scrap, at nine in the morning. The boy's uncle was able to identify the body only by the clothes Amjad was wearing. Amjad's aunt, who had brought Amjad up as her own son, broke down weeping as she told the story.
Another family showed us the hole in their bedroom pane where a tank bullet entered the body of a middle-aged man named Mahmoud, as he was looking in a drawer for medicines. Mahmoud's elderly mother cried inconsolably, as the rest of the family told how for 27 hours the army kept the ambulance from getting through to them, and refused to allow a car to go out. (The Red Crescent ambulance station is half a minute's walk away.) There was no fighting in the neighbourhood at the time. The family cannot understand why this law-abiding workman, the family breadwinner, had to die.
This morning a nineteen-year-old died from a tank bullet, fired at close range on the main street of Askar. A few boys claim their friends were throwing stones at the time. Others deny it. Whatever the truth of the matter, the young man is dead. His blood still stains the ground near the Askar mosque. We had hoped that our coming here might help to prevent such horrors.
It is comforting to know that since so many internationals have been in Nablus and the surrounding refugee camps, the nights have been noticeably quieter. People do seem reassured by our presence here. We are overwhelmed by their generosity and kindness. And surprised, in this town where nearly everyone seems to have had a relative violently killed by the army, at how little they seem to hate the Israelis. (No doubt it helps that many in Askar have worked in Israel, and have Israeli friends.) Most people in Nablus we talked to claimed to reserve their anger for Sharon and his government, and for the policies of the United States.
"We want peace," everyone tells us. "All we want is for Israel to end the occupation, and leave us alone."
They ask me to take this message home with me to Bristol.
