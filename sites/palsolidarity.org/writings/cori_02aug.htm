- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

 

We have left Qalqilya and are in Tel Aviv, waiting to fly out tomorrow. it was so strange being on Israeli roads and having no checkpoints. Almost surreal.

So yesterday we did this incredibly exciting action with about 30 farmers. We took shovels and marched to one of the roads that the farmers need to get to their farms which has been blocked by a roadblock.

Roadblocks are mostly big piles of dirt/sand/stone. Some are huge concrete blocks. On this road it was mostly dirt, about as tall as a person & 1/2, and they had made 3-4 piles of dirt because people kept going around the original roadblock.

So we started in with our shovels and fairly soon the soldiers showed up. They fired over our heads to warn us, and all of us internationals put our hands in the air while the farmers stopped digging momentarily. Then the soldiers came closer. They checked the i.d.'s of the 4 press people we had with us (one from the local TV station, one from the French press and one from Reuters) and detained one of them (they were all Palestinian).

Cori and James (another member of our group, a really sweet British man in his 60's) went to talk with the soldiers. We told them this was a peaceful demonstration & the farmers just need to be able to get to their fields. They said the farmers could go to the main checkpoint in the town & get permission to go around from the outside of the town (which is patently false - they can't get permission, they are not allowed through the checkpoint and they can't drive on the settler roads to get to their fields another way). When Cori tried to engage the soldier about how he felt about the occupation, the soldiers ended the conversation abruptly and told us just to leave.

We said we would wait for the journalist who had been detained and they promised to let him go in several minutes after checking his i.d.. Soon after they let him go, and we moved back to the roadblock. The farmers decided to keep digging even though we had told the soldiers that we would leave. The soldiers moved back to their original position and just watched us finish digging.

We moved on to 2 other piles of dirt further on the same road and made small passages in each. enough to get a donkey and cart through. Then we left. This was a small victory in terms of making a small passage in the roadblocks (farmers are still likely to be shot if caught in their fields), but a bigger victory in terms of nonviolent organizing in the town. This kind of protest had not been organized in some time. The townspeople are planning another march (this time to the checkpoint) on Saturday. We wish we could be there for it.


Update from Alma

Piggy-banking on Cori's last thought, I want to relate to all of you an experience that made quite and impact on me and I hope I am able to convey to you the importance of any change in the favor of the living conditions in which Palestinians are forced to live under.

As part of our work, we rode with the Red Crescent ambulances. During one of my evening shifts, we were called to bring a 21 year old woman who had experience complications during birth. After arranging safe transportation for 1 1/2 hours on the phone from the Mayor of Qalqiliya and the Checkpoint folks, we went to deliver her in critical condition to the hospital in Nablus. After a rather unusually speedy crossing, we deliver her and went back. On route, we were stopped by a jeep around 4:00 am.

We were all asked to step down and a search was carried inside the ambulance, all our i.d.s were checked and we were ordered to wait until they checked on them. After about 15 munutes, we were let go. Once in the ambulance, the doctor received a call on his cell phone. The 21 year old woman had just passed away.
