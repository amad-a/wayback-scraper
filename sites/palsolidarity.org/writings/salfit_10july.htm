"It is the sound of a multifloor cement home being reduced to rubble.

When you wake up to this sound your heart sinks and seems to merge with a the fear that is rolling around in your stomach."

Chris and Carl Williams

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

 

Kay, Patty, Bernhard and I left for the village of Salfeet. It is located between Nablus and Jerusalem. It is a farming community of approx. 10,000 people. We had to walk to the check point leaving Nablus, then get a taxi to take us through another check point and to where we walked over 3 roadblocks to get to a small village called Yasuf. From there we called for another taxi which took us into Salfeet. Total distance approx. 5 miles, total time approx. 4 hrs. And we had it easy compared to the Palestinians.

We spent the rest of the day talking to community leaders getting a feel for Salfeet. Our primary task here is to determine if ISM should put people here for the duration of Freedom Summer. We found that 60% of all the settlers in the West Bank live in the district of Salfeet, an area approx. the size of a county in Michigan. Also 40% of the underground water is in Salfeet district, so it is an area coveted by the Israelis very much. For example, overlooking the town of Salfeet is the settlement of Ariel which contains 70,000 settlers.

While the town of Salfeet is under curfew most of the time, the soldiers are not in the town all the time and people move around carefully, in defiance, trying to bring some normality to their lives. But while we were setting in the mayor’s office talking, the soldiers came into town and enforced the curfew, so we had to set and drink tea for several hours until the soldiers left.

Later, we visited several buildings and houses that had been destroyed by the occupation forces. This included a water tower in the process of being built. A sign on the tower said it was a project of the U.N. being financed by USAID. It was damaged beyond repair by tank and machine gun fire. Taxpayers’ dollars at work - building then destroying.

Friday 6am: we were woke by the sound of tanks and APCs 2 blocks away. We saw people being forced out of their house and standing in the street. We called our Palestinian contact person and asked if he wanted us to go out and investigate. He said go ahead. We walk out and down the street towards the tank. We heard an explosion, which later we were informed was a sound grenade. We stood approx. 25 yards away from the tank in full view. The soldiers did not see us until they were leaving the house, which said to us that they know there is no resistance fighters in this town. When they did finally see us they were startled and got into their vehicles. A jeep drove up to us and the soldier inside said to me: "what are you doing here?" I told him: "we are here watching what you are doing here." He looked at me and said: "you can go," and we walked about 10 feet away and stopped and watched them leave.

We then spent about 2 hours with the family who fed us breakfast and told us what happened. It seems as the soldiers were patrolling the town, they saw movement on the roof of this house, and they drove the people out to the street. They made a man who lives across the road go into the house being searched as a human shield and they went to the roof and found a small herd of goats the family keeps there for milk.

We continued to meet with people from the town for the rest of the day. Most of the military activity is for the purpose of harassment and disrupting of normal life. No one can go to work, all roads are blocked, no travel in or out. In the night usually around 2 or 3am they enter the town and start shooting randomly, sometimes at houses. Flares are shot into the air and everyone’s sleep is disrupted. The children are being traumatized, and the school exams which are very important to their future are not allowed to happen. Much of the military activity is pure vandalism.

Saturday - we went to a small farming community called Iskaka, about 6 kilometers from Salfeet where Israeli settlers were in the process of confiscating a farmer’s land. They had bulldozed a road around the land they were taking. Because it was Saturday no one was working at the time we were there, so we were able to see very clearly what they were doing. We told the farmer we would return tomorrow and see if we could talk with the settlers, hopefully not getting shot in the process.

We returned Sunday and walked to the area being confiscated. About 20 Palestinian farmers went with us which caused us some concern as they were at risk of being shot at more than us, and the settlers might not see us at first. So we asked the farmer to stay a way back while we walked slowly towards the settlers. When we were approx 100 yards away they shot several times into the air. We stopped and the farmers moved further back. We stood there for about a half hour in plain sight watching for what they would do next. Finally a small pickup truck came up and drove right by all of us without stopping. Checking us out, I guess. Over the next half hour all the settlers left but 2. Finally they drove up to within 25 yards of us and motioned for us to come to them. We walked up to them, the one not driving was carrying an Uzi on his lap. He offered me his hand and I shook it. They wanted to know what we were about. I told him we were from the U.S. and we were touring the West Bank to see for ourselves what was going on as we did not believe what we saw and heard on our media. I asked him what they were doing and he told me they were constructing a military base, which is the standard first step in land confiscation. They asked us if we were with the farmers up the hill and I said that they asked us to come and see what was happening. At this time the conversation ended and they drove away. They left the area a few minutes later and we went back to the farmers house had tea, coffee, and food. At 2 pm we left to go to Jerusalem to see the rest of Michigan Peace Team’s team off as they were all leaving except Kay, Mike and I.
