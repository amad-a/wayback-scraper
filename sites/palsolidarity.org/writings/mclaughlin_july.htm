INTERNATIONALS BRUTALIZED AND ARRESTED BY MILITARY FOLLOWING NON-VIOLENT DEMONSTRATION

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

Last Training Day Changed to Friday 13 Aug.

 

I recently spent almost 3 weeks in and around Nablus, Palestine - the largest city in the Occupied West Bank. I was a participant volunteer in ‘Freedom Summer’ a campaign with the International Solidarity Movement, a Palestinian led organization dedicated to ending the occupation through non-violent resistance. I joined the ISM because I saw an opportunity to try to compensate for some of the injustices that are being inflicted on the Palestinian people by the Israeli government through American support and with my tax dollars.

Our training at the Faisel hostel in Jerusalem was cut short because of the dire need for Internationals in the West Bank to protect Palestinians from Israeli injustices. My group decided to go to Nablus.

We started out in a taxi with yellow - Israeli 'Jew only' plates so we were allowed to drive on the 'Jew only' roads. These are very nice, well lit highways that criss cross through the West Bank and the Gaza Strip. Palestinians are not allowed to even cross these roads - they will be shot if they try - and so villages are cut off from each other because of them. Along the way we passed a small Palestinian boy of about 9 years of age. Our driver backed up to offer him a ride to his village a few miles down the road. The boy opened the side door of the van and when he saw the 10 of us with our pale skin, light hair and green or blue eyes, his eyes grew wide with fright. He yelled 'Yahood' (Jew) and ran away like the wind. He thought we were Jews and that we would kill him. That wasn't hatred in his face, it was pure fear and it was fear based on experience. The settlers kill the Palestinians frequently and nothing is done to them. On the contrary, they are armed and trained by the Israeli government and the soldiers in the West Bank are there for no reason except to protect the settlers.

We found the turn off to Nablus had been dug up by bulldozers so we ended up having to change taxis 3 times and we were forced to continue our journey along a small dirt path that was in unbelievably poor shape. Eventually we made it to the checkpoint. Our Palestinian taxi driver (white and blue 'non-Jew' plates on his taxi) was afraid to stay to see us through because settlers in this area have a nasty habit of shooting Palestinians here and the soldiers protect the settlers. When we were finally let through we were sternly warned by the soldiers not to take any photos. They weren't worried about weapons or anything - it's the cameras that concern them. They asked me if I worked for CNN. They don't want the world to see what they are doing - for good reason. Other Internationals who tried to pass later were denied access and had to bypass the checkpoints by walking for hours over the mountain.

Nablus is unbelievably beautiful. It sits between mountains and I never once saw a single cloud in the sky - plenty of Apache helicopters - but no clouds. Every day the children fly elaborate homemade kites very high in the sky. Many are in the colors of Palestine. It is impossible to describe just how beautiful it is. Many times I took a few moments just to stare in awe at the beauty of this city and the generosity and kindness of the people living here. The settlers and the soldiers have stolen the mountains so they can look down on the Palestinians in Nablus - it's much easier to shoot them from the hills - and they do...every day. Some internationals rode in UN and Red Crescent ambulances to prevent the soldiers from firing on them. Sometimes they let them through with the wounded and other times they didn't.

I spent most of my time in Balata Refugee Camp where I stayed with a wonderful family. Their home was targeted for destruction by the IDF. The soldiers had already been here on March 1st to destroy the home. They locked the family in a room on the second floor, put all of their furniture in a pile on the first floor, burned it, pocketed the family's life savings, and detonated a plastic explosive on the first floor to weaken the structure so the bulldozer would have an easier time knocking it down. Tiles fell off the kitchen and bathroom walls on all 3 floors, glass broke throughout the home, and huge cracks appeared in the walls. The IDF pulled their bulldozer back though, because someone had called the media and they decided it wasn't such a good idea to bulldoze the home down with the family inside on International TV. The reason they gave for needing to demolish this home - which remember is in the middle of a refugee camp - was that 'suspected militants' sometimes visit there. We stayed in the home to protect it from the bulldozer. Each night the IDF brought their bulldozer by - accompanied by a tank and an APC. Sometimes the cut off the electricity first and other times they didn't. They'd park outside and fire machine guns and throw percussion grenades around. This usually continued from about 11:00 at night until 3:00 AM. They also managed to shoot people from time to time if they saw them peeking out of windows. I attended too many funerals of people who were shot for no reason. The army collectively punishes people by not letting them have the bodies to bury. We, the Internationals, were prepared to carry bodies from the hospital ourselves so they could be buried but the army always backed down and released them. It wouldn't look good on the evening news if Internationals are carrying bodies to the families because the Israelis were holding them hostage. I was able to get some video of the nightly tank/bulldozer/APC parade but the family wouldn't let me risk it most of the time. There are huge holes in the walls of all of the homes which the IDF blasted throughout the camp so they can move from house to house without entering the street. They even spray painted arrows on all of the interior walls to show other soldiers the pathways that have been blasted through. Every wall in these homes is scarred with bullet holes. Balata Camp, well - all of the West Bank and Gaza Strip really - are dotted with demolished homes and families living in tents on top of the rubble. When the army demolishes these homes the families don't have homeowners insurance of anything to help them recover. Israel NEVER compensates anyone for damages. The people are left with nothing and with no hope of every acquiring anything again since no one is allowed to work or move around. Destruction of homes is also illegal under the Geneva Conventions and International Law but since when did Israel care about laws or human rights?

One evening early in my stay the tank and bulldozer came early to Balata camp. They must have been bored that day because at about 4:00 in the afternoon they decided to have some fun with the little kids playing soccer in the clear area near the camp entrance. A tank would pull up close to the kids really fast, turn sharply to blow clouds of dust on them, and then retreat 100 feet or so. I watched this for about 45 minutes to an hour. Boys started picking up stones but I kept knocking them out of their hands. I was videotaping the tank and was getting scared because the children kept crowding around. I didn't want the kids shot if they targeted me because of my camera and with the kids crowding around me there was more chance that the soldiers would notice me & the camera. I put the camera down and started trying to get the kids to go home. Eventually a kid threw a rock - which didn't even come near hitting the tank - and the tank opened fire - not on the one who threw the rock but on a crowd of kids standing at least 50-75 feet away from the one who threw it. 9 children were injured - 2 of them very seriously. I'm not talking about teenagers here - these were little kids. I can't help but think the soldiers in the tank were gleeful that they had finally provoked them enough to get a rock thrown so they would have an excuse to open fire. They did back off after shooting the kids so they could be taken to the hospital. I guess we should be grateful that they were so compassionate. Often times they prevent the ambulances from entering until everyone has bled to death. A similar incident happened nearby a week later but this time the IDF managed to kill at least one child. I soon learned that this kind of thing is what passes for 'normal' in the occupied West Bank.

I spent nights in Balata and days in Nablus. These areas have been under 24 hour curfew for some time. Everything is closed. No one can go to work or even leave their homes. Ambulances are the only vehicles that can be seen and even they are shot at if the occupation forces see them. Of course firing at ambulances is illegal under international law but then so are curfews, occupations that last 35 years, settlements, collective punishments, house demolitions, and most everything else that the Israeli army does.

We, the Internationals, broke curfew openly every day. There is power in having a foreign passport and we used that power every chance we could. The soldiers seemed very confused. On one hand it was clear that they wanted us to like them, that they wanted to be seen as one of us - part of western society. I’d like to think that most of them were ashamed of what they are doing. None of them I spoke to admitted to having shot children yet children are shot all the time so at least some of them were lying.

We staged protests in Nablus opposing the curfew. As we marched and chanted our numbers grew as more Palestinians felt safe enough to join us. No soldiers showed up, perhaps because the press was there.

There are several homes in Nablus that the army has taken over. They put all of the families in one room and lock them in then they put snipers at the windows in the upper floors. We managed to get one family released but not until after the army had thrown percussion grenades at us, fired live ammunition, and hit us with tear gas (which, to our delight mostly blew over us and gassed the soldiers in the home behind us). We still refused to leave and they finally gave up and left the home.

We sat in front of bulldozers and tanks to prevent them from demolishing homes. Success in these endeavors is totally dependent on press presence. The first thing the army does is fire at the press and if that doesn't work they arrest them. Once the press is gone it's open season on any people who happen to be around. We realized that having the cameras there was the key to our success so we made sure that we protected the press with our bodies. With the cameras rolling, the army is not going to shoot Internationals who are standing in front of members of the press to protect them from Israeli bullets.

Israel passed a new law that allows them to deport or turn away at the border anyone who is there to show solidarity with the Palestinian people. They are afraid of this phenomenon of Internationals coming in to do the job that the UN and the press should be doing but cannot because of America. I met fellow ISMers in Nablus from England, the US, Denmark, Switzerland, Italy, Japan, India, Ireland, Wales, Canada, and China. The youngest was 18 and the oldest was a 77 year old man from Edmonton Canada with artificial knees. The people are realizing that it is up to us to stop these horrible daily abuses.

I didn't find one family in Balata camp that hadn't had at least one child who had been maimed or murdered by the Israelis. There isn't one home where guns haven't fired into the windows. There isn't one wall that isn't marked with bullet holes. There is not a single night when sleep is not disturbed by the sound of tanks, machine guns, Apaches, bulldozers, or missile fire. Water is precious. When it is available there is a tiny trickle of lukewarm water from the spicket. This water comes from aqueducts underneath Nablus but is taken by the Israelis for their own use and sold back to the Palestinians. The Israelis get 80% - 90% of it and the Palestinians are allowed to buy back 10% - 20% if they are lucky - 10% of their own water that has been stolen from them. The electricity is shut off by the army frequently - usually for no apparent reason except to show who's boss.

What I find most disturbing is the way that the army deliberately antagonizes the people - pushing and pushing and pushing, humiliating, terrorizing, and harassing everyone - especially children - until someone reacts. Then they go in with full force killing everything that moves and destroying everything in their path and pat themselves on the back for 'rooting out terror'.

I've been asked if the Palestinians display a hatred of Americans and whether this hatred was directed at me personally. Absolutely not! Unlike Americans, they are capable of separating the actions of our government from the people and they love the American people. I was, as usual when I'm in the Middle East, treated with kindness and respect everywhere I went. I never had to find my own way, carry a heavy load, or search for a meal or a cup of tea. It is simply amazing. When I arrived in Israel I was warned by local Israelis not to go to the occupied territories or East Jerusalem and put myself in danger with those evil and violent Arabs. The only thing one has to fear from the Palestinian people is that they will over feed you with delicious and elaborate home-cooked meals or that your schedule will be get delayed because you want to take everyone you meet up on their offers of a meal, tea, coffee, and a place to sleep.

It is also important to point out that many of the Internationals who participate in the solidarity movement in Palestine are Jewish. There were 3 American Jews in my group, and it was an Israeli Jew who trained us and led us into Nablus, and probably almost half of the protesters in my local area are Jewish. Many Jewish people feel a particular need to speak out against the Israeli government because they don't like what is being done in their name. There are many Jewish organizations, several of them in Israel, who are against the occupation and the treatment that the Palestinians are receiving by Israel. They just don't get much publicity here in America.

When I was leaving Nablus our taxi was stopped by tanks and bulldozers blocking the road. They were digging up yet another section of highway to prevent people from being able to move far from their homes if curfew is lifted. As usual, the soldiers jumped out and surrounded our car, shoving M-16s in our faces. The one who looked at my passport said, 'What are you doing here? This is not your place.' It took all my will not to reply 'What are YOU doing here? This is not YOUR place.'

As we drove from Nablus the tears started to come. I didn't want to leave; I felt that I belonged back in Balata. It isn't only the power that I have as an International to protect people and homes that make me want to stay it is because the people welcome us so warmly. It is the genuine love and unlimited generosity that they still manage to have despite the hardships that they face every day. It is the knowledge that I DID something, I was effective. On the scale of things what I did was very little but if all I had accomplished was to let some of the Palestinian people know that there are Americans who care, then I accomplished a lot. I will go back - soon - and I will take more people with me.
