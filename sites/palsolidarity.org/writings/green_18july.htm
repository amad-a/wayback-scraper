INTERNATIONALS BRUTALIZED AND ARRESTED BY MILITARY FOLLOWING NON-VIOLENT DEMONSTRATION

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

Last Training Day Changed to Friday 13 Aug.

 

NABLUS, West Bank, July 5 – At the entrance to the Balata refugee camp in Nablus, the sight of rubble, the smell of rotting meat, and piles of burnt trash are apparent. Emptied shops – resulting either from bombing or from severe economic strangulation – are in abundance. And there are posters of suicide bombers on nearly every surface of this war-weary city.

Nablus has been under almost constant curfew for the past 16 days. The violence has lately been limited to a recent assassination of a local Hamas leader, but more is almost certainly coming. The mood is one of torturous suspension, as Palestinians wait out the military occupation in anticipation of further assaults. Sporadic tank fire throughout the night has kept residents in a state of terror and sleeplessness.

The economy here is at a standstill; businesses are both blockaded from the outside and kept shuttered by the threat of Israeli military violence. Schools are closed; children play in the street within a safe distance from their homes.

Today, approximately 50 Palestinians, joined by a nearly equal number of international solidarity activists, defied the curfew in a public march through the Old City to demand that the UN intervene to enforce international resolutions calling for an end to the occupation. The illegal, nonviolent march was headed by local women, and accompanied by internationals whose presence was intended to keep Israeli soldiers from firing into the crowd. Women from Working Women's Committee, Fatah, and the Union of Palestinian Medical Relief Workers (UPMRC) initiated the march with the support of the International Solidarity Movement (ISM).

The women marched through the densely-packed casbah – the site of a thriving, illegal (under this occupation) commerce of produce – chanting an emotional declaration in Arabic: "This is our land and we are here. These people will not surrender. With our blood and with our spirit, we will build Palestine."

Palestinian men in their middle years watched with weary interest. The organizers had debated whether to make the full march to UN headquarters outside of the relative safety of the Old City. In the end, locals and internationals marched the full route without confrontation from the Israeli military.

Palestinians in Nablus are elated by the presence of the internationals – as attested by friendly horn blasts from passing ambulances, and by children who ran up to the smashed entrances of their homes to wave excitedly and practice English greetings. It is not the official international force that Palestinians have been demanding for the past 35 years, but this patchwork of volunteer medical workers, independent human rights observers, and nonviolent intervention teams is at least a validation from the outside world of the ongoing suffering experienced here.

Outside of the UPMRC headquarters, two adolescent boys presented ISM activists with shrapnel from bombs dropped from F-16 fighter jets that destroyed Palestinian Authority (PA) administrative buildings in April. "Look," one of the boys pleaded to anyone who stopped. "Look." The Ministry of Agriculture, which had formerly been used to jail Hamas militants, is in shambles, a giant gaping hole blown through one corner and pierced by machinegun fire over nearly every foot of its outer wall. Now, it is abandoned, along with every other Palestinian Authority facility. This destruction is courtesy of Lockheed Martin, which rolls F-16s off its assembly lines at its sprawling plant outside of Fort Worth, Tex., drawing profits of over $2 billion per year.

The desperate intensity of outreach to Western Europe and the United States is embodied by a young Palestinian woman, Ansam, whose entreaty to several internationals at the march was simply: "Go back to your country and tell them." When asked what she thought about the United States' complicated role in the conflict, she replied, "You should be with us." To an American ISM activist, she confessed, "I have no hope for the future. I don't want to live anymore. There must be a better place than this."

The phenomenon of "martyrdom" is in stark evidence here, and in fact ISM activists are staying with the families of suicide bombers to ward off retaliation by the Israeli military. But the march today was part of a nonviolent strategy of resistance – a long tradition in the Palestinian movement. The right to resist occupation through armed struggle is enshrined by the 1949 4th Geneva Convention, but the question haunts organizers: what is a credible resistance that will convince Israel to desist, and what actions of resistance will allow reconciliation between Jews and Palestinians after the occupation ends?

"You see that we are not terrorists," says a field coordinator for the UPMRC. "We want to live in peace. These men," she says, gesturing towards a wall of martyr posters, "don't carry out suicide bombings because they are naturally violent; it is because they have lost hope."

With the internationals labeled "supporters" of terror by the Israeli Interior Ministry, the issue of use of force is a sensitive one. Huwaida Arraf, a Palestinian-American who is a local coordinator of ISM based in Ramallah, confronts the charge head on: "As a movement we do not agree with the suicide bombing operations or any violence against civilians or non-combatants, but we stand behind the Palestinian right to armed struggle. We don't criticize it, but ours is a nonviolent movement."

The suicide bombings, in fact, have crept in to fill the vacuum of real power in Palestinian society. "Sadly," says Arraf, "the resistance among Palestinians is less than what we conceived last December."

"Since Jenin," Arraf continues, "Palestinians believe that in a massive march against the checkpoints they will just be gunned down” -- with no accountability whatsoever from the Israeli government. The local Palestinian leaders make the decisions, she stresses, but "with internationals there, we've seen a reduction of Israeli military violence – and we've seen increased international media coverage."
