"It is the sound of a multifloor cement home being reduced to rubble.

When you wake up to this sound your heart sinks and seems to merge with a the fear that is rolling around in your stomach."

Chris and Carl Williams

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

 

On Thursday, 8/15 two of us from Qalqilia who have been in Palestine for a while traveled north to Tul Karem to see if we could help with a new group from the International Solidarity Movement to get settled and up and running quickly. We spent two days there.

Tul Karem is about 15 Kilometers north of Qalqilia. It is also along the border of the West Bank and Green Line Israel. I will describe the process we went through to get there. Keep in mind that we were both privelidged enough to have fairly light skin and US passports so you can imagine the difference in risk if Palestinians were trying to do what we did.

The main road from Qalqilia to Tul Karem is completely shut down by the Israeli military. There is simply no way to get ther by that route. So first we crossed out of the main Qalqilia checkpoint to the East. The soldiers were happy to see us leave so that was not a problem, for Palestinians it could easily be an unsurmountable challenge depending on the mood of the soldiers. From there we took a taxi about three kilometers east to the village of Azzoun. Azzoun has a roadblock cutting it off from the main road so we had to get out of the taxi and cross this roadblock in order to catch another taxi. This taxi we took north along a small windy road for about 20 minutes untill that road dead ended at another roadblock. On the other side of this roadblock was a Jewish-only settler road and incidentally a military jeep. We had to wait about 15 minutes untill the jeep left. We were then able to scramble across the road, luckily no other military vehicles spotted us, and climb up a dirt road on the other side. At this point a Palestinian drove by us crossing around a roadblock off road. We asked him if he was going to Tul Karem and he said yes and offered us a ride if we wanted. He took us around a few more road blocks under conditions that must have been extremely damaging to his car. We reached another road and he gave up because there were roadblocks on every side of the intersection. He dropped us off and headed back, giving up on visiting his family, which had been his goal. we were able to walk across another roadblock and call someone in Tul Karem to send a taxi for us. A total of four taxi rides and a lucky free ride, an illegal scramble across a settler road, and a whole lot of stress, just to travel what should have been 15 kilometers.

Tul Karem area contains 45 villages, camps and towns. There are 150,000 people in the area and about 60,000 in Tul Karem itself. Many buildings have been destroyed during the intifada. There have been more than 100 martyrs in Tul Karem, 10 have been students and many have been children. Before the Intifada there were two checkpoints in the area, now there are dozens, both fixed and mobile. Tul Karem has been under continuous 24 hour curfew for the last two months. Curfew is lifted on average once a week, usually for 4 hours. In this time people must take care of all their needs such as shopping for the week.

Last week the army announced a lifting of curfew at 8:00 am. they announced it would last until 6:00 pm. After less than one hour tanks reinvaded and fired into the market. The soldiers assassinated Ziad Ada'as, a leader of Al Aqsa Martyrs Brigade and two others. The Israelis allowed a funeral march for the three martyrs but only allowed a very small number of people to attend. (A part of occupation I had never thought about, people here do not even have the right to mourn their dead.)

The day before we arrived a similar incident took place. The army announced a lifting of curfew, apparently hoping that a particular student at the university. They seem to have hoped that he would take the opportunity to go to school because when they reinvaded, long before they said the curfew was to end they surrounded the university. He was not found.

One of the first things we did in Tul Karem was meet the former mayor of the town and his family. They used to live in a village called Shufa to the east of Tul Karem. About one year ago when they were out at a party, Israeli soldiers occupied there house. This meant that they lost access to their house, their land, and all the possessions they had in the house, while they were simply out at a party. They have been able to get some of their possessions but it has been difficult. Now they are forced to rent a flat in town and live their. Every morning their three year old wakes up and says he wants to return to their beautiful home. This man was named Muneer and his wife was named Layla. We talked with them for several hours about many different things.

Layla is a refugee. Her family is from the village of Taitabah which has been destroyed but is in what would now be considered norhtern Israel. Her family fled to Lebanon in 1948 during the ethnic cleansing of what is now Israel. She was born in Lebanon, moved to Syria and then to Jordan before she was able to move to Tul Karem. She spoke a lot about wanting to return to her land. Here are a few quotes from the two of them:

"Without the resolution of the refugees we will not reach any solution."

"Sharon is a bloody man"

They like many Palestinians specifically mentioned September 11 of last year and said they were against any such act.

"Sharon has the green light to do whatever he wants to the Palestinians."

"The tanks have destroyed all of the economic life."

"Many families are hungry, but there is no work."

"In spite of this the people are ready to resist more and more."

"We want liberty like all the other people of the world."

When asked how she would feel about returning to her village as an Israeli citizen in Israel, Layla responded:

"We don't care if we are called Israelis, its just a name. The most important thing is to go home."

Later this day I went out on an ambulance run with the Red Cresent in Tul Karem. This is an office where three ambulances have been destroyed in less than a year. One was shelled by a tank in March, killing the driver and another member of the staff. The other two were crushed by tanks. Now there are two ambulances left to serve a huge population that requires many more. On the ride we passed through the Taybeh checkpoint outside of Tul Karem. Once the soldiers saw my US passport we were waved through with only the most basic of searches. On the way back I didn't even have to get out, they waved us through without even checking the id's of the patients. The driver told me they often have to wait at the checkpoint for a half hour or hour. Its great that we can help to speed things up for them and yet horrible at the same time.

Yesterday, 8/16 we visited the families of two martyrs. The first family had contained seven brothers. One, Omar, who was 19 at the time was killed September 6, 2001 when an Apache fired several missles into his car. The eldest brother described finding his brothers face, without the rest of his head or body several meters from the car and his hands several meters beyond that. He said he did not take any pictures because no one should have to see how his brother was killed. Another of the brothers is in prison in the Negev in the south of Israel. Another had just gotten out of a prison near Jenin three days before. He had been in prison for 9 months. Another brother is a fighter, he was forced into hiding and the family does not know where he is and has not seen him for more than one year. The brother who had just gotten out of prison was there simply because his two other brothers had been wanted. When he was arrested the family had no idea where he was for 6 or 7 days. While in jail he was beaten at the beginning of his stay. The entire time he was kept awake almost all of the time between Sunday and Thursday each week and interrogated regularly. He was generally allowed to rest on Fridays and Saturdays. When he got out of prison near Jenin the family feared he would be immediately re-arrested. So they brought him home through the mountains, travelling across country but avoiding the military checkpoints. The military has come to the house many times. Whenever they do all of the brothers must hide or risk being arrested. The family has up several martyr posters for Omar who was killed last year. The soldiers always destroy the posters but the family has a new one made every time and replaces it. Once in March the soldiers told the father of the houshold that they would make sure that he had another poster like that for his other son.

Some quotes from the eldest son in the family. he was about 40 years old.

"We give the Israelis 78% of our land but its not enough."

Talking about different solutions to the situation in Palestine...

"I am sure Israel will never give us one centimeter."

"If we have the same rights [as an Israeli] its okay with us."

"Sharon thinks Israel is alive with war. [He thinks] if the war finishes there will be no more Israel."

"George Bush is crazy. He will destroy the USA. He is not a president, he is a cowboy."

"Not all the people here are fighters or want Intifada, maybe you can say 20%. They want all of us to be fighters. They destroy houses and kill family members. What choice do people have?"

A reminder, this next quote came from someone who had to pick up the pieces, including the severed face, of his younger brother...

"People ask me what I think when I see Israelis killed. I'm a human being, it makes me sad. It makes me sad to see a dead cat. I am a human being."

The second martyrs family we met was the family of Amen Al-Jalad. He was killed on 10/24/01. He, his cousin, and two friends were sitting on the curb on a street corner one evening. Israeli special forces opened fire on them even though none of them were armed or even had any idea that there were Israeli soldiers nearby. He, his cousin, Mahmoud Al-Jalad, and one of the others, Saleh El-Assi, were killed by the gunfire, the fourth was shot in the leg and only survived by pretending to be deadand lying among the bodies of his friends until the soldiers left. Amen was 20. He was in his second year in a university in Navlus. He was also working, his father had died of cancer. He worked in a clothing factory. He had 5 sisters and 2 brothers. He played handball on a local team. His family said he was always happy and always had a smile on his face. He had made dinner for the whole family earlier on the night he was killed. The family talked about him so lovingly, it was very painful. They showed us two videos on their computer of the funeral march. Each was set to a different song written about the three of them. The songs are now famous and sung by children on the streets of Tul Karem and the surrounding villages. On the same night these three were killed, the Israelis also killed two other Palestinians so there funeral march was for 5 martyrs. There were between 15,000 and 30,000 people at the funeral. The videos were amazing. Massive numbers of people out to mourn 5 more people killed by the occupying forces of the Israeli military.

We returned to Qalqilia this morning the same way we had come, with several taxi rides and a fair amount of walking.

Just two more quotes at the end of my report about Tul Karem. I met several young men who helped to show us around town, show us some of the bombed out buildings and introduce us to some of these families. They were great. They all loved Che Guevara and some things one of them said really struck me. I asked one who was wearing a Che Guevara t-shirt if the Israelis would not like the shirt. He replied,

"In Israel it is okay for Israelis to wear a Che Guevara shirt, but its not okay for Palestinians."

but...

"I don't care about them they don't organize my life."

I only hope I am able to be half so defiant in the face of such brutal repression.

Love and Rage from Occupied Palestine,

Garrick
