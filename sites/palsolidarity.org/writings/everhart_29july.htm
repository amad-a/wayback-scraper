Palsolidarity Updates:

Archives

"It is the sound of a multifloor cement home being reduced to rubble.

When you wake up to this sound your heart sinks and seems to merge with a the fear that is rolling around in your stomach."

Chris and Carl Williams

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

 

We walk down the narrow dusty alley and stop when we come to a pile of water tanks blocking our path. The tanks have obviously been put here as a barrier between the Israeli settlement in the middle of Hebron and the rest of the city. Israeli settlers entered into Hebron in 1967. The approximately 500 settlers that live in Hebron now are accompanied by over 1,000 soldiers. Since 1967, the settlers have continually expanded the settlement, taking over Palestinian houses by force. Last Sunday, on July 28, Settlers went on a rampage, injuring four Palestinians, killing a 14 year old girl, and occupying two homes. We turn back from the alley and check our directions, we are trying to find one of the houses that was occupied and damaged during the settler rampage. As we walk up the ally a man opens his door and invites us inside, “Welcome” he says motioning us in.

The small door opens into a large patio. Plants and broken plates, vases, cups, and other assorted ornaments scatter the ground. A woman in a blue hejab leads us into the home. She speaks to us assertively in fluent English, “Look, look at what they have done.” She points to the ground and the rubble left scattered about the house. She leads us into a dark room where the smell of fire still lingers in the air. Settlers started a fire here to destroy part of the house; the military entered the building with them. They just stood by and watched.

Inside, it is hard to distinguish much in the suit. Its hard for me to imagine that this was once a sort of home made museum with handwritten books and artifacts dating back over a thousand years. The house itself is over a thousand years old. “This is the home of our grandmothers and grandfathers…. They want to destroy our history. This is our history.” Afifah, the woman in the blue hejab tells us. The room is still hot from the fire, thousands of years of history destroyed in a few minutes.

Afifah turns to us, knowing that the majority of our group is from the U.S., “America doesn’t want to hear what is happening to the Palestinians, they don’t want to see…. nobody wants to hear, but in the future everyone will hear.” I can tell she is not just saying these words, but she means it. Her eyes have an intensity to them that lets you know that she is serious. “If the Israeli wants to make their home over the blood of the Palestinians this is a dream.”

We continue up to the second floor, broken plates and ceramics crack under my feet as I walk up the steps. An older woman and two children huddle on the ground, a broken T.V. and assorted house belongings cover the floor. Walking to the back of the house, we see how the settlers entered. Behind a chain-wire fence lie the barb wire walls of the Israeli settlement. Despite the wreck that the settlers have caused, the house is still amazing, probably the most beautiful house I have seen in Palestine. We comment about the beauty of the house, but Afifah disagrees with us. “When you have neighbors like the settlers nothing is beautiful. You are always afraid.”

We walk into another room that the settlers were unable to enter. Inside, we see a humble yet elegant living room. The room stands in stark contrast to the other rooms in the house, which have been ransacked. The contrast reminds me of how violating this must be to the family. I remember how I felt when my apartment was broken into in the U.S. and how difficult it was for me to even be in the apartment after it had happened. I know that they must have this same feeling hundredfold; the fear, the sadness, the anger.

We start back out of the house, stepping on the broken remains of this family’s life. Matt, one of the other internationals, looks in disgust, “What kind of people would do this?” Jerry, another international turns and responds, “The kind of people who have experienced the same thing before.”
