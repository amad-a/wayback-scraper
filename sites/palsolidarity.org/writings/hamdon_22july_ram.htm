"It is the sound of a multifloor cement home being reduced to rubble.

When you wake up to this sound your heart sinks and seems to merge with a the fear that is rolling around in your stomach."

Chris and Carl Williams

- Urgent Appeal for Equipment Donations

 

Once again summer holidays for the children of Palestine are filled with long hot days of lockdown, and danger. I arrived in the West Bank a week ago to work with the Palestine Red Crescent Society (observer member of the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies), and in spite of the fact that I was well aware of the situation I was walking into I never could have imagined what I found. As I drove into Ramallah with the ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross), the city I once knew as cosmopolitan and teeming with life felt like a ghost town. Under curfew, people were imprisoned behind their closed doors and windows, the streets were deserted, it was eerie and so still that not even the air was moving.

Arriving at the PRCS Headquarters, I could hear the sounds of children’s voices coming from inside the homes and apartments in the neighbourhood. I tried to imagine being locked into my home in Edmonton with my 3 children in temperatures of 35 C for days on end. I tried to imagine what it would be like to know that we were running low on food and other essential supplies; what it would feel like to be unable to refill my son’s ventolin prescription for asthma; or unable to guarantee my children’s safety if they went out to play in the yard.

But the children here are resilient; they defy the curfew, and when the tanks and jeeps and guns are not in sight they take to the streets in small numbers to scooter and ride their bikes down the hilly roads unrestricted by other traffic. When the soldiers come, they run and hide because everyone knows someone who has been shot, or at the very least shot at, for being out of the house during curfew.

In the evening, as the sun begins to set, the children’s air force takes to the sky. Homemade kites in the colors of the Palestinian flag fly far above their prisons – dipping and diving in the evening sky. Leaning out the windows or bravely taking to their roofs, the children send their symbols of freedom soaring upward and for a moment they are flying too. The kites look like guitars from the ground, with their long tails trailing behind, and the sounds of the children who fly them fill the air – like music. A small pleasure, a moment of joy.

Yesterday they lifted the curfew for a couple hours and the streets were full of people hurrying to buy food, diapers, formula, medicine and other essentials. Things seemed normal for a while. The Coordinator of Emergency Medical and Ambulance Services in the West Bank and I needed to go to Jerusalem for a meeting. At the Kalandia checkpoint, all illusions of normalcy were shattered as I witnessed hundreds of men, women and children being treated like cattle…no, worse than that…in the scorching heat. A trip from Ramallah to Jerusalem that used to take my family and I 15 minutes now takes two to four hours. Of course, this depends on the mood of the soldiers and the day of the week. Yesterday was particularly bad. As we stood in this mass of humanity, we were yelled at and told to move, even though we were surrounded by hoops of razor wire and there was nowhere at all to go.

An American accent in an Israeli uniform called out for foreign journalists: “Just show me your GPC (government press card) and I can get you right through.” His attempt to make the process appear humane. I thought to myself, if you want to be humane let the children through. And then it occurred to me: of course he’s clearing journalists, because the last thing he wants is for a journalist to witness and report the truth of this ugliness.

Women fainted in the heat, small children cried, hot and frightened. The Israeli soldiers thought nothing of pushing grandmothers and young children or pointing their loaded and large automatic weapons at us if someone complained too loudly or tried to plead their case in an effort to get through sooner. The soldier up on the lookout tower cocked and uncocked his rifle over and over as he glared at us. Tension was building, and then one grandmother began to shout. “Why are you doing this to us? We are not the problem, we are just the people. Why?” I was angry and scared. And then, completely unprovoked, they began to open fire. I was stunned. Deafened for a few seconds, I tried to make sense of what had just happened. For his part, the soldier simply scanned the crowd with an intimidating glare and walked away.

I wonder how Palestinian children will remember their summer holidays of 2002, unable to swim or go to camp or run free in their yards like their Israeli counterparts in the illegal settlements on the tops of the hills around them? I wonder if anyone, anyone beyond the many shifting borders of this place that is, even notices anymore, much less cares?

(Karen Hamdon is a director of Humanserve International, an Alberta based NGO. She lived in Ramallah with her husband and 3 children in 1999-2000 and has recently returned for a short posting with the Palestine Red Crescent Society.)
